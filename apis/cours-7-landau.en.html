Maths APIS · Lesson 7 · for the final exam

Comparing two sequences by their quotient.

Landau notations tell you which of two sequences “weighs” more at infinity: \(u_n = o(v_n)\) when \(u_n\) is negligible compared with \(v_n\), \(u_n = O(v_n)\) when it is dominated, \(u_n \sim v_n\) when they are equivalent. In practice, everything can be read from the limit of \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n}\).

  • Time ≈ 45 min
  • Parts 5
  • Course MiMos of the week of 30 November · Tutorial 4, part 5

Everything from a quotient

When \((v_n)\) does not vanish from some rank on:

\[u_n = o(v_n) \iff \frac{u_n}{v_n} \to 0\]
\[u_n = O(v_n) \iff \left(\frac{u_n}{v_n}\right) \text{ is bounded}\]
\[u_n \sim v_n \iff \frac{u_n}{v_n} \to 1\]

Part 1 summary sheet, definition 1 · Tutorial 4 § 5.2 “must know”

Negligible, dominated, equivalent

Definition Landau notations

Let \((u_n)\) and \((v_n)\) be two real sequences.

  1. \((u_n)\) is negligible compared with \((v_n)\), written \(u_n = o(v_n)\) or \(u_n \ll v_n\), if there exists a sequence \((\varepsilon_n)\) with limit zero such that, from some rank on, \(u_n = \varepsilon_n v_n\).
  2. \((u_n)\) is dominated by \((v_n)\), written \(u_n = O(v_n)\), if there exists a bounded sequence \((b_n)\) such that, from some rank on, \(u_n = b_n v_n\).
  3. \((u_n)\) is equivalent to \((v_n)\), written \(u_n \sim v_n\), if there exists a sequence \((k_n)\) with limit 1 such that, from some rank on, \(u_n = k_n v_n\). (Tutorial: \(u_n = v_n(1 + \varepsilon_n)\) with \(\varepsilon_n \to 0\).)
In practice \((v_n)\) with non-zero terms from some rank on
\[u_n = o(v_n) \iff \frac{u_n}{v_n} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 0 \qquad u_n = O(v_n) \iff \left(\frac{u_n}{v_n}\right) \text{ bounded} \qquad u_n \sim v_n \iff \frac{u_n}{v_n} \xrightarrow[n \to +\infty]{} 1\]

Immediate examples: \(n = o(n^2)\) because \(\frac{n}{n^2} = \frac{1}{n} \to 0\) ; \(n\sin(n) = O(n)\) because \(\frac{n\sin n}{n} = \sin(n)\) is bounded; \(n + 1 \sim n\) because \(\frac{n+1}{n} = 1 + \frac{1}{n} \to 1\).

The January 2025 exam opens with: “Recall the definition of \(u_n = o(v_n)\), \(u_n = O(v_n)\) and \(u_n \sim v_n\)” for two sequences that do not vanish: in that case, the three characterisations by the quotient are the expected answer.

Traps
  • The “=” sign in \(u_n = o(v_n)\) is not an equality: it reads “is a”, and cannot be reversed.
  • \(\sim\) requires the limit 1, not “a finite limit”: \(2n\) and \(n\) are not equivalent.
  • \(O\) does not require a limit: a bounded quotient is enough.

Links between the three, special cases

Remarks from the summary sheet
  1. \(u_n = o(v_n) \Rightarrow u_n = O(v_n)\) and \(u_n \sim v_n \Rightarrow u_n = O(v_n)\).
  2. \(u_n = o(1)\) means \(u_n \to 0\).
  3. \(u_n \sim 0\) means that \((u_n)\) is zero from some rank on.
  4. If \(u_n \sim v_n\), then \((u_n)\) and \((v_n)\) have the same limit (when it exists).

\(u_n = O(1)\)\((u_n)\) is bounded\(u_n = b_n \times 1\)

Property Tutorial 4, ex. 5.14 (7)
\[u_n \sim v_n \iff u_n = v_n + o(v_n).\]
Proof

\(u_n \sim v_n\) means: from some rank on, \(u_n = v_n(1 + \varepsilon_n)\) with \(\varepsilon_n \to 0\), that is \(u_n = v_n + \varepsilon_n v_n\). Now \(\varepsilon_n v_n\) is, by definition, an \(o(v_n)\). The two notations say the same thing.

Is \(u_n \sim v_n \iff u_n - v_n \to 0\) true? No, in neither direction.

  • \(n + 1 \sim n\), but \((n + 1) - n = 1\) does not tend to 0.
  • \(\frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n^2} \to 0\), but \(\frac{1/n}{1/n^2} = n\) does not tend to 1: the sequences are not equivalent.

Equivalence compares quotients, not differences.

Traps
  • Writing \(u_n \sim 0\) to mean “tends to 0”: that is \(u_n = o(1)\).
  • \(u_n = O(v_n)\) implies neither \(o\) nor \(\sim\): \(2n = O(n)\) only.

The comparison scale

Must know comparative growth, with \(\ll\)

For \(0 \lt \alpha \lt \beta\) and \(q \gt 1\):

\[1 \ll \ln(n) \ll n^{\alpha} \ll n^{\beta} \ll q^n \ll n!\]

The tutorial gives the example \(\ln(n) \ll n \ll e^n\). Taking inverses, the order is reversed: \(\dfrac{1}{e^n} \ll \dfrac{1}{n^2} \ll \dfrac{1}{n} \ll \dfrac{1}{\ln(n)} \ll 1\).

Method ranking sequences with \(\ll\)
  1. First sort by “family”: constants, logarithms, powers, exponentials.
  2. For two neighbours, form the quotient and simplify: it must tend to 0.
  3. A factor \(\ln(n)\) weighs more than 1 and less than any power \(n^{\alpha}\), \(\alpha \gt 0\).
Tutorial 4, ex. 5.14RankingQuotient to check
(3)\(1 \ll \ln(n) \ll n \ll n\ln(n) \ll n^2 \ll n^3 \ll 2^n\)\(\frac{n}{n\ln n} = \frac{1}{\ln n} \to 0\) ; \(\frac{n\ln n}{n^2} = \frac{\ln n}{n} \to 0\)
(4)\(\ln(n) \ll n \ll n\ln(n) \ll \dfrac{n^2}{\ln(n)} \ll n^2 \ll e^n\)\(\dfrac{n\ln n}{n^2/\ln n} = \dfrac{(\ln n)^2}{n} \to 0\)
(5)\(\dfrac{1}{n^2} \ll \dfrac{\ln(n)}{n^2} \ll \dfrac{1}{n\ln(n)} \ll \dfrac{1}{n} \ll \dfrac{\ln(n)}{n} \ll \dfrac{1}{\ln(n)}\)\(\dfrac{\ln n/n^2}{1/(n\ln n)} = \dfrac{(\ln n)^2}{n} \to 0\)
 Tutorial published with no answer key: every quotient of two neighbours has been recomputed. \(\frac{(\ln n)^2}{n} \to 0\): any power of the logarithm is negligible compared with \(n\).
Traps
  • \(n\ln(n)\) lies between \(n\) and \(n^2\), not above \(n^2\).
  • For sequences that tend to 0, “negligible” means “tends to 0 faster”: \(\frac{1}{n^2} \ll \frac{1}{n}\).
  • \(2^n \ll 3^n\): two exponentials with different bases are not of the same order.

Calculating with equivalents

Proposition operations on equivalents

Let \((u_n)\), \((v_n)\), \((w_n)\), \((x_n)\) be four sequences that never vanish, with \(u_n \sim v_n\) and \(w_n \sim x_n\). Then:

  1. \(u_n w_n \sim v_n x_n\) and \(\dfrac{u_n}{w_n} \sim \dfrac{v_n}{x_n}\);
  2. \(|u_n| \sim |v_n|\);
  3. for \(\alpha \in \R^*\), \(u_n^{\alpha} \sim v_n^{\alpha}\);
  4. for \(\ell \in \R^*\): \(u_n \to \ell \iff u_n \sim \ell\).

Equivalents carry over to products, quotients and powers. They do not carry over to sums: \(n + 1 \sim n\) and \(-n \sim -n\), but \((n + 1) - n = 1\) is not equivalent to \(n - n = 0\).

For a sum, keep the dominant term: if \(v_n = o(u_n)\), then \(u_n + v_n \sim u_n\). In particular, a polynomial in \(n\) is equivalent to its highest-degree term.

Model answer Tutorial 4, ex. 5.14 (6): a simple equivalent

\(\ln(n) + 2n - 3\): \(\ln(n) = o(n)\) and \(3 = o(n)\), so \(\ln(n) + 2n - 3 \sim 2n\).the dominant term

\(\dfrac{1}{n - 1} - \dfrac{1}{n + 1} = \dfrac{2}{n^2 - 1}\), and \(n^2 - 1 \sim n^2\), so by quotient \(\dfrac{1}{n-1} - \dfrac{1}{n+1} \sim \dfrac{2}{n^2}\).reduce first: no equivalents inside a difference

\(2e^n - n \sim 2e^n\) (because \(n = o(e^n)\)) and \(n + 1 \sim n\): by quotient \(\dfrac{2e^n - n}{n + 1} \sim \dfrac{2e^n}{n}\).

Traps
  • Adding or subtracting equivalents: forbidden. Put over a common denominator or factorise first.
  • \(e^{n+1}\) is not equivalent to \(e^n\): the quotient is \(e\). You cannot apply the exponential to an equivalent.
  • An equivalent must be given simple: a single term, no sum.

Part 5 January 2024 exam (6 points) and January 2025 exam (7.5 points)

In the exams: “state all the possible comparisons”

The instruction is the same both years: “Compare at \(+\infty\) the sequences \((u_n)\) and \((v_n)\) using the Landau comparison symbols \(\sim\), \(= o(\cdot)\), \(= O(\cdot)\), stating all the possible comparisons and justifying your answers”.

Method one limit, then a table
  1. Form \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n}\) and factorise the dominant terms.
  2. Compute its limit (comparative growth if needed).
  3. Read all the comparisons from the table below, and justify the \(O\): “the sequence \(\left(\frac{u_n}{v_n}\right)\) converges, so it is bounded”.
\(\displaystyle\lim \frac{u_n}{v_n}\)Comparisons to state
\(0\)\(u_n = o(v_n)\), \(u_n = O(v_n)\)
\(1\)\(u_n \sim v_n\), \(u_n = O(v_n)\), \(v_n = O(u_n)\)
\(\ell\) real, \(\ell \neq 0\) and \(\ell \neq 1\)\(u_n = O(v_n)\), \(v_n = O(u_n)\); neither \(o\) nor \(\sim\)
\(\pm\infty\)\(\dfrac{v_n}{u_n} \to 0\) : \(v_n = o(u_n)\), \(v_n = O(u_n)\)
Model answer January 2024 exam, exercise 3 (official answer key)

1. \(u_n = 1 - 2n^2\), \(v_n = -2n^2 + n - 3\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \dfrac{-2n^2\left(1 - \frac{1}{2n^2}\right)}{-2n^2\left(1 - \frac{1}{2n} + \frac{3}{2n^2}\right)} \to 1\). So \(u_n \sim v_n\). The sequence \(\left(\frac{u_n}{v_n}\right)\) converges, so it is bounded: \(u_n = O(v_n)\).factorise −2n²

2. \(u_n = e^{2n} - \sqrt{n}\), \(v_n = n + 1\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \dfrac{e^{2n}}{n} \times \dfrac{1 - \frac{\sqrt{n}}{e^{2n}}}{1 + \frac{1}{n}}\). By comparative growth, \(\dfrac{e^{2n}}{n} \to +\infty\) and \(\dfrac{\sqrt{n}}{e^{2n}} \to 0\): \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} \to +\infty\), so \(\dfrac{v_n}{u_n} \to 0\). Hence \(v_n = o(u_n)\), and also \(v_n = O(u_n)\).the quotient goes to infinity: flip it

3. \(u_n = 4^n + 1\), \(v_n = 5^n\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \left(\dfrac{4}{5}\right)^n + \dfrac{1}{5^n} \to 0\), because \(-1 \lt \frac{4}{5} \lt 1\) and \(5^n \to +\infty\). So \(u_n = o(v_n)\), and \(u_n = O(v_n)\).

Model answer January 2025 exam, exercise 3 (no published answer key)

(a) \(u_n = -n^3 + n - 2\), \(v_n = -2n^2 + n\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \dfrac{-n^3\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2} + \frac{2}{n^3}\right)}{-2n^2\left(1 - \frac{1}{2n}\right)} = \dfrac{n}{2} \times \dfrac{1 - \frac{1}{n^2} + \frac{2}{n^3}}{1 - \frac{1}{2n}} \to +\infty\). So \(v_n = o(u_n)\) and \(v_n = O(u_n)\).

(b) \(u_n = e^n + \sqrt{n}\), \(v_n = e^n + e^{-n}\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \dfrac{1 + \sqrt{n}\,e^{-n}}{1 + e^{-2n}} \to 1\). So \(u_n \sim v_n\), \(u_n = O(v_n)\) and \(v_n = O(u_n)\).

(c) \(u_n = 3n + 6\), \(v_n = 5n - 1\). \(\dfrac{u_n}{v_n} = \dfrac{3 + \frac{6}{n}}{5 - \frac{1}{n}} \to \dfrac{3}{5}\). The quotient converges to a non-zero real number: \(u_n = O(v_n)\) and \(v_n = O(u_n)\). The sequences are not equivalent (\(\frac{3}{5} \neq 1\)) and neither is negligible compared with the other.

In the exam

“All the possible comparisons”: you lose points by stopping at the first one. After \(\sim\) or \(o\), always add the \(O\), with its justification.
