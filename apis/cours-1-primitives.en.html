Finding a primitive means differentiating backwards.

A primitive of \(f\) (also called an antiderivative) is a function whose derivative is \(f\). Two tables are enough: the one for usual functions, and the one for composite functions \(u' \times g(u)\). All the work is recognising the right line and adjusting a constant. This lesson is the basis of integrals (lesson 2) and of the rest of the ECUE.

Part 1 handout § 1.1 · summary § 1

Primitive of a function: definition, existence, constant

Definition primitive

Let \(f\) be a continuous function on an interval \(I\) of \(\R\). A primitive of \(f\) on \(I\) is a differentiable function \(F\) on \(I\) such that \(F' = f\), that is:

\[\forall x \in I,\quad F'(x) = f(x).\]

It is often written \(F(x) = \displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx\).

Examples. A primitive of \(x \mapsto \frac{1}{x}\) on \(]0, +\infty[\) is \(x \mapsto \ln(x)\). A primitive of \(x \mapsto x\) on \(\R\) is \(x \mapsto \frac{x^2}{2}\). To check a primitive, differentiate it: this is the check to make every time.

Theorem existence, and all the primitives

Let \(I\) be an interval of \(\R\).

  1. Every continuous function on \(I\) has a primitive on \(I\).
  2. If \(F\) is a primitive of \(f\) on \(I\), then the primitives of \(f\) on \(I\) are exactly the functions \(F + c\), where \(c\) is a real constant.
Why

\((F + c)' = F' = f\): adding a constant still gives a primitive. Conversely, if \(G\) is another primitive, \((G - F)' = f - f = 0\) on the interval \(I\), so \(G - F\) is constant.

A continuous function therefore has infinitely many primitives, which differ by a constant: \(x \mapsto 2x\) and \(x \mapsto 2x + \pi\) are two primitives of \(x \mapsto 2\). Say “a primitive”, never “the primitive”, unless a condition fixes the constant.

Model answer the primitive that equals 3 at 4 (handout)

Find the primitive \(F\) of \(x \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\) on \(]0, +\infty[\) such that \(F(4) = 3\).

The primitives of \(x \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\) on \(]0, +\infty[\) are the functions \(F(x) = 2\sqrt{x} + c\), \(c \in \R\).the general form first

\(F(4) = 3 \iff 2\sqrt{4} + c = 3 \iff c = -1\).the condition fixes c

So \(F(x) = 2\sqrt{x} - 1\).

Traps
  • Mixing up primitive and derivative: the primitive of \(x^2\) is \(\frac{x^3}{3}\), not \(2x\). If in doubt, differentiate your answer.
  • On a set that is not an interval (such as \(\R^*\)), two primitives can differ by a different constant on each piece. Always work on an interval.

Method powers: a single formula
  1. Write the function in the form \(x^\alpha\): \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\), \(\frac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3}\), \(x\sqrt{x} = x^{3/2}\).
  2. Add 1 to the exponent, divide by the new exponent: \(\frac{x^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha+1}\).
  3. If \(\alpha = -1\), the formula does not work (division by 0): the answer is \(\ln|x|\).

Applications from Tutorial 3 (exercise 1.2). On \(\R_+\): \(x\sqrt{x} = x^{3/2}\) has primitive \(\frac{x^{5/2}}{5/2} = \frac{2}{5}x^{5/2}\). On \(\R\): \(-3\sin(x) + 2e^x\) has primitive \(3\cos(x) + 2e^x\).

Where tan and arctan come from

Tutorial 3, exercise 1.1: \(\tan = \frac{\sin}{\cos}\), so \(\tan' = \frac{\cos^2 + \sin^2}{\cos^2} = \frac{1}{\cos^2} = 1 + \tan^2\). For arctan: if \(y = \tan(x)\), then \(\arctan(y) = x\); differentiating \(\arctan(\tan(x)) = x\) gives \(\arctan'(\tan x) \times (1 + \tan^2 x) = 1\), hence \(\arctan'(y) = \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\).

Traps
  • The sign: a primitive of \(\sin\) is \(-\cos\); a primitive of \(\cos\) is \(+\sin\).
  • \(\frac{1}{x^2}\) does not have \(\ln(x^2)\) as a primitive: the logarithm is only for the exponent \(-1\).
  • Dividing by the old exponent: \(\frac{x^{\alpha+1}}{\alpha}\) is wrong.

Part 3 handout § 1.2 · summary, proposition 2

Sums and multiplication by a real number

Theorem linearity

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be continuous on an interval \(I\), \(F\) a primitive of \(f\) and \(G\) a primitive of \(g\) on \(I\). For every \((\alpha, \beta) \in \R^2\), a primitive of \(\alpha f + \beta g\) on \(I\) is \(\alpha F + \beta G\).

Proof

\((\alpha F + \beta G)' = \alpha F' + \beta G' = \alpha f + \beta g\).

Examples from the handout. On \(\R\), a primitive of \(x + 3x^2 + 6x^5\) is \(\frac{x^2}{2} + 3 \times \frac{x^3}{3} + 6 \times \frac{x^6}{6} = \frac{x^2}{2} + x^3 + x^6\). A primitive of \(\cos(x) + 6e^x\) is \(\sin(x) + 6e^x\). On \(\left]-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right[\), a primitive of \(\frac{3}{\cos^2(x)} + 2\) is \(3\tan(x) + 2x\) (the handout adds the constant 42: any constant works).

Tutorial 3. On \(]0, +\infty[\), \(g(x) = \frac{3}{x} - 5x + 6x^4\) has primitive \(3\ln(x) - \frac{5}{2}x^2 + \frac{6}{5}x^5\).

Trap: the product
  • Linearity says nothing about the product: a primitive of \(f \times g\) is not \(F \times G\), because \((FG)' = fG + Fg\).
  • For a product, only two options: recognise a form \(u' \times g(u)\) (part 4), or use integration by parts (lesson 3).

Primitives of composite functions: the form \(u' \times g(u)\)

The derivative of a composite function is \((G \circ u)' = u' \times (G' \circ u)\). Read backwards: if \(G\) is a primitive of \(g\), then \(G \circ u\) is a primitive of \(u' \times (g \circ u)\). Each line of table 1 thus gives a “composite” line. This is the objective “compute a primitive using functions of the form \((v' \circ u) \times u'\)”.

Examples from the handout. A primitive of \(e^{2x}\) on \(\R\) is \(\frac{e^{2x}}{2}\); a primitive of \(\cos(3x)\) is \(\frac{\sin(3x)}{3}\); a primitive of \(\frac{2x}{x^2 + 1}\) is \(\ln(x^2 + 1)\).

Cases to know by reflex (Tutorial 3, question 3a): for \(a \neq 0\), a primitive of \(\sin(ax + b)\) is \(-\frac{1}{a}\cos(ax + b)\), a primitive of \(\cos(ax + b)\) is \(\frac{1}{a}\sin(ax + b)\), a primitive of \(e^{ax + b}\) is \(\frac{1}{a}e^{ax + b}\). So \(\sin(2x) \to -\frac{\cos(2x)}{2}\), \(\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \to 2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\), \(e^{6x - 7} \to \frac{e^{6x-7}}{6}\).

Traps
  • You divide by \(a\) when looking for a primitive of \(e^{ax}\); you multiply when differentiating. Dividing by \(\frac{1}{2}\) means multiplying by 2: \(\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \to 2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\).
  • \(\frac{u'}{\sqrt{u}}\) has primitive \(2\sqrt{u}\), not \(\sqrt{u}\): the table line has a 2 in the denominator.
  • The \(u'\) must be present as a factor. Without it, the line does not apply: \(\frac{1}{x^2 + 1}\) does not have \(\ln(x^2 + 1)\) as a primitive, but \(\arctan(x)\).

Recognising the form and adjusting the constant

Method “without integration by parts or change of variable”
  1. Spot \(u\): what is “inside” (under the root, in the denominator, in the exponent, in the cosine).
  2. Compute \(u'\) and compare it with the remaining factor. If it is there up to a constant, you are done.
  3. Make \(u'\) appear exactly, by compensating in front: \(x = \frac{1}{2} \times 2x\).
  4. Read the line of table 2, then check by differentiating.

\(e^{2x}\) yes, \(e^{x^2}\) no

Tutorial 3 asks: “can you compute a primitive of \(x \mapsto e^{2x}\)? of \(x \mapsto e^{x^2}\)?”. For \(e^{2x}\), \(u' = 2\) is a constant: compensate, \(\frac{e^{2x}}{2}\). For \(e^{x^2}\), you would need the factor \(u' = 2x\), which is not a constant: you cannot create it. This function has primitives (it is continuous), but none can be written with usual functions. By contrast \(x\,e^{x^2}\) can be handled: \(\frac{1}{2}e^{x^2}\).

In course tests and exams

The first question on integrals is almost always a primitive of this type, with the instruction “without integration by parts or change of variable”:

  • October 2022 course test: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 x\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\,dx\) (\(u = x^2 + 1\), form \(\frac{1}{2}u'u^{1/2}\)).
  • November 2022 course test: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{2x^3 + 1}{(x^4 + 2x + 1)^3}\,dx\) (\(u = x^4 + 2x + 1\), form \(\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{u'}{u^3}\)).
