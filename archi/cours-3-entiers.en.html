Part 1 course § III.1

Bits, words and bytes

A bit (an abbreviation of binary digit) is the smallest unit of information a computer can store: it is either 0 or 1. A word consists of one or more bits that can be manipulated as a whole. Its size, its number of bits, is fixed.

Each extra bit doubles the number of possibilities: an n-bit word can be arranged into 2n patterns.

Table 3.1 — Number of patterns of a word (course, p. 17)
Word sizePatterns
1 bit2: 0 and 1
2 bits4: 00, 01, 10, 11
3 bits8: 000, 001, …, 110, 111
4 bits16: 0000, 0001, …, 1111
8 bits (a byte)256
16 bits65,536
n bits2n

A byte is an 8-bit word: 256 patterns. Microprocessors are commonly byte oriented: they manipulate bytes or groups of bytes. The size of a “word” depends on the context: for a 68000 microprocessor, a word is 16 bits and a long word 32 bits; for a 32-bit ARM microprocessor, a word is 32 bits and a half-word 16 bits. Out of context, the size of a word should be specified.

The leftmost bit is the most significant bit, or MSB. The rightmost bit is the least significant bit, or LSB. The bits of an n-bit word are numbered from 0 (the LSB) to n − 1 (the MSB).

A word in itself represents nothing. What does the byte 1111 1111 represent? The number 255, the number −1, the number 0.72, the character ‘X’, an instruction, a colour: it all depends on the context, that is, on the encoding used. This chapter studies two ways to encode integers, unsigned (part 3) and signed (parts 4 to 6), then three other codes (part 8).

For the sake of convenience, a word is commonly written in its hexadecimal form, one digit per group of 4 bits (chapter 1, part 8): 1111 11112 = FF16. A group of 4 bits is called a nibble; the course does not use the word, but you will come across it elsewhere.

Remember
  • An n-bit word: 2n patterns, bits numbered from 0 (LSB, on the right) to n − 1 (MSB, on the left).
  • A byte: 8 bits, 256 patterns, two hexadecimal digits.
Pitfall: counting from 0

In a byte, the MSB is bit 7, not bit 8: numbering starts at 0. For the same reason, 8 bits give 256 patterns but the largest unsigned value is 28 − 1 = 255: the first pattern, 0000 0000, encodes 0.

One’s complement and two’s complement

The one’s complement of a word inverts each bit: each 0 becomes 1, each 1 becomes 0.

The two’s complement inverts each bit and adds one: it is the one’s complement plus one. The course writes it 2’sC (…). The result keeps the size of the word: a carry out on the left is left out. The example of the course, a 10-bit word:

word: 11 1001 10002
one’s complement: 00 0110 01112
00 0110 01112 + 12 = 00 0110 10002
2’sC (11 1001 10002) = 00 0110 10002

In the register below, press “Two’s complement”: it shows both steps. Press it again: you get the starting word back, because the two’s complement of the two’s complement is the word itself.

The shortcut of the course. Keep the first bits as they are, from the LSB up to the first 1, and invert all the others.

Why it works

In the one’s complement, the 0s to the right of the first 1 have become 1s, and that first 1 has become a 0. Adding one turns those 1s back into 0s with a carry, which stops on that 0 and turns it back into 1. The bits further left do not move: they stay inverted.

Pitfall: the first 1 is kept
  • The first 1 from the right stays a 1: 2’sC (1011 1100) = 0100 0100. Inverting that 1 as well gives 0100 0011, which is the one’s complement.
  • The two’s complement of 0000 0000 is 0000 0000: 1111 1111 + 1 = 1 0000 0000, and the carry out is left out.

The two’s complement is mostly used for encoding signed integers: see part 4.

Part 3 course § IV.1

Unsigned integers

This is the most natural encoding: the word is the binary representation of the number, padded with 0s on the left up to the size of the word. The binary number 1012 encoded into an unsigned 8-bit word gives 0000 0101.

An n-bit word can be arranged into 2n patterns, and the first one encodes 0: it represents the integers from 0 to 2n − 1. With 8 bits, from 0 to 255.

Encoding: successive divisions

The remainders of the successive divisions by 2 (chapter 1, part 6), read from bottom to top, give 11 0010. Pad on the left up to 8 bits:

5010 = 0011 00102

Decoding: the sum of weights

Add the weights of the bits set to 1 (chapter 1, part 5). The 6-bit word of the course:

10 01102 = 32 + 4 + 2 = 3810

Unsigned overflow. Operations are performed with fixed-size words. When the exact result is outside the range 0 … 2n − 1, it cannot be encoded properly: an unsigned overflow occurred. The example of the course, an 8-bit addition (the two operands and the result are 8 bits wide):

The exact result needs 9 bits: 1 0000 01002 = 260. This ninth bit is the carry: it does not fit in the word, so it is left out. The result word is 0000 01002 = 4. A C program that adds two unsigned char variables equal to 250 and 10 displays 250 + 10 = 4.

The incrementation cycles through the patterns of the word. Adding 1 to 1111 1111 (255) gives 0000 0000 (0): back to the start. Subtracting 1 from 0 gives 255. With unsigned 8-bit words:

255 + 1 = 0
0 − 1 = 255
250 + 10 = 4
4 − 10 = 250

The wheel of part 4 shows this cycle with 4 bits. The course also notes that the unsigned overflow can be called the carry when adding numbers and the borrow when subtracting numbers.

Remember: spotting an unsigned overflow
  • Addition: unsigned overflow if and only if the addition produces a carry out, an (n + 1)th bit.
  • Subtraction a − b: unsigned overflow if and only if a < b as unsigned numbers. The exact result would be negative: the subtraction borrows beyond the MSB.
Pitfall: the word is still written down

After an overflow, the 8 bits of the result exist: 0000 0100. In the exam, write this word in the “Binary Result” column, and ‘ERROR’ only in the column of the decimal value concerned.

Signed integers: range and encoding

For signed integers, the course studies the most commonly used encoding: the two’s complement system. The 2n patterns are divided into halves. The words whose MSB is 0 represent positive integers, those whose MSB is 1 represent negative integers: the MSB is therefore called the sign bit.

An n-bit word can represent integers from −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1: from −8 to 7 with 4 bits, from −128 to 127 with a byte. The wheel shows the 16 four-bit words, with the unsigned value inside and the signed value outside.

In this system, the two’s complement of a word represents the additive inverse (or the opposite) of the number. The example of the course: 310 = 00112 and 2’sC (00112) = 11012 = −310.

Encoding a signed integer. A positive integer is encoded as an unsigned integer. For a negative integer:

  1. Convert its absolute value into its binary form, with n bits.

  2. Work out the two’s complement of this word.

−17 with 8 bits:
1710 = 0001 00012
−17 = 2’sC (0001 00012) = 1110 11112

Remember
  • With n bits, 2n unsigned integers can be encoded, and as many signed integers.
  • Largest unsigned: 2n − 1. Largest signed: 2n−1 − 1. Smallest signed: −2n−1.
Pitfall: the range is not symmetric
  • −128 fits in a signed byte (1000 0000), +128 does not. So −128 cannot be encoded by “absolute value, then two’s complement”: 128 is not a positive signed 8-bit integer. The mechanical calculation 2’sC (1000 0000) = 1000 0000 still gives the right word.
  • 2021 exam: 8,192 = 213 needs 15 bits as a signed number, because 14 bits only go up to 213 − 1 = 8,191. But −8,192 = −213 fits in 14 bits: it is the smallest value.
  • Pad to n bits before the two’s complement. With 5 bits, 2’sC (1 0001) = 0 1111, and padding with 0s afterwards would give 0000 1111, that is 15. With 8 bits, you need 2’sC (0001 0001) = 1110 1111.

Decoding a signed word

The method depends on the sign bit, the MSB:

  1. Sign bit 0: the integer is positive. Decode it as an unsigned integer, with the sum of weights.

  2. Sign bit 1: the integer is negative. Work out the two’s complement of the word, convert it into its decimal form and write down the minus sign in front of it.

The example of the course, the 6-bit word 10 01102: its sign bit is 1.

2’sC (10 01102) = 01 10102
01 10102 = 16 + 8 + 2 = 2610
10 01102 = −2610

The same word is 10 01102 = 3810 as an unsigned integer (part 3): the course deliberately contrasts the two readings.

To check (not the method of the course)
  • A negative n-bit word is worth its unsigned value minus 2n: 38 − 26 = −26.
  • In other words, the MSB weighs −2n−1 instead of +2n−1: −32 + 4 + 2 = −26. The register of figure 1 reminds you of it when you click a bit.

In hexadecimal (Tutorial 2, exercise 3). The first hexadecimal digit contains the sign bit: an 8-bit word is negative when its first digit is 8 or more (8 to F). The words used to encode positive integers range from 00 to 7F, those used to encode negative integers from 80 to FF.

  • The minus sign goes in front of the value of the complement: for 1000 0000, 2’sC (1000 0000) = 1000 0000, which is 128 unsigned, so the word is −128.

Subtraction using the two’s complement, signed overflow

Since the two’s complement represents the opposite of a number, subtraction can be replaced by addition: a − b = a + 2’sC (b), as long as we ignore the carry. The example of the course, 115 − 62 with 8 bits: 11510 = 0111 00112, 6210 = 0011 11102 and 2’sC (6210) = 1100 00102.

Both calculations give the same 8-bit word, 0011 01012 = 5310. The addition produces a carry, a ninth bit: ignore it. Therefore 115 − 62 = 115 + (−62) = 53.

Signed overflow. Just like with unsigned integers, a result may be outside the range −2n−1 … 2n−1 − 1: a signed overflow occurred. The example of the course:

The resulting word, 1000 0010, has its sign bit set to 1: 1000 00102 = −126, whereas the expected result, 130, is too large for a signed byte (127 at most). The wrong result is negative, although both operands are positive. A C program with signed char variables displays 120 + 10 = -126. The incrementation still cycles, but the cut is between 127 and −128 (the purple line on the wheel):

127 + 1 = −128
120 + 10 = −126
−126 − 10 = 120

The sign rule (Tutorial 2, exercise 3). The course gives no rule to spot a signed overflow; the tutorial makes you build it, by comparing the signs of the operands and of the result. “Positive” means MSB = 0, “negative” means MSB = 1.

The rule to remember
  • Addition: signed overflow only if both operands have the same sign and the result has the other sign (P + P = N, N + N = P). Two operands of opposite signs never overflow.
  • Subtraction a − b = a + (−b): signed overflow only if a and b have opposite signs and the result does not have the sign of a (P − N = N, N − P = P).
Carry without signed overflow · 2017 exam

1000 1100 + 0111 1110 = 1 0000 1010

Carry out: unsigned, 140 + 126 = 266 > 255, ERROR. Signed, −116 + 126 = 10: right, the word 0000 1010 is 10.

Signed overflow without carry · 2023 exam

0110 1001 + 0110 1110 = 1101 0111

No carry: unsigned, 105 + 110 = 215, right. Signed, P + P = N: 215 > 127, ERROR.

Main pitfall: the carry says nothing about signed numbers
  • The carry out signals the unsigned overflow of an addition, and only that. For signed numbers, look at the signs.
  • In a subtraction worked out as a + 2’sC (b), the carry does not even signal the unsigned overflow. 2023 exam: 1101 0011 − 1001 1111 becomes 1101 0011 + 0110 0001 = 1 0011 0100, with a carry, whereas 211 − 159 = 52 is right. The unsigned overflow of a subtraction is a < b.

The 8-bit table of the exams

The instruction, the same in the exams and in Tutorial 2 (exercise 4): “Perform the following 8-bit binary operations (the two operands and the result are 8 bits wide). Then, convert the result into unsigned and signed decimal values. If an overflow occurs, write down ‘ERROR’ instead of the decimal value.” Line by line:

  1. Perform the 8-bit operation: the addition with its carries, the subtraction with its borrows (or a + 2’sC (b)). Write the 8 bits of the result in “Binary Result”, even if an overflow occurs.

  2. “Unsigned” column. Addition: ERROR if there is a carry out. Subtraction: ERROR if a < b (compare the words from the left). Otherwise, decode the result as an unsigned integer.

  3. “Signed” column. Look at the sign bits of the operands and of the result: P + P = N, N + N = P, P − N = N or N − P = P gives ERROR. Otherwise, decode the result as a signed integer (two’s complement if it starts with 1).

  4. Check in decimal: do the operation again with the values of the operands. The exact result must be between 0 and 255 for the unsigned column, between −128 and 127 for the signed column.

The “positive − negative” of the exams

Every table of the exams has a subtraction of a positive minus a negative that gives ERROR in both columns. Unsigned, it is bound to happen: the first word starts with 0 and the second with 1, so a < b. Signed, a − b is a large positive number: as soon as it goes over 127, the result lands among the negatives. 2025-2026 exam: 0101 0100 − 1001 1100, with 84 − 156 = −72 < 0 unsigned and 84 − (−100) = 184 > 127 signed.

Sign extension

The sign-extension operation increases the number of bits used to encode a signed integer without changing its value, by replicating the sign bit to the left. To go from 8 to 16 bits, the sign bit of the 8-bit word (bit 7) is copied to all the new bits, from bit 8 to bit 15.

Table 3.9 — Sign extension from 8 to 16 bits (examples of the course, p. 28)
Decimal Representation8-bit Binary Word16-bit Binary Word

Pitfall: padding with 0s

Padding a negative number with 0s changes its value: 0000 0000 1000 11012 = 141, whereas 1111 1111 1000 11012 = −115. Leading 0s are right for an unsigned integer (part 3) or for a positive one, whose sign bit is precisely 0.

Sign extension came up in none of the 10 exams. It is part of chapter 1, hence of Moodle test 2.

Part 8 course § V

Other codes: BCD, Gray, ASCII

There is a multiplicity of codes for various types of applications. The course presents three of them.

BCD

BCD stands for Binary-Coded Decimal. This code is mostly used to display decimal numbers. Each decimal digit is encoded separately into a group of four bits: 3,76810 = 0011 0111 0110 1000BCD.

Decimal3768 BCD0011011101101000
Pitfall: BCD is not binary

In natural binary, 3,76810 = 1110 1011 10002: nothing in common. In BCD, a group never goes beyond 1001 (9): the groups 1010 to 1111 are not used.

Gray code or reflected binary code

The Gray code is another way to encode unsigned integers. Its main characteristic: only one bit changes from one pattern to the next. That is the reason why it is used in some algorithms and specific applications, in error correction and in Karnaugh maps, which the course will describe later.

It is also called the reflected binary code: its least significant bits are symmetric about the axes drawn in the table. To build the list, copy the previous list as a mirror image after it, then put a 0 in front of the first half and a 1 in front of the second:

1 bit: 0, 1
2 bits: 00, 01, 11, 10
3 bits: 000, 001, 011, 010, 110, 111, 101, 100

The Gray code is an unweighted code: unlike natural binary, its digits do not have any weights, and it is not decoded with a sum of weights.

To check a line (not in the course)

The leftmost bit of the Gray code is that of natural binary; each following bit is 1 when the binary bit in the same position differs from its left neighbour. For 11 = 10112: 1, then 1 ≠ 0 gives 1, 0 ≠ 1 gives 1, 1 = 1 gives 0. The result is 1110, as in the table.

ASCII

ASCII stands for American Standard Code for Information Interchange. The ASCII code is used to encode characters. The three examples of the course:

  • ‘0’ = 3016 = 0011 00002
  • ‘A’ = 4116 = 0100 00012
  • ‘a’ = 6116 = 0110 00012

The course does not give the table. The digits follow one another in order, from ‘1’ = 3116 to ‘9’ = 3916, and so do the capital letters (‘B’ = 4216) and the lowercase letters (‘b’ = 6216).

Pitfall: the character ‘0’ is not the integer 0

The character ‘0’ is the word 0011 0000, that is 3016 = 48; the integer 0 in 8 bits is 0000 0000. Likewise, ‘A’ and ‘a’ differ by 6116 − 4116 = 2016 = 32.

In the exam

BCD, Gray and ASCII came up in none of the 10 exams, and neither Tutorial 1 nor Tutorial 2 practises them. They are part of chapter 1: Moodle test 2 may ask about them, on the definitions and the examples of the course.
