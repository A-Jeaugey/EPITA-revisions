Part 1 scientific notation

Fixed point and floating point

In Chapter 1, the point of a binary number sits at a fixed place: 100 0011.0112. A computer could store a real number this way, with a fixed number of bits before the point and a fixed number after it. This is fixed point.

On 8 bits, with 4 bits after the point: 0101.10002 = 5.510. The largest number is 1111.11112 = 15.937510, and two neighboring numbers are always 0.00012 = 0.062510 apart. There is no way to write 1,000 or 0.001: fixed point wastes its bits.

Floating point works like scientific notation: keep the significant digits, and let a power say where the point goes. In base 10, 67.375 = 6.7375 × 101. In base 2, the idea is the same with powers of 2:

67.37510 = 100 0011.0112
= 1.0000 1101 12 × 26

Move the point just after the first 1: this is the normalized form, ± 1.xxx × 2e. Each place to the left adds 1 to the exponent e; each place to the right takes 1 off. The numbers from the exams:

Table 4.1 — Normalized form of four numbers from the 2023 and 2025 exams
DecimalBinaryNormalizedPoint

67.37510100 0011.01121.0000 1101 12 × 266 places left

Remember
  • In base 2, the only non-zero digit is 1: a normalized number always starts with “1.”. The IEEE 754 standard uses this to avoid storing that 1 (part 2).
  • A negative number is written the same way, with a minus sign in front: −12.5 = −1100.12 = −1.10012 × 23.
  • “Floating”: the point goes wherever the exponent says. With the same number of significant digits, you can write 2−100 as easily as 2100.
Pitfall: one digit before the point, and it is a 1

10.112 × 2−2 = 0.10112 × 20 = 1.0112 × 2−1: three ways to write 0.6875, but only the last one is normalized. Count the places from the original point: in 0.1011, the point moves one place to the right to get past the first 1, so e = −1.

Part 2 IEEE 754 standard

The single-precision format: S, E, M

The IEEE 754 standard stores a normalized real number in a 32-bit word in single precision (64 bits in double precision, part 5). The word has three fields, which the exams call S, E and M:

Table 4.2 — The three fields of a single-precision word (bit 31 on the left, bit 0 on the right)
FieldBitsBit numbersContents
S · sign1310: positive; 1: negative
E · biased exponent830 to 23E = e + 127, unsigned integer
M · mantissa2322 to 0the digits after the point of 1.M
value = (−1)S × 1.M × 2E − 127
for a normalized number: 1 ≤ E ≤ 254

Here −12.5 = −1.10012 × 23: S = 1 (negative), E = 3 + 127 = 130 = 1000 00102, M = 1001 0000 0000 0000 0000 000.

The hidden 1. In 1.M, the leading 1 is always there, so it is not stored. M only holds the digits after the point, padded on the right with 0s up to 23 bits. One bit is gained: 24 significant digits for 23 stored bits.

The bias. The exponent e can be negative (0.0937510 = 1.12 × 2−4). Rather than encoding it in two’s complement, 127 is added to it: E = e + 127 is a positive integer, stored unsigned on 8 bits. E = 0 and E = 255 are reserved (part 6): this leaves E from 1 to 254, so e from −126 to 127.

The exponent linesingle precision · bias 127
E = 0normalizedE = 255

Why a bias rather than two’s complement

With the bias, E grows with the exponent. For two positive numbers, the larger one therefore has the larger word, read as an unsigned integer: 3F80 0000 (1, E = 127) is smaller than 4286 C000 (67.375, E = 133). Comparing two positive floats comes down to comparing two integers.

Pitfall: E is not the exponent, M is not the whole mantissa
  • E = 133 means e = 133 − 127 = 6. To read the exponent, always subtract the bias.
  • M = 1001 0… means 1.1001…2: the leading 1 is missing, and you must put it back to read the value.

Vocabulary: the English exams only use the letters S, E and M and the words “single-precision” and “double-precision” (“single-precision IEEE-754 representations”, “double-precision IEEE-754 words”). Sign, biased exponent, bias, mantissa and hidden bit are the usual textbook terms (the standard itself says significand rather than mantissa).

Part 3 exams, exercise 4.1

Encoding a real number in single precision

The method, with the example of 67.375 (2023 exam):

  1. Sign. S = 0 if the number is positive, 1 if it is negative.

    67.375 > 0, so S = 0.

  2. Binary. Convert the absolute value: successive divisions for the integer part, successive multiplications for the fractional part (Chapter 1, parts 6 and 7).

    67 = 100 00112; 0.375 × 2 = 0.75 → 0; 0.75 × 2 = 1.5 → 1; 0.5 × 2 = 1 → 1. So 67.37510 = 100 0011.0112.

  3. Normalization. Move the point just after the first 1 and count the places: this is the exponent e.

    100 0011.0112 = 1.0000 1101 12 × 26: 6 places to the left, e = 6.

  4. Exponent. E = e + 127, written in binary on 8 bits, leading zeros included.

    E = 6 + 127 = 133 = 1000 01012.

  5. Mantissa. The digits after the point of 1.xxx, without the leading 1, padded on the right with 0s up to 23 bits.

    M = 0000 1101 1 then 14 zeros = 0000 1101 1000 0000 0000 000.

  6. The word (if asked). S, E and M one after the other, regrouped in groups of 4 bits, then in hexadecimal.

    0 1000 0101 0000 1101 1000 0000 0000 000
    = 0100 0010 1000 0110 1100 0000 0000 0000 = 4286 C000

On the exam paper: S 0 · E 10000101 · M 00001101100000000000000

Your turn. The tool repeats these steps for any number. It starts from 0.09375 (2025-2026 exam), which is less than 1: the point moves to the right and the exponent is negative.

Pitfall: common mistakes
  • E on 8 bits, leading zeros included: 0.0937510 = 1.12 × 2−4, so E = −4 + 127 = 123 = 01111011, not 1111011.
  • The leading 1 is not written: 32010 = 1.012 × 28, so M = 01000000000000000000000, not 1010…
  • M is padded on the right. 0s added on the left (0000…0001) shift the digits: that is another number.
  • Negative: only S changes. −12.5 has the same E and the same M as 12.5, with S = 1; the word then starts with a hexadecimal digit ≥ 8: C148 0000.

Part 4 the reverse path

Decoding a word

To read a word back, take the reverse path: split the fields, subtract the bias, put the hidden 1 back. Example: 43A2 8000, the 325 of the 2023 exam.

  1. In binary: 4 bits per hexadecimal digit.

    43A2 8000 = 0100 0011 1010 0010 1000 0000 0000 0000

  2. Split: S = the 1st bit, E = the next 8, M = the last 23.

    0 1000 0111 0100 0101 0000 0000 0000 000: S = 0, E = 1000 01112 = 135.

  3. Subtract the bias: e = E − 127.

    e = 135 − 127 = 8.

  4. Put the 1 back before the point: the value is 1.M × 2e. The trailing 0s of M do not count.

    1.0100 01012 × 28 = 1 0100 01012 = 325.

Pitfall: in single precision, E straddles two hexadecimal digits

1 + 8 = 9 bits: S and E do not fill a whole number of hexadecimal digits. In 43A2, the 4 (0100) holds S and the first 3 bits of E, and the last bit of E is the first bit of the A (1010). Always split in binary, never directly on the hexadecimal digits.

The k × 2n form. The exams ask for the value in the form k × 2n, with k and n integers. Move the point of 1.M to just after its last 1: each place takes 1 off the exponent. Example: 3F30 0000 (the 0.6875 of 2023) has E = 0111 11102 = 126, so e = −1, and M = 0110 0…:

1.0112 × 2−1 = 10112 × 2−4
= 11 × 2−4 = 0.687510
Pitfall: forgetting the hidden 1

With M alone, without the leading 1, you would read 0.0112 × 2−1 = 0.187510 instead of 0.6875. The 1 is not in the word, but it is in the value (except for the special values of part 6).

Double precision

Double precision follows the same rules on 64 bits: S on 1 bit, E on 11 bits with a bias of 1023, M on 52 bits. The two biases are 127 = 27 − 1 and 1023 = 210 − 1.

Table 4.3 — Single and double precision
SingleDouble
Word32 bits, 8 hexadecimal digits64 bits, 16 hexadecimal digits
S · E · M1 · 8 · 23 bits1 · 11 · 52 bits
Bias1271023
E of normalized numbers1 to 2541 to 2,046
Exponent e−126 to 127−1022 to 1023
Value1.M × 2E − 1271.M × 2E − 1023

The hexadecimal shortcut. 1 + 11 = 12 bits, exactly 3 hexadecimal digits: S and E take up the first 3 digits of the word, M the last 13. If the first digit is less than 8, S = 0 and E can be read directly from the first 3 digits. If it is 8 or more, S = 1 and you subtract 80016.

Example from the 2023 exam, 3548 0000 0000 0000:

  1. S and E: the first 3 digits. 3 < 8, so S = 0.

    3548 0000 0000 0000 → E = 35416 = 3 × 256 + 5 × 16 + 4 = 852

  2. Exponent: e = E − 1023.

    e = 852 − 1023 = −171

  3. M: the last 13 digits. Only the first one is not zero: 8 = 1000.

    M = 1000 0000 … → 1.M = 1.12

  4. Value: 1.M × 2e, then the k × 2n form.

    1.12 × 2−171 = 112 × 2−172 = 3 × 2−172

A negative word. C029 0000 0000 0000 starts with C ≥ 8: S = 1 and E = C0216 − 80016 = 40216 = 1026, so e = 3. M starts with 9 = 1001: the value is −1.10012 × 23 = −1100.12 = −12.5, the number from part 2.

The tool decodes the word from the 2025-2026 exam. On a narrow screen, the 64 bits run over several lines, still in groups of 4.

Encoding in double precision (never asked in the exams): same steps, with E = e + 1023 on 11 bits and M on 52 bits. For 67.375: E = 6 + 1023 = 1029 = 100 0000 01012, and the word is 4050 D800 0000 0000.

Pitfall: keeping single-precision habits
  • Bias 1023, not 127: with 127, 3548… would give e = 852 − 127 = 725, a huge number instead of a tiny one.
  • E on 11 bits: with S = 0, these are the first 3 whole hexadecimal digits, not 8 bits.
  • M on 52 bits = 13 hexadecimal digits, aligned this time: in double precision, the boundary between E and M falls between two digits.

Part 6 standard · exams

Zeros, infinities, NaN, denormalized numbers

The two extreme values of E do not encode normalized numbers. E all zeros and E all ones are reserved for four families of values. Look at E first:

Table 4.4 — Reading a double-precision word from E and M (examples from the exams)
EMValueExample
00±00000 0000 0000 0000 = +0
0≠ 0denormalized: ±0.M × 2−1022000A 8000 0000 0000 = 21 × 2−1027 (2023)
1 to 2,046anynormalized: ±1.M × 2E − 10230015 8000 0000 0000 = 43 × 2−1027 (2024)
2,047 (all ones)0±∞7FF0 0000 0000 0000 = +∞ (2024)
2,047 (all ones)≠ 0NaN7FF0 0001 0000 0000 = NaN (2025)

Zero. 0 cannot be written 1.M × 2e: it has its own code, E = 0 and M = 0. The sign gives two zeros, +0 and −0 (0000 0000 and 8000 0000 in single precision), equal in value.

Infinities. E all ones and M = 0: +∞ if S = 0, −∞ if S = 1. This is the result of a calculation that goes beyond the largest representable number, or of 1 / 0. In single precision: 7F80 0000 and FF80 0000.

NaN (Not a Number). E all ones and M ≠ 0: this is the result of 0 / 0, or of the square root of a negative number. A single 1 bit in M is enough: 7FF0 0001 0000 0000 is a NaN (2025-2026 exam). The sign of a NaN does not matter: the 2023 answer key writes “NaN” for FFFF 0000 0000 0000, which has S = 1.

Denormalized numbers. The smallest positive normalized number is 1.0 × 2−1022 (E = 1). Below it, E = 0 and M ≠ 0 encode denormalized numbers (also called subnormal): no hidden 1, and the exponent stays that of E = 1, i.e. −1022 (−126 in single precision). They fill the gap between 0 and the smallest normalized number.

denormalized, double: ±0.M × 2−1022
denormalized, single: ±0.M × 2−126
000A 8000 0000 0000: E = 0, M = 1010 1000 0…
0.1010 12 × 2−1022 = 1 01012 × 2−1027 = 21 × 2−1027
Pitfall from the 2024 exam: E = 1 is a normalized number

0015 8000 0000 0000 looks like a denormalized number, but E = 00116 = 1: it is not 0. It is a normalized number, with its hidden 1: 1.0101 12 × 2−1022 = 10 10112 × 2−1027 = 43 × 2−1027.

Both words have the same exponent, −1022: E = 1 gives 1 − 1023 = −1022, and the denormalized rule keeps that exponent. Writing 0.M × 2−1023 for the 2023 word would give 21 × 2−1028: wrong.

Part 7 IEEE 754 standard

Precision: the numbers that cannot be written

M has only 23 bits. A real number is encoded exactly only if its normalized form fits in 24 significant digits (the 1 and the 23 of M), with an exponent within the limits. In other words, it must be k / 2n, a fraction whose denominator is a power of 2, with k not too large.

All the numbers to encode in the exams are of this kind: 67.375 = 539 / 8, 0.6875 = 11 / 16, 0.09375 = 3 / 32. The longest, 67.375, has 10 significant binary digits. Their encoding is exact, without rounding.

0.1 is not exact. Its binary expansion is infinite (Chapter 1, part 7): 0.110 = 0.0001 1001 1001 1001 1001…2, i.e. 1.1001 1001 1001…2 × 2−4. It has to be cut after the 23rd bit of M.

The standard’s rule: round to nearest

Look at what is left after the 23rd bit of M:

  • less than half a unit of the last bit (the next bit is 0): keep the 23 bits as they are;
  • more than half (the next bit is 1, and at least one other 1 follows): add 1 to the last bit;
  • exactly half (a 1 followed by 0s only): of the two neighbors, choose the one whose last bit is 0 (“ties to even”).
0.1 = 1.1001 1001 1001 1001 1001 1001 1001 1…2 × 2−4
what is left, 1 1001…, is more than half: round up
M = 1001 1001 1001 1001 1001 101

Round or truncate?

We do not have Chapter 2 or Tutorial 3: we do not know whether your course rounds to nearest, as the standard does, or truncates, like the “n digits after the point” of Chapter 1. The exams do not settle it: all their numbers are exact. If an exercise gives you a number like 0.1, apply your course’s rule.

How many decimal digits? 24 significant binary digits give about 7 decimal digits (2−23 ≈ 1.19 × 10−7); in double precision, 53 binary digits give 15 to 16 decimal digits (2−52 ≈ 2.22 × 10−16). Integers also run out of room: in single precision, every integer up to 224 = 16,777,216 is exact, but 16,777,217 = 224 + 1 needs 25 significant digits. The extra bit is worth exactly half: it is a tie, so it is rounded to even, and the word 4B80 0000 is worth 16,777,216.

In a program. The errors add up as calculations go on. In OCaml, whose float values are doubles, 0.1 +. 0.2 gives 0.300000000000000044 and 0.1 +. 0.2 = 0.3 is false. To compare two floats, check instead whether they are very close to each other.

Pitfall: not every number with a fractional part is inexact

0.5, 0.375, 0.15625 or 59.75 are encoded exactly: their fractional part is a sum of negative powers of 2, and their successive multiplications by 2 stop. Only numbers whose binary expansion is infinite (0.1, 0.2, 0.3…) or too long need rounding.

Part 8 2023 and 2025 answer keys

Answering in the exam format

Since 2023, the last exercise of the exam has always been the same, in two questions. The instructions, word for word (English version):

Exercise 4 · 2023 and 2025-2026 exams

1. Convert the numbers below into their single-precision IEEE-754 representations. Write down the final result in its binary form and specify the three fields.

2. Convert the double-precision IEEE-754 words below into their associated representations. If a representation is a number, use the base-10 following form: k×2n where k and n are integers (either positive or negative).

The answer-key format
  • Encoding: three boxes, in binary, in one block without spaces. S: 1 digit. E: 8 digits, leading zeros included (01111011). M: 23 digits, trailing zeros included. The hexadecimal word is not asked for.
  • Decoding: the words are given in hexadecimal, in groups of 4 (“IEEE-754 Representation (base 16)”). The answer is written k × 2n with k odd: 3 × 2−172, not 6 × 2−173 (equal, but not the form of the answer keys).
  • Quick rule: if 1.M has f digits after the point (up to the last 1), k is the binary integer 1M and n = e − f. For a denormalized number, k is the integer M without its leading 0s, and n = −1022 − f.
  • NaN is written “NaN”, without a sign. Infinity was asked only once (2024), with no answer key: “+∞” is the most likely answer.

Pitfall: what costs points
  • An E with 7 digits or an M with 22 or 24 digits: count the digits before handing in.
  • k × 2n not reduced: each digit after the point of 1.M takes 1 off n. 1.012 × 2−379 = 1012 × 2−381 = 5 × 2−381.
  • The bias 127 in double precision, or E = 0 read as a normalized number.
  • A NaN taken for an infinity because M has a single 1 bit, far to the right.
In the exam
  • IEEE 754 came up in the last 3 exams (2023, 2024, 2025-2026 B1 Examination), never before: always Exercise 4, for 6, 6 then 4 points out of 20.
  • Always two questions: encode 2 or 3 real numbers in single precision, then decode 2 or 3 double-precision words.
  • The 8 numbers to encode are positive and exact: a 9-bit integer every time (325, 298, 320: E = 10000111 all three times), a number less than 1 every time, a number greater than 1 with a fractional part in 2023 and 2024.
  • The words to decode: a normalized number with a very negative exponent every time, a NaN (2023, 2025), +∞ (2024), a denormalized number (2023), the E = 1 trap (2024).
  • Never asked: encoding in double precision, decoding in single precision, a negative number, zero, rounding.
  • The 2023 and 2025 answer keys are correct (all worked out again); the 2024 exam has no answer key and no English version.
  • Moodle test 2 (23 to 25 October 2026, 30 min, one attempt) covers Chapters 1 and 2 of the course, Tutorials 1 and 2 and exercises 1 to 4 of Tutorial 3; its exact format is not known.
