Part 1 course § II.1

Binary addition: carry from 2

The course says it in one sentence: binary operations “follow the same rules” as decimal ones. Line the numbers up on the right, ones under ones, and add column by column, from right to left. The carry of a column is added to the next column.

The only new thing: in base 2, two is already written 10. As soon as a column reaches 2, write down the rightmost digit and carry the rest. With two numbers and a carry, there are only four cases:

Table 2.1 — A column of two numbers, with or without a carry
ColumnSum in base 2Write downCarry
0 + 000none
0 + 1 or 1 + 011none
1 + 110 (two)01
1 + 1 + 111 (three)11

The course’s example, in 8 steps. Carries are written small, above the column that receives them:

10101102 + 10111012 = 101100112
= 86 + 93 = 179

The course writes each step like this: “1 + 1 = 10 (0 carry 1)”, “1 + 1 + 1 = 11 (1 carry 1)”. The last carry, on the left, creates one more digit: 7 digits to start with, 8 in the result.

More than two numbers Tutorial 1 · ex. 3.1 A column can then hold three or four 1s, plus the carry. Work out the sum of the column, write it in binary, write down its last digit and carry all the rest. The first column of 1100011 + 1110111 + 1001011 + 101 holds four 1s: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4 = 1002. Write down 0 and carry 102 = 2.

A carry of 2: two ways to write it

The course has no example of a carry of 2: it only adds two binary numbers at a time, and the exam keys only write the result. Both ways below are right and give the same total:

  • “10” above the next column: that column receives 102 = 2. This is what the tools on this site do.
  • The split carry: 0 above the next column, 1 above the one after. A 1 two columns further left is worth 2 in the next column. But the marks soon pile up.

  • Line up on the right, on the ones: the 101 of the tutorial goes under the last three digits, not under the first three.

Part 2 course § II.2

Binary subtraction: the course’s borrow method

Subtract column by column, from right to left: 1 − 0 = 1, 1 − 1 = 0, 0 − 0 = 0. The only hard case is a top digit smaller than the bottom one: 0 − 1. You borrow 1. The course does it in two moves, always the same:

  1. The top digit receives the base: 0 becomes 10 (two), 1 becomes 11 (three). The course writes a small 1 in front of the digit: 10.

  2. In return, the bottom digit of the next column receives + 1. The course writes 1+1 or 1+0.

  3. In the next column, you therefore subtract the bottom digit plus 1. If that digit is already 1, you subtract 1 + 1 = 10, and you often have to borrow again.

The bottom number has one digit less: it is completed with a 0 on the left (0110110), which receives the last borrow. The 7 steps, in the course’s words:

Step 1: 1 − 0 = 1
Step 2: 0 − 1 → borrow 1 → 10 − 1 = 1
Step 3: 0 − (1 + 1) = 0 − 10 → borrow 1 → 10 − 10 = 0
Step 4: 1 − (1 + 0) = 1 − 1 = 0
Step 5: 0 − 1 → borrow 1 → 10 − 1 = 1
Step 6: 1 − (1 + 1) = 1 − 10 → borrow 1 → 11 − 10 = 1
Step 7: 1 − (1 + 0) = 1 − 1 = 0
11010012 − 1101102 = 1100112
= 105 − 54 = 51
Why it works

In one column, the top digit gains 2 units of that column. In the next column, the bottom digit gains 1, and one unit of the next column is also worth 2 units of the first. The same value has been added to both numbers: their difference does not change. This is the borrow “by compensation” often taught in base 10.

Pitfall: step 5 of the course, and two methods mixed up
  • The course (French and English) prints step 5 as “0 – 1 = 1 → borrow 1 → 10 – 1 = 1”. The “= 1” should not be there: 0 − 1 is not 1. You borrow straight away, as in step 2.
  • You may have learnt another method: take 1 away from the top digit of the next column. It gives the same result, but follow only one of them. Mixing both counts the borrow twice. The course puts the borrow at the bottom.
  • A column “0 − (1 + 1)” reads 0 − 10: borrow, 10 − 10 = 0, and the borrow goes on to the next column.

Figure 1, at the top of the page, sets out the subtraction of the 2025-2026 exam with the same method: 9 digits minus 8 digits, as in every exam.

If the top number is the smaller one

Then a − b = −(b − a): swap the two numbers, set out b − a and write a minus sign in front of the result. The exams never ask for it: the result is always positive. The tool in part 7 does it if you try. On 8 bits (chapter 3), it is a different story: no minus sign, and the final borrow signals an overflow.

Part 3 course § II.3

Binary multiplication: copy and shift

Write the multiplicand on top and the multiplier below it. Each digit of the multiplier gives a partial product. In binary, the multiplication table fits on one line: 0 × 0 = 0 × 1 = 0 and 1 × 1 = 1. So a partial product is either a row of zeros or a copy of the multiplicand. The course’s steps:

  1. The rightmost multiplier digit is 1: write down the multiplicand.

  2. The next multiplier digit is 0: write down zeros shifted left.

  3. The next multiplier digit is 1: write down the multiplicand shifted left.

  4. Add the partial products.

101102 × 1012 = 11011102
= 22 × 5 = 110

The shift. The multiplier digit of weight 2k gives a row shifted k places to the left: shifting one place multiplies by 102 = 2, just as adding a 0 on the right multiplies by 10 in base 10.

The exam keys skip the rows of zeros: they only write the rows of the 1 digits, each one preceded by “+”. The 2023-2024 exam, reused as is in 2025-2026: the multiplier 101010 has its 1s at weights 21, 23 and 25, so three rows, shifted 1, 3 and 5 places.

101010101002 × 1010102 = 11011111110010002
= 1,364 × 42 = 57,288

The final addition adds several numbers (part 1): with three rows, a column can hold three 1s and a carry. In 2021-2022, the multiplier 1010110 gave four partial products, and several columns a carry of 10.

Pitfall: skipping a row without shifting the next one

Whether you write the rows of zeros or not, the partial product of the digit of weight 2k is shifted k places. With 101010, the first row written is already shifted one place, because the rightmost digit is 0. Forgetting that place for every row gives a result half as big as it should be.

Rough-work tip

The product does not change when the two numbers are swapped. Taking as multiplier the one with fewer 1s gives fewer rows: in 10101010100 × 101010, the multiplier has three 1s and the multiplicand five.

Practise: Tutorial 1, ex. 3.3.

Part 4 course § II.4

Binary long division

Set out the division as in decimal: the dividend on the left, the divisor on the right, the quotient under the divisor. In base 2, the divisor goes into a partial remainder 0 or 1 time: no table to know, you only have to compare. The course’s steps:

  1. Take the shortest leading part of the dividend that contains the divisor: the divisor “goes into” those digits one time. Record a ‘1’ in the quotient, then subtract the divisor.

  2. Bring down the next digit from the dividend to the remainder.

  3. If the new remainder is smaller than the divisor, record a ‘0’ in the quotient. Otherwise, record a ‘1’ and subtract the divisor.

  4. Repeat until the last digit of the dividend. The final remainder is 0: the division is exact.

1010011012 / 10012 = 1001012
= 333 / 9 = 37
Comparing two binary numbers

Without leading zeros, the one with more digits is the larger. With the same number of digits, compare from left to right: the first different digit decides. 1011 > 1001 (third digit), 101 < 1001 (fewer digits).

What the key writes. The 2025-2026 key only keeps the partial remainders at each subtraction: 1011, then 1001, then 0, with the digits brought down in bold. The course also writes the “− divisor” rows; so do the keys from 2016 to 2021.

Pitfall: the 0 forgotten in the quotient

Each digit brought down gives one quotient digit, 0 or 1. Here, after 10102 − 10012 = 12, you bring down 0 (remainder 10) then 1 (remainder 101): twice smaller than 1001, so two 0s in the quotient. Forgetting them gives 1101 instead of 100101.

Division: the binary point, the remainder, the digits asked for

When the last digit of the dividend has been brought down and the remainder is not 0, the course goes on (step 7): record a binary point in the quotient, then bring down an extra 0 to the remainder, and carry on with the same method. Each extra 0 gives one digit after the point. The course’s example:

111110012 / 1102 = 101001.12
= 249 / 6 = 41.5

After the last digit (1), the remainder is 11. Record the point, bring down an extra 0: 110, which contains the divisor one time. The final remainder is 0, and 0.12 = 0.5.

“n digits after the point” Tutorial 1 · ex. 3.4 Often, the division never comes out exactly. The question then sets the number of digits: bring down an extra 0 that many times, and stop there, without rounding, as for the conversions of chapter 1.

11001102 / 1012 → 10100.0110012
102 / 5 = 20.4

The final remainder is 11: the 7th digit would be 1. It is not added. Rounding would give 10100.011010, which is not what long division does.

The integer quotient and the remainder. With no digit asked for after the point, stop at the last digit of the dividend: you get an integer quotient and a remainder, smaller than the divisor. They satisfy dividend = divisor × quotient + remainder. With the second division of Tutorial 1:

10110001002 = 11012 × 1101102 + 1102
= 13 × 54 + 6 = 708

The tutorial asks for 4 digits after the point: 10110001002 / 11012 → 110110.01112, and the final remainder is 101.

Pitfall: placing the point
  • The point goes into the quotient when the first extra 0 is brought down, after the last digit of the dividend, not before.
  • Each extra 0 gives a quotient digit, even when it is 0: in the tutorial’s division, the first digit after the point is 0 (100 is smaller than 101).
  • Stop at the number of digits asked for, without rounding the last one.
In the exam

The 9 divisions in the 2016 to 2025 exams are exact: integer quotient, remainder 0, no digit after the point. A non-zero final remainder in the exam almost certainly signals a mistake. Digits after the point are still on the syllabus (course and Tutorial 1).

Part 6 course § II.5

Hexadecimal addition: go through decimal

The course’s method: “add each column with the decimal values of the digits. Then convert the result of a column to hexadecimal and propagate the carry if it exists”. So, for each column:

  1. Replace each letter with its value: A = 10, B = 11, C = 12, D = 13, E = 14, F = 15.

  2. Add in decimal, with the carry from the previous column.

  3. Write that sum in hexadecimal: write down the right digit, carry the left one. A sum from 16 to 31 gives a carry of 1; from 32 to 47, a carry of 2.

Reading the table

Find the sum of the column: its row gives the digit to write down, its column the carry. The course’s example: 5 + 10 + 15 = 30 → row E, column “carry 1”: write down E and carry 1, since 30 = 1E16.

Without the table: take 16 (or 32) away from the sum; what is left is the digit written down.

A carry of 2 in hexadecimal

With three numbers, a column is at most 15 + 15 + 15 + 2 = 47 = 2F16: the carry can be 2. It is written with a single digit, above the next column: FFF16 + FFF16 + FFF16 = 2FFD16. Nothing to split as in binary (part 1).

Step 1: 516 + A16 + F16 = 510 + 1010 + 1510 = 3010; 3010 = 1E16 (carry = 1)
Step 2: 116 + B16 + 116 + 216 = 110 + 1110 + 110 + 210 = 1510; 1510 = F16 (no carry)
Step 3: 316 + C16 + D16 = 310 + 1210 + 1310 = 2810; 2810 = 1C16 (carry = 1)
3B516 + C1A16 + D2F16 = 1CFE16
= 949 + 3,098 + 3,375 = 7,422

The last carry is written on the left: the result has one more digit than the numbers added. The 2025-2026 exam has the same shape, three numbers of three digits:

76316 + FAA16 + 5B616 = 1CC316
= 1,891 + 4,010 + 1,462 = 7,363
Pitfall: writing the decimal sum
  • The column 5 + 10 + 15 = 30 is written E, carry 1, not “30”, nor “0, carry 3”: the carry counts sixteens, not tens.
  • A letter one unit off spoils the whole column: go over A = 10 … F = 15 before you start.
  • The carry is added once, in the next column only (step 2 of the course: 1 + B + 1 + 2, the carry first).

Checking a result

A long operation can be checked in a minute, and the most common mistake (a forgotten carry or borrow) changes the result a lot. Three checks, from the safest to the quickest.

1. Go back to decimal. Convert the numbers (chapter 1, part 5), redo the operation in decimal and compare with the value of the result you set out. The four operations of the 2025-2026 exam:

Table 2.3 — Exercise 2 of the 2025-2026 exam, checked in decimal

2. The inverse operation, without leaving the base. A subtraction is checked with an addition: result + bottom number = top number. An exact division, with a multiplication: quotient × divisor = dividend.

100110112 + 110110112 = 1011101102: the top number comes back.
1001012 × 10012 = 1010011012: the dividend comes back.

3. Orders of magnitude, at a glance: the number of digits and the last digit.

The last digit
  • In binary, a product ends with as many 0s as its two factors put together: 10101010100 (two 0s) × 101010 (one 0) ends with three 0s, …1000.
  • In hexadecimal, the last digit of a sum only depends on the last digits: 3 + 10 + 6 = 19 = 1316, so 763 + FAA + 5B6 ends with 3.

Set out your own operation. Type two numbers (or more for an addition) separated by +, −, × or /: the tool sets it out with the course’s method and checks it in decimal.

Part 8 exams 2016-2025

Setting it out in the exam

The question is always the same: “Perform the operations below. Show all calculations.” Since 2023, it is the only exercise of the paper that asks for the working; the general instruction says “Do not show any calculation unless you are explicitly asked”. The operations are set out in grids headed “Base 2” or “Base 16”, one box per digit.

Addition, subtraction

Line up on the right, one digit per box. Write the carries or borrows small above the digits, then the result under the line. The keys only write the result, but the carries are the working asked for: they cost nothing.

Multiplication

Multiplicand, multiplier, then one row per 1 digit of the multiplier, shifted according to its position and preceded by “+”, then the sum. The keys skip the rows of zeros; the course writes them.

Division

Dividend on the left, divisor on the right, quotient under the divisor, then each partial remainder with the digit brought down. The keys from 2016 to 2021 also write the “− divisor” rows; those of 2022, 2023 and 2025, only the remainders.

Everywhere

A readable result, under the line, without crossings-out. No pencil and no red ink (exam instruction since 2023). If you run out of room, the paper provides a blank frame at the end.

The mistakes that cost points
  • Subtraction: the borrow carried over to the wrong digit, or twice (part 2).
  • Addition: the carry of 10 from a column of four 1s taken as 1; the last carry forgotten (part 1).
  • Hexadecimal: the decimal sum of a column copied as it is (“30” instead of E, carry 1) (part 6).
  • Multiplication: a row shifted wrongly after a skipped row of zeros (part 3).
  • Division: a 0 forgotten in the quotient; a point placed too early; a last digit rounded (parts 4 and 5).
Two typos in the course
  • Page 13 of the French version: “effectuons la multiplication suivante : 11111001 / 110”. It is a division, as the English version says.
  • Page 12, step 5 (both versions): “0 – 1 = 1 → borrow 1”. The “= 1” should not be there (part 2).

    In the exam
    • An exercise of operations to set out in 9 exams out of 10 (all but 2020-2021), worth 4 or 5 points until 2023, 4 points in 2024 and 6 points in 2025.
