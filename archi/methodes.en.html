Method 1 exercise 1 · sheet 1, part 5

To base 10: the sum of products

  1. Number the positions from the point: 0 for the digit just left of it, then 1, 2, 3… to the left, −1, −2… to the right.

  2. Write each digit × its weight: the base raised to the position. In hexadecimal, A = 10 … F = 15.

  3. Add up. In binary, just add the weights of the 1 bits. After the point: 2−1 = 0.5; 2−2 = 0.25; 2−3 = 0.125; 2−4 = 0.0625; 2−5 = 0.03125; and in base 16: 16−1 = 0.0625; 16−2 = 0.00390625.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026, exercise 1

1101.1000 12 = 23 + 22 + 20 + 2−1 + 2−5
= 8 + 4 + 1 + 0.5 + 0.03125
= 13.5312510
28.2816 = 2×161 + 8×160 + 2×16−1 + 8×16−2
= 32 + 8 + 0.125 + 0.03125
= 40.1562510
On the exam paper

The result only, in decimal: 40.15625. No fraction or power (“write down 0.25 and not ¼ or 2−2”). Without a number of digits, the result is always finite in the papers: write all its digits.

Trap

Reading the hexadecimal fractional part as decimal: 28.2816 is neither 40.28 nor 40.175. And from one row to the next, the numbers look alike on purpose: 1A516 = 421, but 1BA16 = 442 the year before.

Method 2 exercise 1 · sheet 1, parts 6 and 7

From base 10: divisions, multiplications, truncation

  1. Split the integer part and the fractional part: each one is converted on its own.

  2. Integer part: successive divisions by the base, until the quotient is 0. Read the remainders from the last to the first. In base 16, a remainder from 10 to 15 is written A to F.

  3. Fractional part: successive multiplications by the base. The integer part of the product gives the digit; carry on with the fractional part only. Read the digits from the first to the last.

  4. Stop at the number of digits asked for, without rounding: truncate, as the course and every answer key do.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026: 40.35 to binary, 5 digits after the point

40.3510 → 10 1000.0101 12

Same paper: 22.84 to hexadecimal, 2 digits after the point

22 = 1 × 16 + 6; 0.84 × 16 = 13.44 → D; 0.44 × 16 = 7.04 → 7
22.8410 → 16.D716
Truncate, do not round

2024 paper: 28.35 to hexadecimal with 4 digits. The multiplications give 5, 9, 9, 9 and 0.6 is still left. Stop: 28.3510 → 1C.599916. Rounding would give 1C.599A: that is not the convention of the answer keys, which truncated all five times the two differed.

On the exam paper

The result only, with exactly the number of digits asked for, trailing zeros included: 10 1000.0101 1 (or 101000.01011: the answer keys use both). Hexadecimal digits in capitals: 16.D7.

Traps
  • Multiplying the whole product: after 0.84 × 16 = 13.44, carry on with 0.44, not with 13.44.
  • Writing a digit in decimal: 13 gives D. “16.137” is wrong.
  • A number that “looks decimal”: 52810 = 21016 (B1 Examination).
Method 3 exercise 1 · sheet 1, part 8

Base 2 ↔ base 16: groups of 4 bits

  1. Start from the point: groups of 4 bits leftwards for the integer part, rightwards for the fractional part.

  2. Complete the incomplete groups with 0s: on the left of the integer part, on the right of the fractional part.

  3. One group = one hexadecimal digit (the 0-to-15 table). The other way, each hexadecimal digit gives exactly 4 bits, zeros included.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026: 1011 1011.1011 01 to hexadecimal

1011 1011.1011 012 = 1011 1011.1011 01002 = BB.B416

The other way · 2023 paper: DC.81 to binary

On the exam paper

BB.B4; 1101 1100.1000 0001. The leading zeros of the integer part and the trailing zeros of the fractional part may disappear; those in the middle, never.

Trap: padding on the wrong side

Grouping the fractional part from the right gives 0010 1101, that is BB.2D: wrong. The fractional part is grouped from the point and padded on the right. This case comes up in each of the 10 papers.

Method 4 exercise 2 · sheet 2, part 2

Binary subtraction, with borrows

  1. Align on the right and pad the bottom number with 0s on the left.

  2. Column by column, from right to left: 1 − 0 = 1, 1 − 1 = 0, 0 − 0 = 0.

  3. Top digit too small: borrow 1. The top digit gains 102 (two) and the bottom digit of the next column gains 1 (the course’s method). If that bottom digit is already 1, it becomes 102 and you will borrow again.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026, exercise 2

1011101102 − 110110112 = 100110112
= 374 − 219 = 155
On the exam paper

“Show all calculations”: write the operation in the grid, one box per digit, and note the borrows. Result without leading zeros: 10011011. In the papers, the first number is always the larger one.

Trap

Taking the borrow from the top digit of the next column while also adding 1 at the bottom: the borrow only counts once. With the course’s method, it is the bottom digit that gains 1.

Method 5 exercise 2 · sheet 2, part 6

Hexadecimal addition, with two or three terms

  1. Column by column, from right to left, add the digits in decimal (A = 10 … F = 15), with the carry.

  2. Rewrite the column total in base 16: write down the right digit, carry the left one. From 16 to 31, the carry is 1; with three terms, a column can reach 15 × 3 + 2 = 47 = 2F16: carry 2.

  3. The last carry becomes the leading digit: 8 papers out of 9 create one more digit this way.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026, exercise 2 (three terms, as in 2024)

3 + A + 6 = 3 + 10 + 6 = 19 = 1316: write down 3, carry 1
1 + 6 + A + B = 1 + 6 + 10 + 11 = 28 = 1C16: write down C, carry 1
1 + 7 + F + 5 = 1 + 7 + 15 + 5 = 28 = 1C16: write down C, carry 1
76316 + FAA16 + 5B616 = 1CC316 = 7,363
On the exam paper

The written operation, the carries above, the result 1CC3. Check in decimal if you have time: 1,891 + 4,010 + 1,462 = 7,363.

Trap

Leaving a total in decimal: 28 is written 1C16, write down C (not “28”, nor “8 carry 2”).

Method 6 exercise 2 · sheet 2, part 4

Written binary division

  1. The dividend on the left, the divisor on the right, the quotient under the divisor.

  2. Take the shortest leading part of the dividend that contains the divisor: record a 1 in the quotient, subtract the divisor.

  3. Bring down the next digit. If the remainder is smaller than the divisor, record a 0 in the quotient; otherwise record a 1 and subtract. Go on until the last digit.

  4. The final remainder is 0 in every paper: otherwise, go back over your subtractions. (A division with digits after the point only exists in tutorial 1.)

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026, exercise 2

1010011012 / 10012 = 1001012
= 333 / 9 = 37
On the exam paper

The successive partial remainders, each one under the previous one, and the quotient 100101. The 2022, 2023 and 2025 answer keys only keep the remainders; the older ones also write the “− divisor” lines.

Trap

Forgetting a 0 in the quotient when the remainder is too small: the quotient loses a digit and is worth nothing. Check: 37 × 9 = 333.

Method 7 exercise 2 · sheet 2, part 3

Written binary multiplication

  1. Spot the 1s of the multiplier and their position (0 on the right).

  2. One partial product per 1: the multiplicand, shifted left by the position of that 1. The 0s of the multiplier only give rows of zeros, which the answer keys skip.

  3. Add the partial products, column by column: with three 1s in a column, 1 + 1 + 1 = 112; with four, 1002, the carry goes two columns further.

Example · B1 Examination 2025-2026 and 2023 paper, exercise 2 (the same multiplication)

101010101002 × 1010102 = 11011111110010002
= 1,364 × 42 = 57,288
On the exam paper

One row per 1 of the multiplier (here three, shifted 1, 3 and 5 places), preceded by “+”, then the sum: 1101111111001000.

Trap

Shifting each row one place more than the previous one after skipping 0s: the shift is the position of the 1, not the row number.

Method 8 exercise 3 · sheet 3, part 6

The 8-bit table: unsigned, signed, ERROR

  1. The 8-bit word. Work out the addition or subtraction and keep the 8 rightmost bits, even if a carry or a borrow goes out beyond bit 7.

  2. Unsigned: each operand is worth 0 to 255. Redo the operation in decimal; outside 0 … 255, write ERROR. For an addition, this happens when a carry goes out; for a subtraction, when the first number is the smaller one.

  3. Signed (two’s complement): a word starting with 1 is negative, its absolute value is its two’s complement. Redo the operation; outside −128 … 127, write ERROR. Otherwise, read the result word as signed.

  4. Check with the signs (tutorial 2): there is a signed overflow only for positive + positive = negative, negative + negative = positive, positive − negative = negative, negative − positive = positive.

On the exam paper

The 8-bit binary result, always; then a number or ERROR in each of the two columns. No working: “Do not show any calculation unless you are explicitly asked”.

In the papers

Each of the 4 tables of the papers has a “positive − negative” subtraction that gives ERROR in both columns, and the last three a “positive + positive” addition that overflows when signed only.

Traps
  • Mistaking the carry for a signed overflow: the carry out of an addition signals the unsigned overflow. 10001100 + 01111110 produces a carry (ERROR unsigned), but signed, −116 + 126 = 10 is right (2017 paper).
  • Leaving the binary box empty when there is an overflow.
  • Reading a negative word as unsigned in the “Signed” column: 10111101 gives −67, not 189.
Method 9 exercise 4 · sheet 4, part 3

IEEE 754: encoding a real number in single precision

  1. Sign: S = 0 for a positive number (every number in the papers), 1 for a negative one.

  2. In binary: integer part by divisions, fractional part by multiplications (method 2). In the papers, the expansion is always finite.

  3. Normalize: move the point just after the first 1, which gives 1.… × 2e. Moving it left, e is positive; moving it right (a number smaller than 1), negative.

  4. Exponent: E = e + 127, written on 8 bits, leading zeros included.

  5. Mantissa: M = the digits after the point (the leading 1 is not stored), padded on the right with 0s up to 23 bits.

Example · 2023 paper, exercise 4

67.37510 = 100 0011.0112 = 1.0000 1101 12 × 26
S = 0; E = 6 + 127 = 133 = 10000101; M = 0000 1101 1000 0000 0000 000

On the exam paper

Three boxes, in binary, without spaces: S = 0, E = 10000101, M = 00001101100000000000000 (23 digits). The hexadecimal word has never been asked.

Traps
  • Writing E without its leading zeros: 0.0937510 = 1.12 × 2−4, E = 123 = 01111011.
  • Storing the 1 before the point: 320 = 1.012 × 28 gives M = 0100…, not 1010….
  • Padding M on the left: the zeros go on the right.
Method 10 exercise 4 · sheet 4, parts 4 to 6

IEEE 754: decoding a double-precision word as k × 2n

  1. S and E: the first 3 hexadecimal digits give 12 bits, S (1 bit) then E (11 bits). If the first digit is less than 8, S = 0 and E reads directly: the three digits in hexadecimal.

  2. M: the last 13 hexadecimal digits (52 bits).

  3. The case: E = 7FF16 (all ones): infinity if M = 0, NaN otherwise. E = 0: zero if M = 0, otherwise denormalized, 0.M × 2−1022. Otherwise: 1.M × 2E − 1023.

  4. k × 2n: move the point to the right up to the last 1, to get an odd integer k; take the number of places moved off the exponent.

Example · 2023 paper, exercise 4

3548 0000 0000 0000: 354 = 0011 0101 01002 → S = 0, E = 35416 = 852
M = 8 0000 0000 000016 = 1000 0…2; exponent 852 − 1023 = −171
1.12 × 2−171 = 112 × 2−172 = 3 × 2−172

On the exam paper

3 × 2−172, with an odd k as in every answer key; NaN for a NaN, without a sign. Infinity has never been in an answer key: +∞ is the most likely way to write it.

Traps
  • Using the bias 127: in double precision, it is 1023.
  • Denormalized: the exponent is −1022, not −1023, and there is no 1 before the point: 0.101012 × 2−1022 = 21 × 2−1027.
  • Fake denormalized number: 0015 8000 0000 0000 has E = 1, it is normalized: 1.010112 × 2−1022 = 43 × 2−1027.
  • A single 1 bit in M is enough for a NaN: 7FF0 0001 0000 0000.

Before handing in your paper

!To read the day before

The traps, exercise by exercise

Conversions
  • Rounding instead of truncating: 141.310 → 8D.4C16 (not 8D.4D); 92.4710 → 5C.785116 in tutorial 1.
  • Reading the remainders the wrong way: divisions from the last to the first, multiplications from the first to the last.
  • Losing the inner zeros: DC.8116 = 1101 1100.1000 00012, not ….11.
  • Grouping the fractional part from the right: 10 0101.1100 112 = 25.CC16 (tutorial 1), not 25.33.
  • Same digits, another base: 151.32 in base 8 and in base 16 in the same table (2022).
Written operations
  • A borrow counted twice or carried to the wrong place (course’s method: the bottom digit of the next column gains 1).
  • A carry over two columns: four 1s in a column make 1002 (tutorial 1, four-term addition).
  • A hexadecimal column total left in decimal: 5 + 10 + 15 = 30 = 1E16, write down E.
  • A 0 forgotten in the quotient; partial products shifted wrongly.
8-bit integers
  • A carry mistaken for a signed overflow (and the other way round).
  • An unsigned subtraction going below 0: 11001000 − 11100010 gives ERROR unsigned, but −56 − (−30) = −26 signed (2023).
  • −128 is 10000000: its opposite does not fit in 8 signed bits. And 0111 1111 + 0000 0001 = 1000 0000: 128 unsigned, ERROR signed (2024).
  • Asymmetric ranges: from −2n−1 to 2n−1 − 1 signed; the largest unsigned is 2n − 1, not 2n.
  • Quick two’s complement: from right to left, keep the bits up to the first 1 included, invert the others.
IEEE 754
  • A forgotten bias, or the bias 127 in double precision.
  • E without its leading zeros, M without its trailing zeros, the hidden 1 stored.
  • Exponent of a number smaller than 1: negative. 0.10112 = 1.0112 × 2−1, E = 126.
  • An unreduced k: 6 × 2−173 equals 3 × 2−172, but the answer keys give an odd k.
  • The truncation rule of conversions does not hold for IEEE 754: the standard rounds to nearest (0.110 → 3DCC CCCD). No paper has asked for it.
Typos in Chapter 1: do not copy them
  • p. 9: “0.402510 = 0.011012”: read 0.4062510 = 0.011012.
  • p. 12, step 5: “0 – 1 = 1 → borrow 1”: the “= 1” should not be there.
  • The French version also writes “0.C816” with a point (p. 9) and calls the division 11111001 / 110 a “multiplication” (p. 13).
