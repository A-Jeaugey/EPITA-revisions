Part 1

Millman’s theorem

Millman’s theorem is Kirchhoff’s current law written with potentials. It gives you directly the potential of a node A where several branches arrive, all the potentials being measured relative to one common reference M (the ground, 0 V). The course proves it by writing the current of each branch (Vk − VA + Ek)/Rk, then Kirchhoff’s current law at A.

VA = Σ (Vk ± Ek)/Rk + Σ Ientering − Σ IleavingΣ 1/Rk
top: what each branch “pushes” toward A · bottom: the sum of the conductances
  1. Choose the ground: the point common to all the sources, often the bottom wire.
  2. For each branch that reaches A through a resistance Rk: in the numerator, the potential of the other end (plus the emf of the branch, + if its arrow points toward A, − otherwise), divided by Rk; in the denominator, 1/Rk.
  3. Current sources that reach A: in the numerator only (+ if they enter, − if they leave). A current source in series in a branch sets the current of that branch: the resistor in series with it does not count in the denominator.

The example from the course

Ground = bottom wire. Three branches reach A: (E₁, R₁), (E₃, R₃) and (I₂ // R₂). The source I₂ leaves A and E₃ has its arrow pointing down, hence the minus signs:

U = E₁/R₁ − E₃/R₃ − I₂1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

Multiplying top and bottom by R₁R₂R₃: U = R₂(E₁R₃ − E₃R₁ − I₂R₁R₃)/(R₂R₃ + R₁R₂ + R₁R₃). A single formula, where Thévenin or superposition would take three calculations.

In the end-of-semester exam

Millman is asked four times: as a bonus in January 2016, 2017 and 2019, and graded in January 2018 (Ex 4, 2 points). The January 2017 model answer also chooses it for exercise 3, and it is used everywhere to check a no-load voltage Eth in one line.

Key points
  • VA = (Σ “what each branch pushes”/Rk + entering currents) / Σ 1/Rk.
  • Sign + if the emf or the current goes toward A, − otherwise. A branch with a current source appears only in the numerator.

Millman: the special branches

Every branch that reaches node A brings something to the numerator and, if it has a resistor, to the denominator. The papers mix all kinds of branches: here is how to write each one, with the ground chosen at B.

The graded exercise: January 2018, Ex 4

Ground at B. I1 leaves A, I2 enters it; R2, in series with I2, does not count; the bottom of R3 is at potential −E3 (arrow pointing down), and R4, in parallel with E3, does not count:

U = E1/R1 − I1 + I2 − E3/R31/R1 + 1/R3
Key points
  • In the denominator, only the resistors of the branches without a current source.
  • The sign of each term: + if the emf or the current goes toward A.
  • Simplify by multiplying top and bottom by the product of the resistors.

Part 3

The superposition theorem

The course cites the superposition theorem: in a linear circuit with several sources, a voltage (or a current) is the sum of the contributions of each source acting alone, the others being turned off (voltage → wire, current → gap). It came up once in a midterm (2017, 2.5 points); in the end-of-semester exam, four times (January 2020, January 2021, June 2018, and in the 2018 and 2019 model answers). It is also an excellent way to check a result.

Example, 2024 Q2: with E alone (I open), you get E/6; with I alone (E as a wire), I leaves A and flows through 2R // 4R = 4R/3, hence −4RI/3. Total: (E − 8RI)/6, the same result as with the transformations.

The bench below takes the circuit of the 2017 midterm (Ex 4) with numerical values. Change E₁ and I₃: each single-source diagram gives its share of I₁, and the sum of the two shares always lands on the current of the full circuit. The arrow I₁ stays the same in all three diagrams: with I₃ alone, the red band shows the current flowing through R₁ against this arrow, hence the minus sign.

Key points
  • One state per source: you keep it, you turn off the others (voltage → wire, current → gap).
  • You add the contributions with their signs, relative to the same arrow.
  • Conditions: linear circuit, independent sources.

Superposition in the exam: one state per source

January 2020 (Ex 2): four sources, a voltage U and a current I to find, with a numerical application. The method fits in one table: one state per source, and in each state, what is left of the circuit.

E1 = −5 V, E2 = 3 V, I1 = 1 A, I2 = 2 A, R1 = 2 Ω, R2 = 3 Ω.

  1. Number the states and draw each single-source circuit: the model answers do it, and the grader marks the diagrams.
  2. Spot the short circuits and the cuts: a voltage source turned off short-circuits whatever is in parallel with it; a current source turned off cuts its branch.
  3. Calculate each contribution with a divider, Ohm’s law or KVL, using the arrow of the paper.
  4. Add with the signs, then do the numerical application (careful with negative sources: −E1 = +5 V).

Checking a result before handing in

Without a calculator and with literal expressions, a sign error or a forgotten resistor slips through easily. Three quick checks catch almost everything.

CheckHowExample
HomogeneityYou only add voltages to voltages (E, RI) or currents to currents (I, E/R)(E + RI)/5: correct; E + I: impossible
Limiting caseCancel a source (I = 0 or E = 0) and compare with the simplified circuitJanuary 2023: I = 0 gives I′ = E/(21R); with E alone, the divider confirms it
Another methodMillman for a no-load voltage, superposition for a literal resultJanuary 2020, Ex 4: Millman gives Eth = (3E1 + E2 + 3RI3)/5 again in one line
Key points
  • One minute of checking per question is worth more than one extra line.
  • A voltage or a current can be negative: the sign only tells the real direction relative to the arrow.

Which method to choose

The “Theorems” papers leave the choice of method open (“using the method of your choice”). The right choice depends on what you are looking for.

SituationFastest method
Voltage across a single resistor (the load) in a circuit with several sourcesThévenin seen by that resistor, then voltage divider
“Find Eth and Rth so that the two circuits are equivalent”Chained Thévenin ⇄ Norton transformations, backed by diagrams
Potential of a node where several branches with sources arriveMillman
A single source, resistorsEquivalent resistance, then dividers
All the voltages and all the currents of a small circuit, numerical valuesKirchhoff’s laws and Ohm’s law
Check a symbolic resultSuperposition or Millman, plus a limiting case (I = 0 or E = 0)
The traps found in the answer keys (2023 and 2024 midterms)
  • Including the 2R load in Eth or Rth.
  • Short-circuiting a current source to calculate Rth.
  • Reading a source arrow backward (2E with its arrow pointing down in 2024) or forgetting that a current source draws current from node A.
  • Forgetting the resistor R on the bottom wire (2024 Q2: Rth = 4R, not 3R).
  • Thinking that the bottom node of the left part is B when an “I // R” block separates them.
  • 6R // 6R = 3R, not 12R.
What the end-of-semester model answers choose

Thévenin/Norton equivalences for 2018 (Ex 3), 2021, 2022, 2023; superposition for 2019 (Ex 3) and 2020 (Ex 2, Ex 4); Millman for 2017 (Ex 3) and all the bonuses. The first two methods check each other.
