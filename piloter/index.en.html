Before the MCQ

What the announcement says, and how to answer

The Moodle announcement (22 October)

“You will have an MCQ of 40 questions. […] Take the time to read each situation carefully. The aim is to see whether you have understood, whether the tools covered are part of how you work, and whether you can recommend them in a given context. There are no trick questions, and no negative marking for a wrong answer.”

How to answer
  • Read the whole situation: the detail that matters is often the context (a deadline coming up, someone who does not speak, a decision that was never written down).
  • Name the tool that answers the problem: you are asked to recommend it, not just to define it.
  • The right answer is usually the most constructive one: it acts early, involves the group and makes things concrete. Imposing, ignoring or doing everything alone is rarely right.
  • No negative marking: do not leave any question unanswered.

Working methodology

Aim of the module: understand how you learn, build your own method, adapt your tools to your goals and take an active part in your studies, relying on internal levers rather than external ones.

1Workshop 1

Generation Z and the demands of higher education

Generation Z’s relationship to work

Born roughly between 1995 and 2010, after the millennials and before Generation Alpha.

  • Time: fragmented, focused on immediacy, overloaded.
  • Structure: collaborative, fair, supportive.
  • Effort: conditional, driven by meaning and quick feedback.
Self-assessment: how do I work?
  • Starting early on work that is due in several weeks.
  • Knowing what I have to do in the coming days.
  • Working even when I do not feel like it.
  • Staying focused without looking at my phone.
  • Estimating how long a piece of work will take.
  • Looking for help quickly when I hit a difficulty.
  • Working regularly rather than in big bursts.
  • Knowing my ideal working conditions.
Key points

Higher education expects you to be autonomous: nobody sets every step for you any more. The summary of the workshop, “my current user manual”, fits in three lines: one strength, one point to watch, and one concrete action to test this week (writing down your deadlines, starting earlier, 30 minutes without your phone…).

Learning how to learn: memory

The forgetting curve · Ebbinghaus, 1885

Most information is lost very quickly if it is not reviewed, and forgetting continues over time.

Remedy: recall the information at increasingly spaced intervals (for example 10 minutes, 1 day, 1 week, 1 month). Each recall brings retention back up and makes it fall more slowly.

The VAK profile · Neil Fleming’s VARK model, 1978
  • Visual: likes to see; uses mind maps and diagrams.
  • Auditory: likes to listen; retains information better in lectures and when speaking aloud.
  • Kinesthetic: likes to do; learns through practice and hands-on activities.

It is a starting point for reflection, not a rigid classification: you use it to adapt your tools.

The five tips from the course material for memorizing well
TipIn practice
VisualizeVary your learning materials (text, image, diagram, video); picture in your head what you are learning.
Create cuesMnemonics; associate a piece of information with a drawing or an anecdote from class.
Make connectionsRevision sheets; speaking aloud, rephrasing in your own words; linking to what you already know and to your own experience.
Test yourselfAsk yourself questions, answer quizzes, redo exercises you have already done.
ReactivateWork on it several times, first at close intervals, then more and more spaced out.
The mind map

A visual way of organizing ideas, for an overview and the links between concepts.

  1. The main topic in the centre.
  2. Branches for the main ideas.
  3. Sub-branches for the details.
  4. Keywords, colours, symbols.
Flashcards

A question or keyword on one side, the answer or definition on the other. You review them regularly, against the forgetting curve: it is testing yourself and reactivating at the same time.

The “NEXA protocol” challenge showed it: you have to remember the steps, their order, the conditions and the exceptions, not only the numbers.

Rereading is not learning

Rereading your notes the night before gives an impression of mastery without anchoring anything. What works: testing yourself (quizzes, flashcards, redoing exercises), and coming back to the topic several times, more and more spaced out. Attention matters too: without concentration, there is no memorization.

Giving meaning to learning

To stay engaged over the long term, you link your efforts to your personal and professional goals, and you identify what motivates and what demotivates you. The tool of the workshop is a hopscotch game, square by square, in order.

  1. 7My commitment: the first action, starting this week.
  2. 5My solutions to overcome the obstacles.
  3. 6My support: who or what can help me.
  4. 4My obstacles: what could hold me back.
  5. 2My motivation: why I want to succeed.
  6. 3My strengths: qualities and resources.
  7. 1My main learning goal this year.
Key points

The order makes sense: a goal, then why, then with what (strengths) and against what (obstacles), then how (solutions, support), and finally a concrete, immediate action. Motivation that comes from yourself (your goals, your project) lasts longer than the pressure of a grade alone.

Managing your balance

The balance triangle

Sleep, nutrition, physical activity: each rated out of 10 for the last two weeks. You spot your strongest point and your most fragile one, then compare with your energy level.

My energy level

Energy reads like a battery: from empty to full, and it recharges. The three sides of the triangle are what recharges it.

Key points
  • Balance personal, academic and professional life: success does not come from work without breaks.
  • Reduce stress by anticipating (starting early, planning for deadlines) and by prioritizing (workshop 5).
  • An all-nighter before an exam costs more than it gives: sleep also helps consolidate what you have learned.

Managing time and organization

Summary based on the English course material of workshop 5 and the “weekly planner” sheet; the French material will be on Moodle on 24 October.

The Eisenhower matrix · popularized by Covey (1989)

Sort your tasks into four categories according to two questions: is it urgent? is it important?

Urgent and importantDo it right away.Thursday’s test, the administrative message due tonight.
Important, not urgentPlan it: book a time slot.The project due in 3 weeks, the chapter you did not understand.
Urgent, not importantDelegate it or deal with it quickly, without spending time on it.The week’s shopping.
Neither urgent nor importantLater, or not at all.The WhatsApp group messages, organizing the movie night.

Examples: the tasks of the exercise in the course material, sorted by the site (the material gives no answer key). The trap: never dealing with the “important, not urgent” quadrant, which ends up becoming urgent.

The Pomodoro technique

25 minutes of focused work, then a 5-minute break. After 4 pomodoros, a long break of 25 to 30 minutes.

Useful against procrastination and distractions: your phone stays out of reach during the 25 minutes.

Time blocking

Each task is placed in your calendar like an appointment, breaks and free time included. You place the fixed constraints first (classes, commuting), then precise slots for revision and important tasks.

What to check: realistic slots, breaks, and time for the important before it becomes urgent.

Digital tools and summary

Moodle, a digital calendar, MyStudyLife, Notion, Google Calendar. The summary of the workshop: my main time thief; the tool that helps me prioritize when I have too much to do; what I do to protect my concentration time; the important slot I have really booked this week.

Aim of the module: understand how a group works, fit into a project group, communicate, prevent and manage tensions, contribute to decisions and step back to reflect on your own behaviour. The common thread of the workshops: a team developing a video game.

1Workshop 1

Understanding the group and its dynamics

A group is not a sum of individuals: it has its life stages, its rules, its formal roles (assigned: project manager, technical lead) and its informal roles (taken on spontaneously: the one who gets things moving, the one who lightens the mood).

Tuckman’s five stages
  1. FORMINGSetting upPolite exchanges, everyone is finding their place, disagreements are rarely expressed.Discovery, politeness, dependence
  2. STORMINGTurbulenceDifferences appear, choices are challenged, tensions rise.Conflicts, testing, resistance
  3. NORMINGSetting normsWays of working emerge, responsibilities become clearer, disagreements can be managed.Rules, cohesion, trust
  4. PERFORMINGHigh performanceMore autonomy, smoother decisions, energy goes into getting the work done.Synergy, autonomy, excellence
  5. ADJOURNINGWrapping upThe shared objective is reached or disappears: wrap up, hand over, disband.Review, recognition, transition
Role-play: 15 days of work, 10 days before the demo

Working main menu, playable but buggy level 1, unfinished level 2, partly integrated graphics, no sound, broken save system, incomplete tutorial, unstable multiplayer, and an almost finished bonus feature that the team loves. Three decisions to make and justify.

Common-sense leads: prioritize what makes the demo playable and stable (fix level 1, the save system, the tutorial); take out of scope what will not fit (unstable multiplayer, even the bonus, although people like it); share out clearly who does what, with a deadline.

Positioning oneself within a group

Personalities influence the collective: everyone contributes in their own way. Meredith Belbin’s team roles model distinguishes nine of them. Each strength has its “allowable weakness”.

RoleWhat it bringsAllowable weakness
PlantOriginal ideas, new solutions, solves difficult problems.Ignores incidentals, too preoccupied to communicate effectively.
Monitor EvaluatorSteps back, considers all the options, judges objectively.Lacks drive, can be too critical.
Co-ordinatorClarifies objectives, identifies talents, shares out and delegates.Can seem manipulative, offloads their own share of the work.
Resource InvestigatorLooks outside the group for ideas, contacts and opportunities.Over-optimistic, loses interest once the initial enthusiasm has passed.
ImplementerTurns ideas into concrete actions, organizes the work.Somewhat inflexible, slow to respond to new possibilities.
Completer FinisherChecks the details, looks for errors, makes sure the work is really finished.Worries too much, reluctant to delegate.
TeamworkerListens, encourages cooperation, avoids friction.Indecisive in crunch situations, avoids confrontation.
ShaperPushes the group forward, enjoys challenges and pressure.Prone to provocation, can offend people.
SpecialistA sharp skill that the group needs.Contributes only on a narrow front, dwells on technicalities.
Key points

An effective team needs varied roles: ten Plants with no Implementer or Completer Finisher deliver nothing. The summary of the workshop: “I tend to contribute by…”, “one thing I need to watch out for is…”, “to collaborate more effectively, I can…”.

Communicating effectively in a group

Active listening

A communication technique that uses questioning and paraphrasing to make sure you have understood the other person’s message as accurately as possible, and to show them that you have.

“If I understand correctly, you are suggesting…?” before answering or challenging.

The DESC method
  1. Describe the facts, objectively.
  2. Express how you feel.
  3. Specify a request and possible solutions.
  4. Conclude: the positive consequences if the request is taken into account.

“You changed the engine without telling anyone (D). I had to redo my integration and it annoyed me (E). Could you announce your changes on the project channel before pushing them (S)? We will avoid working twice (C).”

Role-play: listening to each other to move forward

Long meetings with no clear decision, two people taking up most of the speaking time, decisions questioned again the next time, a competent person who speaks very little, changes started without telling anyone, minutes that do not say who is responsible for what.

Possible communication rules: an agenda and a time limit; speaking turns, and you invite those who speak little to contribute; you paraphrase before challenging; every decision is written down, with who does what and by when; a decision is reopened only with new information; you inform the group before changing a shared part.

Managing tensions within the group

What’s really going on?

Behind the personal criticisms (“lack of organization”, “lack of involvement”), the workshop case hides concrete causes, to be dealt with one by one:

  • changes made without informing the group, forcing others to redo their work;
  • a scope too ambitious for the time available;
  • no shared definition of “finished”: tasks that have been “almost finished” for weeks;
  • scattered decisions (in meetings, on Discord) that are never formalized;
  • communication getting worse: abrupt messages, criticism.
The Thomas-Kilmann model: five ways of handling conflict
Concern for your own goals →
CompetingQuick, decisive action is needed; an important issue that is unpopular.
CollaboratingThe concerns of both parties are too important for a compromise; a major long-term issue; everyone’s commitment is needed.
CompromisingModerately important goals; two equally powerful parties with incompatible goals; a quick solution.
AvoidingAn unimportant issue; no chance of getting what you want; confrontation would cost more than it gains; a better time or place exists.
AccommodatingThe issue matters much more to the other party; limiting the damage; granting a favour; when you are in the wrong.
Concern for the goals of others →
Case study: keep or cut multiplayer?

Three weeks before the demo, the group is split in two. “Getting your solution adopted” is competing. “Avoiding making a decision right now” is avoiding. “Building a solution that takes into account the essential needs of both sides” is collaborating: for example, secure the single-player version first, then a reduced multiplayer if time allows. With a deadline this close, you have to decide at the very least.

Working effectively in a project group

The material for this workshop will be on Moodle on 24 October; the site will add to it. In the meantime, here is what the first four workshops have already established for project work.

Clear roles

Who does what, who decides, who checks. Formal roles are written down in the group’s document (the “CDSF focus” of each workshop).

Shared definitions

What “finished”, “urgent” and “approved” mean. Without a shared definition, everyone thinks they are done.

Decisions made and written down

A decision that has been made is noted, with who does what and by when, in the same place for everyone.

Key points

Prioritize together (the scope fits in the time available), inform before changing a shared part, hold regular, short check-ins, and settle tensions early, on the facts (DESC), before they become personal.
