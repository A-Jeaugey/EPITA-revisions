Part 1 course § III.1, 2

Variables, operators and priorities

Boolean algebra deals with expressions made of logic variables (which are 0 or 1) and operators. It is used to describe a combinational circuit, then to simplify it: a shorter expression means a circuit with fewer gates.

Its three fundamental operators are NOT (bar), AND (dot) and OR (+). The other gates are written with them: ~(A.B) for NAND, ~A.B + A.~B for XOR.

Operator priority

Like × before + in arithmetic: NOT first, then AND, then OR. The course writes it A + (B.C) = A + B.C: parentheses around a product are useless. Around a sum, they are essential: (A + B).C is not A + B.C.

A bar is a parenthesis. Everything under a bar is computed first, then complemented: ~(A + B).C is read “(not (A or B)) and C”. When you remove a long bar (De Morgan, part 4), put parentheses back around what it covered.

Pitfall: the forgotten dot

The exams always write the AND dot: A.B.C. Write it in your answers too; AB can be read as a single variable. The marker expects the format of the exam.

The axioms of NOT, AND and OR

These equalities can be checked on a two-row truth table (A = 0, A = 1). They are the toolbox of every simplification.

What is used most

X.~X = 0: a product that contains a variable and its complement disappears. X + ~X = 1: a sum that contains them is 1.

And what surprises

A + A = A and A + 1 = 1: there is no “2A” and no carry in Boolean algebra.

Pitfall: X can be an expression

The axioms hold for any expression in place of A: A.B.~(A.B) = 0, C.D + C.D = C.D, (A + B) + 1 = 1. This is what lets you simplify long expressions in a few lines.

Part 3 course § III.2

Distributivity, in both directions

AND distributes over OR, like × over +: A.(B + C) = A.B + A.C. You use it to expand a product of sums, or to factor (A.~B.C + A.~B.~C = A.~B.(C + ~C) = A.~B).

The surprise: in Boolean algebra, OR also distributes over AND. A + B.C = (A + B).(A + C). This equality has no equivalent in ordinary arithmetic, and it is the one used to prove theorem 2 (part 5).

Expanding a product of sums (example X1 of the course): you multiply each term of the first sum by each term of the second, then remove the null products.

(A + B + C.D).(~A + B)
A.~A + A.B + ~A.B + B.B + ~A.C.D + B.C.Dexpansion
A.B + ~A.B + B + ~A.C.D + B.C.DA.~A = 0, B.B = B
B + ~A.C.Dtheorem 1: B absorbs A.B, ~A.B and B.C.D
Pitfall: expanding too early

Expanding three sums of three terms gives 27 products. First look for what simplifies before expanding: a common factor, a bar to break, an absorption.

Part 4 course § III.2

De Morgan’s theorems

To remove a bar over a product or over a sum:

Bar over a product

~(A.B) = ~A + ~B

Bar over a sum

~(A + B) = ~A.~B

The rule: “break the bar, change the sign”

You cut the bar above the operator, each piece stays barred, and the operator changes: AND becomes OR, OR becomes AND. This works with as many variables as needed: ~(A.B.C) = ~A + ~B + ~C et ~(A + B + C) = ~A.~B.~C.

Nested bars. Break the highest bar first, keeping the parentheses, then start again. Example from the 2023-2024 Exam 2:

~(~C.B)
~~C + ~BDe Morgan
C + ~Bdouble complementation
~(A.B + A.C)
~(A.B).~(A.C)De Morgan on the sum
(~A + ~B).(~A + ~C)De Morgan on each product
Pitfall: forgetting the parentheses

~(A + B.C) = ~A.~(B.C) = ~A.(~B + ~C). Without the parentheses, you would write ~A.~B + ~C, a different function. A bar broken over a product gives a sum: put it in parentheses if it is multiplied by something else.

Part 5 course § III.2

Absorption: theorems 1 and 2

Theorem 1

A + A.B = A

A term absorbs every product that contains it.

Theorem 2

A + ~A.B = A + B

A term erases its complement in a neighbouring product.

Both theorems apply to whole expressions, not only to variables. In the examples from the course:

  • theorem 1: ~A + ~A.~C = ~A, ~A + ~A.~B.C = ~A, ~A.~B.C.D + ~A.C.D = ~A.C.D (example X1): the absorbing term must be entirely contained in the other;
  • theorem 2: B + ~B.A.C = B + A.C (example X2), A.B.~(C.D) + C.D = A.B + C.D (example X3, with X = C.D).
Pitfall: absorbing the wrong way

A + A.B = A, but A.B + A.~B.C does not simplify to A.B: the term A.B is not contained in A.~B.C. Here theorem 2 applies, once A is factored out: A.(B + ~B.C) = A.(B + C).

Part 6 course § III.3

The principle of duality

A Boolean identity remains true if you swap the 0s and 1s everywhere, as well as the ANDs and ORs. The resulting equality is called the dual equality. The variables and the bars do not move.

Examples from the course (§ III.3)
EqualityDual
1 + 0 = 1↔0.1 = 0
X + 1 = 1↔X.0 = 0
X.~X = 0↔X + ~X = 1
X + ~X.Y = X + Y↔X.(~X + Y) = X.Y

Duality doubles the toolbox: each AND axiom has its OR dual (the table in part 2 is made of dual pairs), each distributivity has its dual, and the two De Morgan theorems are duals of each other. The duals of theorems 1 and 2 are used in products of sums:

Dual of theorem 1

A.(A + B) = A

Dual of theorem 2

A.(~A + B) = A.B

Pitfall: the hidden priority

The dual of A + B.C is A.(B + C), with parentheses: the product had priority, and the sum that replaces it must keep it. Put the parentheses back before swapping the operators.

Part 7 course § III.2

The exclusive OR in calculations

Properties of the exclusive OR (course § III.2)
A ^ B = ~A.B + A.~B = ~A ^ ~B
~(A ^ B) = A.B + ~A.~B = ~A ^ B = A ^ ~B
A ^ (B ^ C) = (A ^ B) ^ C = A ^ B ^ C et A ^ B = B ^ A
A ^ 0 = A, A ^ 1 = ~A, A ^ A = 0, A ^ ~A = 1

Recognising an exclusive OR. Two products identical except for two variables, one taken directly in the first and complemented in the second, and the other the opposite: it is an exclusive OR.

  • X.~Y + ~X.Y = X ^ Y (the two variables “different”);
  • X.Y + ~X.~Y = ~(X ^ Y) (the two variables “equal”).

X and Y can be products: this is how the official solutions get their forms with ⊕.

In the exam

“Simplify using the EXCLUSIVE-OR operator” comes back almost every year: after a Karnaugh map (2025-2026: S1 = B ^ (C.D)), or on a given expression (2016-2017, 2020-2021, 2021-2022). Method: first the simplest form without XOR, then spot the pairs of “different” or “equal” products.

Pitfall: simplifying with ⊕ when it is forbidden

Several exams specify “do not use simplification with EXCLUSIVE-OR” for the Karnaugh question. Read the instructions: the form with ⊕ earns points only when it is asked for.

Part 8 course § III.4

Simplifying an expression step by step

A method that works on all the examples from the course and the exams:

  1. Break the long bars (De Morgan), from the highest to the lowest, keeping parentheses.

  2. Expand the products of sums (distributivity).

  3. Clean up: X.~X = 0, X.X = X, X + X = X, X + 1 = 1.

  4. Absorb: theorem 1 (X + X.Y = X), theorem 2 (X + ~X.Y = X + Y), and factor (X.Y + X.~Y = X).

  5. Check on a few rows of the truth table, or with a Karnaugh map (chapter 4).

Everything with NAND gates

The NAND gate is universal: with NAND gates only, you can build a NOT, an AND, an OR, and therefore any circuit. Everything comes from two equalities: ~(A.A) = ~A (idempotence) and De Morgan.

In the exam

2025-2026 exam, exercise 5 (3 points): “Give the wiring diagram of the AND, OR and EXCLUSIVE-OR gates, using only NAND gates. The maximum number of gates is given”: 2, 3 and 5. Tutorial 1 (exercise 2) also asks for the NOT. A NAND gate with its two inputs tied together is an inverter: it is the basic brick of the three schematics.

Pitfall: the number of gates

The exam imposes a maximum. An XOR drawn with one NAND for each NOT, AND and OR of ~A.B + A.~B takes 9 gates: you must use the equality from the table to get down to 5.
