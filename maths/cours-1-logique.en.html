Propositions, negation, “and”, “or”

Mathematics is written with sentences that are either true or false. They are combined with logical connectors: not, and, or, then (parts 3 and 5) implies and is equivalent to.

Definition proposition

A proposition (or assertion) is a mathematical sentence that is either true or false, never both: this is the law of excluded middle. Propositions are written \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\)… A proposition may depend on a variable: \(P(x)\): “\(x \leq 1\)” is true for \(x = 0\) and false for \(x = 3\).

Definition negation, conjunction, disjunction
  • The negation \(\neg P\) (“not \(P\)”) is true when \(P\) is false, and false when \(P\) is true. So \(\neg(\neg P) \Leftrightarrow P\).
  • The conjunction \(P \land Q\) (“\(P\) and \(Q\)”) is true only when \(P\) and \(Q\) are both true.
  • The disjunction \(P \lor Q\) (“\(P\) or \(Q\)”) is true as soon as at least one of them is true.
Table 1.1 — Truth table of the first three connectors (T: true, F: false). With two propositions there are 4 rows: one for each combination.
\(P\)\(Q\)\(\neg P\)\(P \land Q\)\(P \lor Q\)
TTFTT
TFFFT
FTTFT
FFTFF

Example (exercise 1.1). For a real number \(x\), let \(P(x)\): “\(x \leq 1\)”, \(Q(x)\): “\(-1 < x \leq 2\)” and \(R(x)\): “\(0 \leq x < 4\)”. Take \(x = -3\). First evaluate each building block, then apply the table:

Table 1.2 — Exercise 1.1, question 1, for \(x = -3\)
PropositionValueWhy
\(P(-3)\)true\(-3 \leq 1\)
\(\neg Q(-3)\)true\(Q(-3)\) is false, because \(-3 \leq -1\)
\(\neg Q(-3) \lor R(-3)\)true\(\neg Q(-3)\) is true: one of the two is enough
\(\neg Q(-3) \land R(-3)\)false\(R(-3)\) is false, because \(-3 < 0\)
\(\neg P(-3) \land Q(-3)\)false\(\neg P(-3)\) is false

Model answer

For \(x = -3\): \(-3 < 0\), so \(R(-3)\) is false. A conjunction is true only if both its terms are true: \(\neg Q(-3) \land R(-3)\) is therefore false.quote the rule

On the other hand \(-3 \leq -1\), so \(Q(-3)\) is false and \(\neg Q(-3)\) is true. A disjunction is true as soon as one of its terms is true: \(\neg Q(-3) \lor R(-3)\) is true.

Trap: the mathematical “or” is inclusive

\(P \lor Q\) is also true when \(P\) and \(Q\) are both true (first row of the table). On a menu, “cheese or dessert” means one or the other, not both: that is the “exclusive or”, seen in part 5. And we speak of the “conjunction” of two propositions, not their “intersection”: intersection is for sets (lesson 2).

Laws of logic and De Morgan’s laws

Connectors follow rules, just like numbers do: some different-looking formulas say exactly the same thing. To prove it, compare their truth tables.

Definition equivalent propositions

Two propositions are equivalent when they have the same truth value on every row of their table. We write \(A \Leftrightarrow B\) (or \(A \equiv B\)).

Theorem laws of logic
  • Commutativity: \(P \land Q \Leftrightarrow Q \land P\) and \(P \lor Q \Leftrightarrow Q \lor P\).
  • Associativity: \((P \land Q) \land R \Leftrightarrow P \land (Q \land R)\), written \(P \land Q \land R\); the same holds for \(\lor\).
  • Distributivity: \(P \land (Q \lor R) \Leftrightarrow (P \land Q) \lor (P \land R)\) and \(P \lor (Q \land R) \Leftrightarrow (P \lor Q) \land (P \lor R)\).
  • De Morgan’s laws: \(\neg(P \land Q) \Leftrightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q\) and \(\neg(P \lor Q) \Leftrightarrow \neg P \land \neg Q\).

De Morgan in practice. Negating an “and” gives an “or”, negating an “or” gives an “and”. The negation of “\(x > 0\) and \(x < 1\)” is “\(x \leq 0\) or \(x \geq 1\)”.

Example (exercise 1.1, question 3): sets of values. With the propositions of part 1, find for which \(x\) each proposition is true, as an interval. \(Q(x) \land R(x)\): \(0 \leq x \leq 2\). So \(P(x) \lor \big(Q(x) \land R(x)\big)\) is true for \(x \in \,]-\infty, 1] \cup [0, 2] = \,]-\infty, 2]\).

On the other side, \(P(x) \lor Q(x)\) gives \(]-\infty, 2]\) and \(P(x) \lor R(x)\) gives \(]-\infty, 4[\). Their conjunction: \(]-\infty, 2]\). Same set: this is the distributivity of \(\lor\) over \(\land\).

Model answer

Let us prove that \(\neg(P \land Q) \Leftrightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q\). We draw the truth table of both propositions (4 rows, one for each value of the pair \((P, Q)\)).

The columns of \(\neg(P \land Q)\) and of \(\neg P \lor \neg Q\) are both F, T, T, T: they are identical row by row.the concluding sentence counts

Hence \(\neg(P \land Q) \Leftrightarrow \neg P \lor \neg Q\).

Trap: negating an “and” and keeping the “and”

\(\neg(P \land Q)\) is not \(\neg P \land \neg Q\): click the third example above, the columns differ on rows 2 and 3. For “\(0 < x < 1\)”, writing “\(x \leq 0\) and \(x \geq 1\)” gives a proposition that is always false: the right negation is “\(x \leq 0\) or \(x \geq 1\)”.

Implication

Definition implication

\(P \Rightarrow Q\) reads “\(P\) implies \(Q\)” or “if \(P\), then \(Q\)”. It is false in one case only: \(P\) true and \(Q\) false. In the three other cases it is true.

Table 1.3 — Truth table of the implication. The last two rows are surprising: if \(P\) is false, \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is true.
\(P\)\(Q\)\(P \Rightarrow Q\)
TTT
TFF
FTT
FFT

An implication is a promise: “if you score more than 10, you pass”. It is broken only if you score more than 10 and do not pass. If you score less than 10, the promise said nothing: it still holds.

Theorem to know
  • \(P \Rightarrow Q \;\Leftrightarrow\; \neg P \lor Q\) (same table: check it below).
  • Transitivity: if \(P \Rightarrow Q\) and \(Q \Rightarrow R\), then \(P \Rightarrow R\).
Definition converse

The converse of \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is \(Q \Rightarrow P\). Implication is not commutative: a true implication may have a false converse.

Examples (seminar notes). “\(n\) divisible by 4 \(\Rightarrow\) \(n\) even” is true; its converse “\(n\) even \(\Rightarrow\) \(n\) divisible by 4” is false: \(n = 2\). Likewise, “\(x > 2 \Rightarrow x^2 > 4\)” is true, but its converse “\(x^2 > 4 \Rightarrow x > 2\)” is false.

Model answer a false implication

Let us show that “\(\forall x \in \R,\ x^2 > 4 \Rightarrow x > 2\)” is false. One counterexample is enough: an \(x\) that makes \(P\) true and \(Q\) false.

Take \(x = -3\): \(x^2 = 9 > 4\), but \(x > 2\) is false.a specific number

The implication is therefore false for \(x = -3\): the property is false.

Trap: an implication does not say that P is true
  • “If \(P\), then \(Q\)” says nothing when \(P\) is false. “If \(1 = 2\), then I am the Pope” is a true implication.
  • Do not mix up an implication and its converse: to disprove \(P \Rightarrow Q\), you need \(P\) true and \(Q\) false, not the other way round.

Necessary condition, sufficient condition

Definition necessary, sufficient condition

When \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is true:

  • \(P\) is a sufficient condition for \(Q\): it is enough for \(P\) to be true for \(Q\) to be true;
  • \(Q\) is a necessary condition for \(P\): for \(P\) to be true, \(Q\) has to be true.

If \(P \Leftrightarrow Q\), \(P\) is a necessary and sufficient condition for \(Q\).

Example (notes). “\(n\) divisible by 4” is a sufficient condition for \(n\) to be even. “\(n\) even” is a necessary condition for \(n\) to be divisible by 4, but not a sufficient one (\(n = 2\)).

Method placing \(\Rightarrow\), \(\Leftarrow\) or \(\Leftrightarrow\)
  1. Write the set of the \(x\) that make each proposition true (an interval, a list).
  2. If the left set is included in the right one, the arrow goes from left to right: \(\Rightarrow\). The other way: \(\Leftarrow\). Both: \(\Leftrightarrow\).
  3. For each arrow you reject, keep a counterexample: it is your justification.

(a) The sufficient conditions for \(P\) are (2), (4) and (5). For instance for (4): if \(x < -3\) or \(x > 3\), then \(|x| > 3\), so \(x^2 > 9 > 4\).

(b) The only necessary condition for \(P\) is (5): \(x^2 > 4 \Leftrightarrow |x| > 2\).the others: a counterexample

(c) Negation of \(P \Rightarrow (5)\): “\(x^2 > 4\) and \(-2 \leq x \leq 2\)”.

Trap: the direction of the arrow

The necessary condition is at the head of the arrow: in \(P \Rightarrow Q\), it is \(Q\). Remember: “for \(P\), you need \(Q\); \(P\) is enough for \(Q\)”. A necessary condition is often “wider” than \(P\): (1) and (3) are neither, because they neither contain \(P\) nor are contained in it.

Contrapositive, negation of an implication, equivalence

Definition contrapositive

The contrapositive of \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\). Negate both propositions and swap them.

Theorem

An implication and its contrapositive are equivalent: \((P \Rightarrow Q) \Leftrightarrow (\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P)\).

Proof you have to know asked in the OBM exam 2024, 1.3b

Draw the truth table of \(P \Rightarrow Q\) and of \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\):

\(P\)\(Q\)\(\neg Q\)\(\neg P\)\(P \Rightarrow Q\)\(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\)
TTFFTT
TFTFFF
FTFTTT
FFTTTT

\(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\) is false only if \(\neg Q\) is true and \(\neg P\) is false, that is \(Q\) false and \(P\) true: exactly the only case where \(P \Rightarrow Q\) is false (row 2). The last two columns are identical, hence \((P \Rightarrow Q) \Leftrightarrow (\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P)\).

Definition negation of an implication, equivalence
  • \(\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \Leftrightarrow P \land \neg Q\): the negation of an implication is an “and”, not an implication.
  • The equivalence \(P \Leftrightarrow Q\) (“\(P\) if and only if \(Q\)”) means \(P \Rightarrow Q\) and \(Q \Rightarrow P\). It is true when \(P\) and \(Q\) have the same truth value.
  • \(\neg(P \Leftrightarrow Q) \Leftrightarrow (P \land \neg Q) \lor (\neg P \land Q)\): exactly one of the two is true. This is the exclusive or.
Table 1.6 — The four sentences around “if \(x > 2\), then \(x^2 > 4\)” (for a real number \(x\))
NameSentenceTrue for every \(x\)?
implication\(x > 2 \Rightarrow x^2 > 4\)yes
contrapositive\(x^2 \leq 4 \Rightarrow x \leq 2\)yes (equivalent)
converse\(x^2 > 4 \Rightarrow x > 2\)no: \(x = -3\)
negation\(x > 2\) and \(x^2 \leq 4\)never true

Example (exercise 1.2, question 4). \(P(x)\): “\(x^2 > 1 \Rightarrow (x < -1\) or \(x > 1)\)”. Its negation is “\(x^2 > 1\) and \(-1 \leq x \leq 1\)”; its contrapositive is “\(-1 \leq x \leq 1 \Rightarrow x^2 \leq 1\)”.

Model answer in everyday language (ex. 1.5)

“If the weather is fine, then I go to the beach.”

Contrapositive: “If I do not go to the beach, then the weather is not fine.”swap

Negation: “The weather is fine and I do not go to the beach.”an “and”

“You can go out if and only if you do your homework” has the negation: “You can go out without doing your homework, or you do your homework and you cannot go out.”

Trap: three words, three meanings
  • The converse swaps without negating: \(Q \Rightarrow P\). It is not equivalent to the implication.
  • The contrapositive swaps and negates: \(\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P\). It is equivalent.
  • The negation is not an implication: “If the weather is fine, I do not go to the beach” is wrong as a negation.

The quantifiers \(\forall\) and \(\exists\)

Definition quantifiers
  • \(\forall x \in E,\ P(x)\): “for all \(x\) in \(E\), \(P(x)\) is true”. This is the universal quantifier.
  • \(\exists x \in E,\ P(x)\): “there exists at least one \(x\) in \(E\) such that \(P(x)\) is true”. This is the existential quantifier.
  • \(\exists !\, x \in E,\ P(x)\): there exists one and only one.

On a finite set, \(\forall\) is a big “and”, \(\exists\) a big “or”: for \(A = \{1, 2, 3\}\), \(\forall x \in A,\ P(x)\) means \(P(1) \land P(2) \land P(3)\).

The quantified letter is a dummy variable: \(\forall x \in \R,\ x^2 \geq 0\) and \(\forall t \in \R,\ t^2 \geq 0\) say the same thing. But the order of the quantifiers matters:

true
\(\forall x \in \R,\ \exists y \in \R,\ y > x\)

For each \(x\), choose \(y\) depending on \(x\): \(y = x + 1\).

false
\(\exists y \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ y > x\)

The same \(y\) would have to be greater than every real number, \(x = y\) included.

Method translating a sentence
  1. Spot the words “all”, “every” (\(\forall\)) and “there exists”, “some”, “at least one” (\(\exists\)); “no” is a \(\forall\) followed by a negation.
  2. Write the quantifiers in the order the choices are made: what is chosen first comes first.
  3. End with the proposition and its connectors (\(\Rightarrow\) for “if … then”, “only”).
Table 1.7 — Translations to know, for \(f : \R \to \R\) (seminar 1.3, 2021 and 2023 exams)
SentenceTranslation
\(f\) vanishes at least once\(\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) = 0\)
\(f\) never vanishes\(\forall x \in \R,\ f(x) \neq 0\)
\(f\) is a constant function\(\exists a \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ f(x) = a\)
\(f\) is upper bounded\(\exists M \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ f(x) \leq M\)
\(f\) is bounded\(\exists (m, M) \in \R^2,\ \forall x \in \R,\ m \leq f(x) \leq M\)
\(f\) is even / odd\(\forall x \in \R,\ f(-x) = f(x)\) / \(f(-x) = -f(x)\)
\(f\) is periodic\(\exists T \in \R^*,\ \forall x \in \R,\ f(x + T) = f(x)\)
\(f\) is not the zero function\(\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) \neq 0\)
\(f\) is positive on \(\R^+\)\(\forall x \in \R^+,\ f(x) \geq 0\), or \(\forall x \in \R,\ x \geq 0 \Rightarrow f(x) \geq 0\)

Example (exercise 1.4, the pond). \(M\): what is in the pond, \(F\): the fishes, \(R\): what is red. “All fishes are red”: \(\forall x \in M,\ x \in F \Rightarrow x \in R\). “Only the fishes are red”: \(\forall x \in M,\ x \in R \Rightarrow x \in F\). “There exists a fish which is not red”: \(\exists x \in F,\ x \notin R\).

Model answer LE exam 2023, exercise 2.1

Let \(f : \R \to \R\).

(a) “\(f\) vanishes at least once”: \(\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) = 0\).

(b) “\(f\) is constant”: \(\exists a \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ f(x) = a\).\(a\) first

(c) “\(f\) is upper bounded”: \(\exists M \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ f(x) \leq M\).

Trap: swapping \(\forall\) and \(\exists\)

“\(\forall x \in \R,\ \exists a \in \R,\ f(x) = a\)” is true for every function (take \(a = f(x)\)): it does not say “\(f\) is constant”. In a definition, the constant (\(a\), \(M\), \(T\)) is chosen before \(x\), so its \(\exists\) comes first. And “some” means “at least one”, never “exactly one”.

Negating a quantified statement

Theorem negation of quantifiers
\[\neg\big(\forall x \in E,\ P(x)\big) \Leftrightarrow \exists x \in E,\ \neg P(x) \qquad \neg\big(\exists x \in E,\ P(x)\big) \Leftrightarrow \forall x \in E,\ \neg P(x)\]

“It is not true for all” means “it is false for at least one”. “There is none” means “it is false for all”.

Method negating in three moves
  1. Turn each \(\forall\) into \(\exists\) and each \(\exists\) into \(\forall\), in the same order, keeping the same sets.
  2. Negate the final proposition with the rules of parts 2 and 5: an “and” becomes “or”, an “or” becomes “and”, \(P \Rightarrow Q\) becomes \(P \land \neg Q\).
  3. Negate the relations: \(=\) becomes \(\neq\), \(<\) becomes \(\geq\), \(\leq\) becomes \(>\), \(\in\) becomes \(\notin\).

Press “Next”: each step applies one rule, and the part that changes is highlighted. You can type your own statement, in TeX or with the symbols \(\forall\) \(\exists\) \(\in\) \(\Rightarrow\).

Model answer LE exam 2023, exercise 2.2a

\(P\): “\(\forall x \in \R,\ f(x) = 0\)”, \(Q\): “\(\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) = 0\)”, \(R\): “\((\forall x \in \R,\ f(x) > 0) \lor (\forall x \in \R,\ f(x) < 0)\)”.

\(\neg P\): “\(\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) \neq 0\)”.

\(\neg Q\): “\(\forall x \in \R,\ f(x) \neq 0\)”.

\(\neg R\): “\((\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) \leq 0) \land (\exists x \in \R,\ f(x) \geq 0)\)”.De Morgan, then \(\neg\forall\)

In the OBM exam 2024 (exercise 1.2b), with \(P\): “\(\forall x \in \R,\ \exists y \in \R,\ \cos(x) = y\)”, \(Q\): “\(\exists y \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ x = \cos(y)\)” and \(R\): “\(\exists x \in \R,\ \exists y \in \R,\ \cos(x) < \cos(y)\)”:

\(\neg P : \exists x \in \R,\ \forall y \in \R,\ \cos(x) \neq y\)
\(\neg Q : \forall y \in \R,\ \exists x \in \R,\ x \neq \cos(y)\)
\(\neg R : \forall x \in \R,\ \forall y \in \R,\ \cos(x) \geq \cos(y)\)
Trap: negating the domain, or the wrong relation
  • The domain of a quantifier is not negated: the negation of “\(\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \ldots\)” starts with “\(\exists \varepsilon > 0\)”, not with “\(\exists \varepsilon \leq 0\)”.
  • The negation of \(f(x) < 0\) is \(f(x) \geq 0\), not \(f(x) > 0\): the case \(f(x) = 0\) changes sides.
  • The order of the quantifiers does not change: you swap their type, not their place.

True or false: proof, witness, counterexample

“True or false? Justify.” is the most frequent question. The shape of the statement tells you what to write.

Table 1.8 — What to write depending on the statement (seminar, “To write a nice proof redaction”)
The statement is…To prove it
\(\forall x \in E,\ P(x)\), true“Let \(x \in E\) be given.” then a proof that works for this unspecified \(x\)
\(\forall x \in E,\ P(x)\), falsea counterexample: a specific \(x\) for which \(P(x)\) is false
\(\exists x \in E,\ P(x)\), truea witness: a specific \(x\), and a check of \(P(x)\)
\(\exists x \in E,\ P(x)\), falseprove its negation \(\forall x \in E,\ \neg P(x)\)
\(P \Rightarrow Q\)“Assume that \(P\) is true.” then prove \(Q\)
Definition counterexample

A counterexample to “\(\forall x \in E,\ P(x)\)” is an element \(x_0 \in E\) such that \(P(x_0)\) is false. A single one is enough to prove that the statement is false; no number of examples is enough to prove that it is true.

Model answer ex. 1.6

(1) “\(\forall x \in \R,\ \exists y \in \R,\ x + y > 0\)” is true. Let \(x \in \R\) be given. Take \(y = 1 - x\): then \(x + y = 1 > 0\).\(y\) depends on \(x\)

(2) “\(\forall x \in \R,\ \forall y \in \R,\ x + y > 0\)” is false: for \(x = y = 0\), \(x + y = 0\).

(3) “\(\exists y \in \R,\ \forall x \in \R,\ x + y > 0\)” is false. Let us prove its negation, \(\forall y \in \R,\ \exists x \in \R,\ x + y \leq 0\). Let \(y \in \R\) be given; with \(x = -y\), \(x + y = 0 \leq 0\).

(4) “\(\exists x \in \R,\ \exists y \in \R,\ x + y > 0\)” is true: \(x = y = 1\) gives \(x + y = 2 > 0\).

OBM exam 2024, 1.2a. \(P\) is true: for a given \(x\), \(y = \cos(x)\) works. \(Q\) is false: for every \(y\), \(\cos(y) \leq 1\), so \(x = 2\) is never equal to \(\cos(y)\). \(R\) is true: \(\cos(\pi) = -1 < 1 = \cos(0)\).

Negations in everyday language (ex. 1.5). “All truths are good to know” becomes “some truths are not good to know”. “Some fishes are red” becomes “no fish is red”. “No criminal hypothesis can be discarded” becomes “at least one criminal hypothesis can be discarded”.

Trap: an example does not prove a \(\forall\)
  • Checking \(P(1)\), \(P(2)\) and \(P(3)\) does not prove \(\forall x \in \R,\ P(x)\): you need “Let \(x \in \R\) be given”.
  • A counterexample is given with a specific number and checked: “\(x = -3\): \(x^2 = 9 > 4\) but \(-3 \leq 2\)”, not “a negative \(x\)”.
