Seminar learning outcomes

OBM

Logic

  • Recognize the logical connectors in a property.
  • Use the “for all” and “there exists” quantifiers.
  • Translate a property from everyday language to mathematical language, and combine such properties to make a new one.
  • Write the negation and the contrapositive of an implication, in everyday language as well as in mathematical language.

Sets

  • Use the set vocabulary properly, especially the difference between the membership and inclusion symbols.
  • Handle the operations on sets and subsets: inclusion, union, intersection, complement, partition, Cartesian product.
  • For finite sets, compute the cardinal of a set, of a union, of a Cartesian product and of a power set.

Functions

  • Prove that a function (defined by a figure or a formula) is injective, surjective or bijective.
  • Change the input or output set to make a function injective, surjective or bijective.
  • Find the image or the pre-image of a set under a function.
  • Handle the arctan function as the inverse of the tan function: its graph, some particular values.

Reasoning

  • Prove a simple property by a direct proof, by contrapositive, by contradiction or by induction.
  • Prove a set inclusion or a set equality.

Tutorial 1 learning outcomes

DP · counting

Enumerate and count finite sets which can be handled by combinations, arrangements and permutations. In detail:

  • Count the number of ways of picking elements from a finite set, with or without replacement, with or without repetitions.
  • Count the number of mappings from a finite set to another.
  • Adapt the typical methods to real-life situations.
  • Expand a binomial expression to a high whole power.
  • Prove Pascal’s formula, by a counting argument and by computation.
  • Prove the binomial formula by a counting argument.
  • Imagine simple situations associated with a given way of counting.

Tutorial 2 learning outcomes

DP · probability, random variables
  • Model a random experiment in the language of probability (probability space, distribution of a random variable).
  • Compute probabilities in equiprobable situations, given conditional probabilities or an independence hypothesis.
  • Interpret the expectation and variance of a finite random variable and of any affine transformation of it.

In detail

  • Model a random experiment with a suitable probability space: the outcomes, the events, the probability.
  • Compute probabilities in case of equiprobability.
  • Compute conditional probabilities, with a tree or not, in a finite sample space.
  • Compute probabilities with the law of total probability.
  • Interpret the independence of two events.
  • Find the distribution, the expectation and the variance of a finite random variable.

What the answer keys write · DP
  • One sentence per factor and the model named: “the order no longer matters”, “we go through the complement”.
  • Events defined by a letter and a sentence; “since \(\{S, \overline{S}\}\) is a partition of \(\Omega\)” before the law of total probability; the formula in letters, then the numbers, then the simplified fraction.
  • Random variables: \(X(\Omega)\), each probability, the name of the distribution; \(\E\) and \(\V\) in detail, “do not just drop the results”. Binomial: “sum of \(n\) independent Bernoulli variables with the same parameter”.

What the answer keys write · OBM
  • Definitions with their quantifiers and their sets: \(f^{-1}(B) = \{x \in E,\ f(x) \in B\}\).
  • A true or false is always justified: a proof, or a counterexample with numbers (“\(g(0) = g(2) = 1\) and \(0 \neq 2\)”).
  • A restriction comes with both sets and its reason (“strictly increasing on \([1, +\infty[\)”). A proof starts with the required line: “Let \(x \in A\).” for \(A \subset B\) (a seminar rule, required by the 2025 OBM exam).
