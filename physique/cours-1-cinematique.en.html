Part 1

The framework: system, reference frame, coordinates, time

Kinematics studies motion independently of its cause. It describes how a point moves, not why: forces come in chapter 2.

Before any calculation, you fix four things. Without them, “the velocity of the point” means nothing.

The system

What you study. You model it as a material point: a system whose spatial extension and rotation on itself can both be neglected.

It depends on the problem: the Earth moving around the Sun is a point; the Earth spinning on itself (length of the day) is not.

The reference frame

The space and time frame you observe from: terrestrial, geocentric, heliocentric. It is Galilean if it moves without any acceleration.

For a ball, a car, a pebble: terrestrial reference frame, assumed Galilean.

The coordinate system

An origin O and axes, to give the point coordinates. In Cartesian coordinates: (O, ux, uy).

Suitable for most cases of motion in a plane, except circular motion (polar coordinates, chapter 4).

The time origin

The instant chosen as t = 0: usually the start, the release or the launch.

The initial conditions are the position and the velocity at that instant.

Example TD 1 · Ex 1 A car starts moving. The origin of the frame is the entry door of the driver’s home, the axis follows the road, and t = 0 when the car starts. The car is parked 7 m from the door: its initial position is x(0) = 7 m, not 0.

Write-up: the first lines of an answer
  • System: the projectile, modelled as a material point M.
  • Reference frame: terrestrial, assumed Galilean.
  • Coordinate system: (O, ux, uy), O at the launch point, uy pointing up.
  • Time origin: t = 0 at the launch.

This is exactly question 1 of exercise 3 in TD 1: “describe the study framework (reference frame, system under study, coordinate system)”.

Trap

Motion depends on the reference frame. A passenger sitting in a train is at rest in the train’s reference frame and rushes along at 300 km/h in the terrestrial reference frame. “It is at rest” only makes sense once the reference frame is given.

Part 2

The position vector

In the Cartesian coordinate system, O is at the crossing of the axes. The vectors ux and uy are unit vectors and fixed; they form a right-handed orthonormal basis. Point M is defined by two numbers:

  • x, the projection of M on the axis (O, ux);
  • y, the projection of M on the axis (O, uy).
OM(t) = x(t) ux + y(t) uy = x(t)y(t)

The functions x(t) and y(t) are the equations of motion (the time equations; the English midterm of November 2021 calls them “hourly equations”). They tell you where the point is at each instant. The distance from O to M is the norm of the position vector:

OM = ‖OM‖ = √x² + y²

Example TD 1 · Ex 4 A pebble is thrown horizontally; the origin is its starting point, uy points up. Motion tracking gives x(t) = 0.5 t and y(t) = −5 t² (in metres). So OM(t) = 0.5 t ux − 5 t² uy. At t = 1 s, M is at (0.5; −5): 0.5 m to the right and 5 m below the starting point, at OM = √25.25 ≈ 5.0 m from O.

Remember
  • The position vector depends on the choice of origin O. Move O, and every coordinate changes.
  • A coordinate is a position, in metres, with a sign: y = −5 m means 5 m below the x-axis.
In the midterm

Midterms of November 2021 and October 2022: the paper gives OM(t) and the first question asks for “the equations of motion”. Answer with the two functions x(t) = … and y(t) = …: they are numbers, with no ux or uy.

Trap

Do not write OM = x + y: a vector is not a number. You need the basis vectors (x ux + y uy) or the column of coordinates.

Part 3

The velocity vector

The velocity vector is the first derivative of the position vector with respect to time. Its norm is measured in m·s⁻¹.

v(t) = dOMdt

In Cartesian coordinates, ux and uy are fixed: differentiating OM means differentiating each coordinate. A dot above a letter means “derivative with respect to time”: ẋ = dxdt. You also write vx = ẋ and vy = ẏ.

v = dxdt ux + dydt uy = ẋ ux + ẏ uy = ẋẏ‖v‖ = √ẋ² + ẏ²

Its direction. During an instant dt, M moves by the infinitesimal displacement dOM = dx ux + dy uy, a tiny piece of trajectory. v is collinear to this displacement: it is tangent to the trajectory, in the direction of motion.

Reading a chronophotograph

The exposures are taken at regular intervals Δt. Two exposures far apart: the point was moving fast. Two exposures close together: it was moving slowly. On the plate at the top of the page, the exposures bunch up near the top of the trajectory: the speed is smallest there, but not zero.

  1. The pebble TD 1 · Ex 4 x = 0.5 t and y = −5 t² give vx = 0.5 m·s⁻¹ (constant, positive: it moves to the right) and vy = −10 t (negative: it goes down).

    At t = 0, v(0) = (0.5; 0): the velocity is horizontal, so the pebble was indeed thrown horizontally.

  2. The car TD 1 · Ex 1 x(t) = 1.25 t² + 7 gives v(t) = 2.5 t. It reaches 10 m·s⁻¹ when 2.5 t = 10, that is t = 4 s.

Units

1 m·s⁻¹ = 3.6 km/h. To go from km/h to m·s⁻¹, divide by 3.6: 90 km/h = 25 m·s⁻¹, 180 km/h = 50 m·s⁻¹.

In the midterm

Differentiating Cartesian equations of motion to get v and its norm: asked in all 7 midterm papers found (2017 to 2022). An MCQ example (2020-2021): x(t) = −2t³ − t and y(t) = 4t² give v = (−6t² − 1; 8t).

Traps
  • v is collinear to the displacement dOM, not to the position vector OM. The English course says velocity is “collinear to displacement (that is, the position vector)”: that parenthesis is a slip. On the plate, keep OM displayed and drag t: the two arrows are almost never aligned.
  • The norm uses Pythagoras: ‖v‖ = √(ẋ² + ẏ²), never ẋ + ẏ.

Part 4

The acceleration vector

The acceleration vector is the derivative of the velocity vector, so the second derivative of the position vector. Its norm is measured in m·s⁻².

a(t) = dvdt = d²OMdt² = ẍ ux + ÿ uy = ẍÿ‖a‖ = √ẍ² + ÿ²

Acceleration has two possible effects: change the norm of the velocity, or change its direction (bend the trajectory). The sign of the scalar product v · a tells you which. In Cartesian coordinates, v · a = ẋẍ + ẏÿ.

SignThe two vectorsThe motion
v · a > 0point the same wayaccelerated: ‖v‖ increases
v · a = 0orthogonal (a ≠ 0)only the direction changes: the trajectory bends
v · a < 0point opposite waysdecelerated (slowed down): ‖v‖ decreases

If v and a are collinear, the direction does not change: the trajectory stays straight.

  1. The launch TD 1 · Ex 3 v = (v0 cos α; v0 sin α − gt) gives a = (0; −g), of norm g = 10 m·s⁻²: the same at every point. Then v · a = −g (v0 sin α − gt): negative on the way up (decelerated), zero at the top, positive on the way down (accelerated).

    Axis by axis (question 6): on x, uniform motion in the positive direction; on y, decelerated while going up until the top, then accelerated while coming down. Drag t on the plate: the line under the readings gives this sign.

  2. Average acceleration TD 1 · Ex 2 aavg = Δv / Δt. The TGV goes from 90 to 320 km/h in 5 min 20 s: Δv = 230 km/h ≈ 64 m·s⁻¹, Δt = 320 s, so a ≈ 0.2 m·s⁻², that is 0.02 g. The Formula 1 car goes from 0 to 180 km/h (50 m·s⁻¹) in 4 s: a = 12.5 m·s⁻² ≈ 1.25 g, about 60 times more.

In the midterm

November 2021 midterm, MCQ: x(t) = 10 − 2t² along a straight line. Differentiate: v = −4t and a = −4 m·s⁻². Then v · a = 16t > 0: the motion is accelerated, even though x decreases. None of the answers offered (“uniform”, “uniformly circular”, “decelerated”, “the norm of the acceleration is 2 m/s²”) was right. The same paper also asked for a = d²OM/dt², and the October 2022 paper for the unit m·s⁻².

Traps
  • The sign of ay alone does not tell you whether it speeds up. On the way down, ay = −g < 0 and yet the motion is accelerated, because vy is negative too. Always compare the signs of v and a.
  • At the top, vy = 0 but a = −g uy ≠ 0: zero velocity along one axis does not mean zero acceleration.

Part 5

Rectilinear motion: uniform, uniformly varied

A motion is rectilinear if its trajectory is a straight line. You take an axis along that line: OM = x(t) ux, and everything comes down to three numbers: x, v = ẋ and a = ẍ. It is uniform if the norm of its velocity is constant.

Uniform rectilinear

Constant velocity, zero acceleration.

a = 0v = v0x(t) = v0 t + x0
Uniformly varied rectilinear

Constant acceleration. Accelerated if v and a have the same sign, decelerated otherwise.

a = constantv(t) = a t + v0x(t) = ½ a t² + v0 t + x0

The three equations follow from one another: differentiate to go down from x to a, integrate to go back up. On the graphs, the velocity is the slope of x(t), the acceleration is the slope of v(t), and the area under v(t) between 0 and t is the displacement x(t) − x(0). Try the three presets.

Full example TD 1 · Ex 1 x(t) = 1.25 t² + 7 (in metres), the car of the second preset.

  1. Parked before starting: x(0) = 7 m from home.

  2. v(t) = 2.5 t; 10 m·s⁻¹ at t = 10 / 2.5 = 4 s.

  3. Position at 4 s: x(4) = 1.25 × 16 + 7 = 27 m. Since it started, it has travelled 27 − 7 = 20 m.

  4. a = dv/dt = 2.5 m·s⁻², constant: uniformly accelerated rectilinear motion.

  5. To reach 10 m·s⁻¹ in 2 s with v(0) = 0: v(t) = a t, so a = 10 / 2 = 5 m·s⁻².

  6. With 5 m·s⁻² for 4 s: v(4) = 20 m·s⁻¹; x(4) = 5 × 4²/2 + 7 = 47 m, that is 40 m travelled.

Trap: position or distance travelled?

x(t) is the distance from home (the origin), not the distance driven. The TD correction answers 27 m and 47 m to “what distance has it travelled”: those are the positions. The distance travelled since the start is x(t) − x(0) = 20 m and 40 m (the motion goes one way only). Say what you compute: “position” or “distance travelled”.

In the midterm
  • November 2021, MCQ: “A motion is said uniform if…”: its velocity (its norm) is constant. Not “its trajectory is a straight line”, not “its acceleration is constant”.
  • October 2022, MCQ: “a motion can be uniform along only one of its components” is true (the launch: uniform along x); “in a uniform rectilinear motion, the acceleration is zero” is true.

Part 6

Integrating: from acceleration to the equations of motion

Often you know the acceleration (in chapter 2, Newton’s second law, the fundamental principle of dynamics, gives it) and you look for the motion. You go back up the chain by integrating, twice.

OM(t)position
differentiateintegrate + C2
v(t)velocity
differentiateintegrate + C1
a(t)acceleration

Differentiating wipes out the constants (x0 and v0 no longer appear in a). Integrating forces you to find them again: each integration adds a constant, fixed by the initial conditions, that is the velocity and the position at t = 0.

Method, axis by axis
  1. Write the components of a: ax(t), ay(t).
  2. Integrate: vx(t) = ∫ ax dt + C1. Fix C1 with vx(0).
  3. Integrate again: x(t) = ∫ vx dt + C2. Fix C2 with x(0).
  4. Check: at t = 0 you get the initial conditions back; differentiating twice gives a back.

The course example Course p. 7 A point M is released with no initial velocity from a height y0 = 5 m, axis uy pointing up. Its acceleration is a = −g.

  1. v(t) = ∫ −g dt = −gt + c1. Released with no velocity: v(0) = 0, so c1 = 0.

  2. y(t) = ∫ −gt dt = −gt²/2 + c2. Starting at 5 m: y(0) = 5, so c2 = 5 and y(t) = 5 − gt²/2.

  3. It hits the ground when y(T) = 0: T = √(10/g). With g = 10 m·s⁻²: T = 1 s, and v(T) = −gT = −10 m·s⁻¹ (the minus sign: it goes down). The course takes g = 9.81, rounds T to 1 s and writes v = −9.81 m·s⁻¹.

The launch, on two axes. A projectile leaves O at t = 0 with an initial velocity v0 at an angle α with the horizontal. Only gravity acts: a = −g uy. The angle is measured from the horizontal, so the horizontal component of v0 takes the cosine.

x-axisy-axis
Accelerationax = 0ay = −g
Initial conditionsvx(0) = v0 cos α; x(0) = 0vy(0) = v0 sin α; y(0) = 0
Velocityvx = v0 cos αvy = −gt + v0 sin α
Positionx = v0 cos α · ty = −½ gt² + v0 sin α · t

These are the equations of motion given in exercise 3 of TD 1. On the plate, set t = 0: the readings give vx(0) = v0 cos α and vy(0) = v0 sin α.

Traps
  • Do not set the constant to 0 out of habit: it equals the initial value. c1 = 0 only if the initial velocity (along that axis) is zero.
  • The sign of g depends on the axis: uy pointing up gives ay = −g, pointing down ay = +g. Draw the axis, then write the sign.
  • ∫ gt dt = gt²/2, not gt². Differentiate your result to check.

Part 7

The trajectory equation

The equations of motion tell you where the point is at each instant. The trajectory is the curve it draws, without the time information: a relation between y and x. To get it, you eliminate t.

Method
  1. Isolate t in the simplest equation, often x(t), which is linear in t.
  2. Substitute t into the other equation.
  3. Simplify and recognise the curve: straight line, parabola, circle…

  1. The pebble TD 1 · Ex 4 x = 0.5 t gives t = 2x. Then y = −5 (2x)² = −20 x²: a downward parabola with its top at O.

  2. The launch TD 1 · Ex 3 x = v0 cos α · t gives t = xv0 cos α. Substitute into y(t):

    y(x) = −g2 v0² cos² α x² + x tan α

    A parabola: that is why TD 1 calls it parabolic motion.

Check

There is no t left in y(x). Test a known point: x = 0 must give y = 0 back, the starting point.

In the midterm
  • The trajectory equation is asked in 6 of the 7 papers found (circle, ellipse, straight line, parabola); the parabola of a launch in the last two (2021, 2022).
  • October 2022, MCQ: x(t) = 4t and y(t) = 3t² − 6t + 5. With t = x/4: y = 3x²/16 − 3x/2 + 5, the motion is parabolic. Its velocity (4; 6t − 6) never vanishes, since vx = 4.
  • Same MCQ: “the trajectory equation tells you whether the motion is uniform” is false.
Traps
  • Write y(x) = …, not y(t) = …: the variable is x. A stray t in “y(x)” is a mistake.
  • The trajectory does not say how fast the point travels along it: two bodies can follow the same parabola at different speeds. For instants and velocities, go back to the equations of motion.
  • (v0 cos α)² = v0² cos² α: the square applies to the whole product.

Projectiles and falls: top, range, point of impact

With the equations of motion of the launch (part 6), three questions keep coming back: the top of the trajectory, the range, the point of impact. Each one becomes a simple condition on vy or on y.

You look forConditionResult (launch from the ground)
The top S (apex)the vertical velocity vanishes: vy(tS) = 0tS = v0 sin αg; xS = v0² sin α cos αg; yS = v0² sin² α2g
The point of impact P, the rangeit lands on the ground: y(tP) = 0, with tP ≠ 0tP = 2 v0 sin αg; xP = 2 v0² sin α cos αg = v0² sin 2αg

For P, factorise: y = t (v0 sin α − gt/2) = 0. Two solutions: t = 0, the start, and tP = 2 v0 sin α / g, the impact. Then xP = x(tP).

Without a calculator: v0 = 20 m·s⁻¹, α = 30°, g = 10 m·s⁻², with sin 30° = ½ and cos 30° = √3/2 ≈ 0.87. These are the starting settings of the plate at the top of the page.

  1. tS = 20 × ½ / 10 = 1 s; yS = 400 × ¼ / 20 = 5 m; xS = 400 × ½ × (√3/2) / 10 = 10√3 ≈ 17 m.

  2. tP = 2 tS = 2 s; xP = 20√3 ≈ 35 m.

  3. At the top, v = v0 cos α ux: 10√3 ≈ 17 m·s⁻¹, horizontal.

The fall into a well TD 1 · Ex 4 The same pebble is thrown horizontally from the edge of a well, origin at the starting point: y(t) = −5 t². It reaches the bottom after 2.0 s: y(2) = −5 × 4 = −20 m. Since the origin is at the start, the depth is −y: the well is 20 m deep.

If the origin is on the ground, below a launch point at height h, a horizontal throw gives y(t) = h − ½ gt²: it hits the ground at tP = √(2h/g), and xP = v0 tP. With h = 20 m: tP = 2 s, like the well.

Remember
  • At the top, only vy vanishes: v(tS) = v0 cos α ux, horizontal, not zero.
  • Start and impact at the same height: tP = 2 tS (the parabola is symmetric) and the velocity at impact has the same norm v0.
  • For a given v0, the range is largest at α = 45° (sin 2α = 1): xP = v0²/g. Check it on the plate.
In the midterm

The parabolic launch came up in the last two midterms found. November 2021: OM given with “5t²” (g = 10), equations of motion, trajectory, velocity and its magnitude, then the maximal height v0² sin² α / 20 reached at the apex. October 2022: a ball launched from (0, h), the same questions, the nature of the motion on each axis, vectors v and a to draw at four points (that is the plate), the top xS = v0² sin α cos α / g and yS = h + v0² sin² α / (2g).

Traps
  • If the impact is not at the starting height (launch from a height h, fall into a well), tP ≠ 2 tS: solve y(tP) = landing height and keep the positive root.
  • t = 0 is the “start” solution of y(t) = 0, not the impact.
  • sin α cos α = ½ sin 2α: do not confuse sin 2α with 2 sin α. And √(2h/g), not √(2h)/g.
