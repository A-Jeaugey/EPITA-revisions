Part 1

The work done by a constant force

Newton’s second law is not always enough to derive the equations of motion: some forces are hard to express, and sometimes you only want a speed at a given place. The course then uses a different approach, based on energy. It starts with one quantity: work.

A force F acting on a moving system splits into two components. F⊥, perpendicular to the trajectory, curves it. F∥, parallel to the trajectory, changes the speed. Work measures how much the force accelerates or decelerates the system: only the parallel component counts.

WA→B = ∫𝒞 F · dlin Joules (J), 1 J = 1 N·m; dl: infinitesimal displacement of the point along the trajectory 𝒞

It is a line integral: a priori it depends on both the force and the path. If the force is constant (same magnitude, same direction all along the path), it becomes a simple scalar product with the displacement AB:

WA→B = F · AB = F × AB × cos θθ: angle between the force F and the displacement AB

In Cartesian components, the same scalar product reads F · AB = Fx (xB − xA) + Fy (yB − yA). This is handy when the force is given by its components, like the weight (part 2). Change the angle: only the projection F cos θ on the displacement does work.

  1. Work done by a force TD 4 · Ex 2 F = 3 N, point M moves from A to B, AB = 50 cm, and F makes 30° with the line (AB), on the side of B: the angle between F and AB is 30°.

    W = 3 × 0.50 × cos 30° = 1.5 × √3/2 = 0.75 √3 ≈ 1.3 J, positive. This is the starting setting of the bench.

  2. The toy TD 4 · Ex 2 A string makes 40° with the floor, pulling force T = 10 N, displacement AB = 5.0 m along the floor: WAB(T) = 10 × 5.0 × cos 40° ≈ 38 J. The cosine of 40° (≈ 0.77) needs a calculator: without one, a test would give you the value or use a standard angle.

Remember
  • Work is a number (a scalar), positive, negative or zero. It is not a vector. Unit: the Joule, 1 J = 1 N·m.
  • Without a calculator: cos 0 = 1, cos 30° = √3/2 ≈ 0.87, cos 45° = √2/2 ≈ 0.71, cos 60° = 1/2, cos 90° = 0, cos 180° = −1.
Traps
  • θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. An angle given from the vertical must be converted first: a force at 30° from the vertical, for a horizontal displacement, makes 60° with that displacement.
  • Lengths in metres: 50 cm = 0.50 m. With “50”, the work comes out a hundred times too large.
  • W = F · AB assumes a force that is constant along the whole path. Otherwise, use the integral.

Part 2

Driving force, resisting force, work done by the weight

The sign of the work tells you whether the force helps or hinders the motion. With W = F × AB × cos θ, it all comes from the sign of cos θ. The course distinguishes three cases.

SignAngle θThe force
W > 00 ≤ θ < 90°is driving (positive work): it tends to accelerate the system
W = 0θ = 90°is perpendicular to the displacement: it can curve the trajectory, without changing the speed
W < 090° < θ ≤ 180°is resisting (negative work): it tends to slow the system down
Forces that do no work

The surface reaction force R: the course says it is always perpendicular to the surface, hence to the displacement. W(R) = 0. The weight, during a horizontal displacement.

TD 4, Ex 3 and 5: “Which forces do work?” Start by ruling these out.

Friction

It always opposes the motion: θ = 180°. A friction force of constant magnitude f, over a path of length L: W(f) = −f × L. Always resisting.

TD 4, Ex 3 and 4: friction of constant magnitude.

The work done by the weight

With uy pointing upwards, the weight is W = −mg uy, with components (0, −mg). Between A (xA, yA) and B (xB, yB), the component form gives:

WA→B(W) = Wx (xB − xA)+ Wy (yB − yA)= 0 − mg (yB − yA)= mg (yA − yB)only the difference in height counts; for a descent of height h: W(W) = mgh

Neither the horizontal distance nor the shape of the path matters (part 5). The weight does positive work when the system moves downwards (yA > yB), negative work when it moves upwards.

  1. The ball TD 4 · Ex 2 m = 500 g = 0.500 kg, and WAB = 5.4 J for its weight.

    a. W > 0, so yA > yB: the ball moves downwards.

    b. yA − yB = WAB / (mg) = 5.4 / (0.500 × 10) = 1.08 m. The exercise does not give g: 10 m·s⁻², as in the rest of the TD (1.10 m with 9.81).

Remember

WA→B(W) = mg (yA − yB): starting height minus final height. Moving down: positive. Moving up: negative. Staying at the same height: zero.

Traps
  • “Driving” does not mean the system speeds up. A driving force can be beaten by resisting forces: the sum of all the works decides (part 4).
  • In mg (yA − yB), the order matters. Always check: a falling object receives positive work from its weight.
  • In the English course, the letter W is used twice: W with an arrow is the weight (a vector, in Newtons), WA→B is the work (a number, in Joules). Keep the arrow and the indices.

Part 3

Power

Work says how much energy a force transfers; power says how fast. It is the same idea as in electricity, where electric power describes how fast electric energy is delivered.

P = dWdt = F · dldt = F · vin Watts (W), 1 W = 1 J/s; v: velocity of the system at that instant

Since dl/dt = v, the power of a force is the scalar product of the force and the velocity: P = F v cos θ, with θ the angle between F and v. It has the sign of the work: positive for a driving force, negative for a resisting force, zero if F ⊥ v. If it stays constant for a duration Δt, W = P × Δt.

Example TD 2 · Ex 3 The 10 kg robot moves on horizontal ground. Its motor exerts F = 50 N parallel to the ground, its initial speed is 2 m·s⁻¹. Without friction, Newton’s second law gives v(t) = 5t + 2.

  1. F and v point the same way: P = Fv = 50 (5t + 2). 100 W at the start, 600 W at t = 2 s: with a constant force, the power grows with the speed.

  2. With the friction f = −α v (α = 0.5 kg·s⁻¹, part II of the TD): P(f) = f · v = −α v², always negative.

    At the maximum speed vmax = F/α = 100 m·s⁻¹: P(F) = 50 × 100 = 5000 W and P(f) = −0.5 × 100² = −5000 W. The sum is zero: the speed no longer changes (part 4).

Remember
  • P = F · v, in Watts; 1 kW = 1000 W.
  • Constant power during Δt: W = P Δt. For instance 1 kW for 1 min delivers 60,000 J.
Traps
  • Three W’s in the English course: W the weight (Newtons), WA→B the work (Joules), and W the unit Watt for power. Write the arrow on the weight, the indices on the work.
  • Power uses the velocity at the instant considered: if v changes, P changes, even with a constant force.
  • TD 4 (Ex 3) asks whether a motor is “powerful enough” to climb a slope: the question is settled with works, in Joules (part 7), not with Watts.

Part 4

Kinetic energy and the work-kinetic energy theorem

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has due to its motion. For a system of mass m moving at speed v (the norm of v):

Ek = ½ m v²in Joules; always positive or zero, zero only at rest

The work-kinetic energy theorem connects the work done by the forces with the change in kinetic energy. Between two points A and B, the sum of the works done by all the forces acting on the system is:

Σi Wi = ΔEk = ½ m vB² − ½ m vA²or, with powers: Σi Pi = dEkdt

Positive sum: the system is faster at B than at A. Negative: slower. Zero: same speed. This is what was missing in part 2: a single driving force does not tell you whether the system speeds up, the sum does. On the plate at the top of the page, slide the solid from A to B: only the weight does work, positive work, and the green Ek bar grows.

Method
  1. System, reference frame (terrestrial, assumed Galilean).
  2. Forces involved, with a force diagram.
  3. Work done by each force between A and B. Rule out the ones that do no work (reaction without friction, weight on a horizontal path).
  4. Theorem: sum of the works = ½ m vB² − ½ m vA².
  5. Isolate the unknown (speed, force, distance), then do the numerical application.

  1. The course example Course p. 9 A body of mass m falls from a height H, with no initial velocity, subject only to its own weight. The weight goes down by H: its work is mgH. The theorem gives ½ m vB² − 0 = mgH, so vB = √(2gH). The mass cancels out. It is also question 3 of TD 4 Ex 1, with h: vsol = √(2gh).

  2. The puck TD 4 · Ex 5 On AA’ (horizontal, length L): W and R are perpendicular to the displacement and do no work; the thrust F, in the direction of motion, gives FL. Starting from rest: ½ m vA’² = FL, so F = m vA’² / (2L).

    On A’B (horizontal, no friction, no thrust), no force does work: ΔEk = 0, so vB = vA’.

  3. With powers TD 2 · Ex 3 The robot of part 3, without friction: Ek = ½ × 10 × (5t + 2)². Its derivative is 10 × 5 × (5t + 2) = 50 (5t + 2): exactly the power of the motor, the only force doing work.

Traps
  • v² is the square of the norm: ½ m v² does not depend on the direction of v. The theorem gives a speed, not a vector.
  • ΔEk = final − initial: ½ m vB² − ½ m vA², in that order.
  • Do not leave out any resisting force from the sum, especially friction.
  • Each Wi in the sum is a work, a number: no arrow. Do not confuse it with the weight W.

Part 5

Conservative forces and potential energy

The work done by the weight depends only on the starting and final heights (part 2), not on the path. Forces with this property are called conservative.

Definition

A force is conservative when its work between A and B does not depend on the path used, but only on the positions A and B. Such a force is derived from a potential energy Ep, which characterises the system energetically at each position:

WA→B = −ΔEp = Ep(A) − Ep(B)

PathLength LWork of WWork of f, f = 2 N
① straight line6.7 m30 J−13.4 J
② in steps9.0 m30 J−18.0 J
③ over a hump8.0 m30 J−16.0 J

The weight is conservative: 30 J on all three paths. A friction force of constant magnitude is not: it costs f × L, and the three paths do not have the same length. Friction has no potential energy.

For the weight, WA→B(W) = mgyA − mgyB. Comparing with Ep(A) − Ep(B):

Ep = mghgravitational potential energy; h: height of the system above the ground, with the assumption that Ep = 0 on the ground

Where Ep = 0 is a choice: the ground, the bottom of the track, the lowest point. Only differences in potential energy count, and they do not depend on that choice. Choose it once, write it down, keep it. (There are other potential energies, such as that of a spring, seen with oscillators in midterm 2.)

  1. The circular arc TD 4 · Ex 4 “Show that h = R (1 − cos θ).” O is vertically above B, and OA = OB = R. Measured downwards from O, B is at R and A at R cos θ (side adjacent to the angle θ). So h = R − R cos θ = R (1 − cos θ) = 1.6 × (1 − 1/2) = 0.80 m.

    With Ep = 0 at B, m = 0.10 kg and g = 10 m·s⁻²: Ep(A) = mgh = 0.80 J. This is the track of the plate at the top of the page.

Why a minus sign

When the system moves down, the weight does positive work (W > 0) and the potential energy decreases (ΔEp < 0). The work done by the weight is drawn from the Ep reserve: W = −ΔEp.

Traps
  • h is a height, not the length of the path. On a slope of length L inclined at α, h = L sin α.
  • The sign: W = −ΔEp = Ep(A) − Ep(B), start minus end, as for the work done by the weight.

Part 6

Mechanical energy and its conservation

Mechanical energy is the sum of the kinetic energy and the potential energies. Conservation of mechanical energy (course, p. 10): in all systems in which only conservative forces are acting, it is conserved during motion.

EM = Ek + Ep = const.so EM(A) = EM(B) for any two points of the motion
Where it comes from

If only the weight does work (the other forces do no work), the work-kinetic energy theorem gives ΔEk = WA→B(W) = −ΔEp. So ΔEk + ΔEp = 0: ΔEM = 0. Energy changes from one form to the other, with no loss.

To use it, you must justify it: show that the non-conservative forces do no work (friction neglected, reaction perpendicular to the displacement). It is question 2 of TD 4 Ex 1: “Explain why this theorem can be used.”

Free fall TD 4 · Ex 1 A ball released at a height h with no initial velocity, friction neglected, Ep = 0 on the ground.

y(0) = hy = 0 (ground)
Ek0½ m vsol²
Epmgh0
EMmgh½ m vsol²

Only the weight, a conservative force, acts: EM is conserved. mgh = ½ m vsol², so vsol = √(2gh): the same result as with the work-kinetic energy theorem (part 4).

  1. The circular arc TD 4 · Ex 4 On AB, with no friction, R is perpendicular to the trajectory and does no work; only the weight does work: EM is conserved. Going down, Ep decreases and Ek increases by the same amount (question 1).

    Question 3, with Ep = 0 at B: ½ m vA² + mgh = ½ m vB², so vB = √(vA² + 2gR (1 − cos θ)) = √(9 + 16) = 5.0 m·s⁻¹.

    On the plate, slide the solid from A to B: Ek (green) and Ep (blue) are exchanged, their sum stays at 0.45 + 0.80 = 1.25 J.

  2. The puck TD 4 · Ex 5 From B to D, with no friction, it must reach D with zero velocity; Ep = 0 on AB. ½ m vB² = mgh, so vB = √(2gh) = √30 ≈ 5.5 m·s⁻¹.

    With part 4 (F = m vB² / (2L) and vB = vA’): F = mgh / L = 5.0 × 10 × 1.5 / 0.50 = 150 N. At C, R (1 − cos θ) above B: vC = √(vB² − 2gR (1 − cos θ)).

Remember
  • EM is conserved when only conservative forces do work. Justify it every time.
  • EM(A) = EM(B) gives a speed from a difference in height, without computing the duration or the trajectory.
  • Same height, same speed: the projectile of chapter 1 hits the ground with its launch speed v0 (in norm).
Traps
  • Writing “EM = const.” when there is friction: wrong (part 7).
  • Ek and Ep are not conserved separately: their sum is.
  • Choose where Ep = 0 and keep it throughout the equation: on the ground (Ex 1), at B (Ex 4), on AB (Ex 5).

Part 7

Friction: mechanical energy decreases

Forces of friction are non-conservative: their work, always negative, depends on the length of the path. The course says it: they dissipate the system’s energy (as heat). Mechanical energy decreases.

The course does not write it as a formula, but the work-kinetic energy theorem gives it in one line. With the weight, a reaction that does no work and a friction force f:

W(W) + W(R) + W(f) = ΔEk−ΔEp + 0 + W(f) = ΔEkΔEM = W(f) < 0show it again in your answer, starting from the theorem; in general, ΔEM = sum of the works done by the non-conservative forces (friction, thrust of a motor…)

On the plate at the top of the page, compare with and without “Friction on BC”. On the arc, nothing changes. On BC, the hatched part of EM grows by f × (distance travelled): that is the dissipated energy.

  1. The arc, continued TD 4 · Ex 4 On BC (horizontal), W and R do no work; only f does: W(f) = −f × BC. The theorem gives ½ m vC² − ½ m vB² = −f BC, so

    f = m (vB² − vC²) / (2 BC) = 0.10 × (25 − 9) / (2 × 2.0) = 0.40 N

    Balance: EM goes from 1.25 J to 0.45 J. The 0.80 J lost is indeed f × BC = 0.40 × 2.0.

  2. The robot TD 4 · Ex 3 m = 100 kg, thrust force Fp = 250 N, friction f = 50 N, α = 30°, AB = 10 m, h = 20 m (height of C), starting from rest at A.

    On AB (horizontal), W and R do no work: ½ m vB² = (Fp − f) × AB = 200 × 10 = 2000 J, so vB = √40 ≈ 6.3 m·s⁻¹.

    On BC (uphill, BC = h / sin α = 40 m): Fp is driving, W and f are resistive, R does no work. ½ m vC² = 2000 + 250 × 40 − 50 × 40 − 100 × 10 × 20 = −10,000 J.

    A negative kinetic energy is impossible: the robot does not reach C, the motor is not powerful enough. Each metre of slope costs it 500 + 50 − 250 = 300 J; it stops after 2000 / 300 ≈ 6.7 m. To reach C, it would need Fp ≥ 500 N.

The robot exercise: read with care

Part 3 of this exercise (descent CD, energy stored in the battery) assumes the robot has reached C, which part 2 has just ruled out, and does not give its speed at C. The exercise is inconsistent: if you have to do it, write down the assumption you make (for instance vC = 0).

Remember
  • With friction, ΔEM = W(f) < 0. For a constant magnitude, the dissipated energy is f × (length travelled).
  • Ek cannot be negative: a calculation that gives Ek < 0 at a point means the system never gets there.
Traps
  • “Collinear to the velocity vector” (TD 4 Ex 4): same direction as v, but opposite orientation. The work done by friction is negative.
  • In f × L, L is the distance travelled on the part with friction, not a height.

Choosing between the second law and energy

Two tools for the same problem. Newton’s second law (chapter 2) is a vector law and gives the whole motion, at the cost of two integrations. Energy gives a speed in one line, without time, but only in norm.

The questionThe toolWhy
A speed at a given height, or after a given distanceenergy: work-kinetic energy theorem, or EM if conservativeneither time nor the trajectory is needed
A constant force, knowing two speeds and a distancework-kinetic energy theoremthe work F × L makes the link (TD 4 Ex 4 and 5)
A duration, a time, a position at instant tsecond law, then integrateenergy does not contain time
The trajectory equation, the direction of vsecond lawenergy only gives the norm of the velocity
A reaction R, an equilibriumsecond law, projecteda force that does no work does not appear in an energy balance
A curved track (circular arc)energyonly the height counts; the second law would need polar coordinates (chapter 4)

The same fall, twice Course p. 7 and p. 9 A point released with no initial velocity from 5 m high, with g = 10 m·s⁻².

Second law

a = −g; v = −gt; y = 5 − gt²/2.

y(T) = 0 gives T = √(10/g) = 1 s, then v(T) = −gT = −10 m·s⁻¹.

Five lines, but you also get the duration and the direction (downwards).

Energy

Only the weight does work: EM is conserved.

mg × 5 = ½ m v², so v = √(2 × 10 × 5) = 10 m·s⁻¹.

Two lines, the norm only.

The course takes g = 9.81: it rounds T = √(10/9.81) ≈ 1.01 s to 1 s and writes v(T) = −9.81 m·s⁻¹. Without rounding, both methods give 9.90 m·s⁻¹. Likewise, the parachutist of TD 3 (Ex 1, 3000 m with no friction) reaches √(2 × 9.81 × 3000) ≈ 242.6 m·s⁻¹ by energy, without computing the duration of the fall; the official solution finds 242 m·s⁻¹ with the second law.

Write-up of an energy solution
  • System and reference frame: terrestrial, assumed Galilean.
  • Forces involved (force diagram), and for each one its work between A and B, or “does no work: perpendicular to the displacement”.
  • Theorem and reason: “only the weight does work, EM is conserved”, or “work-kinetic energy theorem between A and B”.
  • Equation, literal result, homogeneity (√(gh): √(m·s⁻² × m) = m·s⁻¹), then numerical application with the unit.
In the midterm

None of the 7 past papers found (2017 to 2022) asks about work or energy, and the content of this year’s midterm 1 has not been published. To practise, the exercises of TD 4 are the best basis; also redo a fall from TD 3 with energy.

Traps
  • Energy gives a norm. For the direction, you need the coordinate system: vy(T) = −10 m·s⁻¹ is a component, 10 m·s⁻¹ its norm.
  • Do not mix the two methods in one equation: either Σ Wi = ΔEk, or Σ F = m a.
