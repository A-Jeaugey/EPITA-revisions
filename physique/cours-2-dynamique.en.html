Part 1

The causes of motion: forces and Galilean reference frames

Kinematics described motion. Dynamics looks for its causes: forces. It rests on Newton’s three laws of motion, which are postulates: you do not prove them, you observe that they work.

The course starts with three definitions, and a point about the reference frame.

Mass m

A measure of a body’s ability to resist being set in motion. In kg, proportional to the amount of matter in the body.

The larger the mass, the more force it takes to change the velocity: this is inertia.

Force F

A vector quantity describing an interaction and its ability to modify motion. In newtons: 1 N = 1 kg·m·s⁻².

A force always has an author: another object (the Earth, the ground, a string, the air, a hand).

Momentum p

p = m v, in kg·m·s⁻¹. Newton’s second law is stated with it.

Same direction as v, since m > 0.

Galilean reference frame

The course: a reference frame that moves without any acceleration is called Galilean. Newton’s laws only hold in such a frame.

The terrestrial reference frame is not exactly Galilean (the Earth spins); for a short experiment, you assume it is Galilean.

A force, four pieces of information
  • Norm (magnitude): its intensity, in newtons (N).
  • Direction: the line it acts along (vertical, along the string, perpendicular to the ground…).
  • Sense: up or down, left or right. The English handout folds this into “direction”.
  • Point of application: on the object that is subjected to the force. In point mechanics, it is the point M itself.

Example TD 2 · Ex 3 “Its motor exerts a force F of magnitude 50 N, parallel to the ground, and directed towards increasing x values.” Magnitude 50 N, horizontal, towards increasing x, applied to the robot. As a vector: F = 50 ux (in N).

In the midterm

This chapter is on this year’s syllabus: course chapters II to IV, TD 2 and 3. None of the 7 old midterms found (2017 to 2022) was about forces: the syllabus was different. To practise, the exercises of TD 2 and 3 are the reference, and this chapter follows their corrections.

Traps
  • A velocity is not a force. “It moves forward, so a force pushes it forward” is wrong: an object can move with no force at all (part 5). It is the sum of the forces that makes the velocity change.
  • Newton’s laws assume a Galilean reference frame. Write it: “terrestrial reference frame, assumed Galilean”.

Part 2

Forces involved and the force diagram

Before writing one of Newton’s laws, you list every force acting on the system: the forces involved. A force forgotten, or invented, makes the acceleration wrong, and everything after it.

Method: listing the forces
  1. Define the system: a single object, modelled as a material point.
  2. Forces acting at a distance: the weight, always, near the Earth (and the electrostatic force if the system is charged).
  3. Contact forces: look at everything that touches the system. A surface (reaction), a string (tension), a fluid (friction, buoyancy), a hand, a motor.
  4. For each force, name its author: “force exerted by … on the system”. No author, no force.

The diagram. The TD 2 correction (in French) says it: “we do point mechanics […], so the systems studied are considered as points. We represent them with a simple cross.” Each force is an arrow starting from that cross, longer or shorter depending on its magnitude. Add the axes: you will project on them.

Your turn: pick a situation, tick the forces, then check. The diagram builds up as you go.

The first four situations are those of exercise 1 in TD 2; the robot comes from exercise 3, the rugby ball and the parachutist from TD 3. The correction concludes: “equilibrium situations translate into a zero vector sum”. The swinging ball is not in equilibrium: its velocity changes direction.

Traps
  • No “force of motion” and no “force of the kick”: once the ball has left, the foot no longer touches it. Only the weight remains (air resistance ignored).
  • A contact force needs contact: no surface reaction force on an object in the air.
  • The list only contains forces exerted on the system. The force of the book on the table acts on the table: it is not among the forces on the book.

Part 3

Weight, surface reaction, tension in a string

Three forces come back in almost every exercise. The weight has a formula; the reaction and the tension do not: they are the unknowns that Newton’s second law lets you find.

Weight

Near the Earth’s surface, the gravitational force exerted by the Earth on an object of mass m is called its weight. It is vertical, pointing down, applied at point M. The English course writes it W (the French handouts use P).

W = m gW = mgg: gravitational acceleration of the Earth, vertical, pointing down; g ≈ 9.81 m·s⁻² (10 m·s⁻² when the paper says so)

With uy pointing up, as in the course: W = −mg uy, that is, the column (0; −mg).

Examples TD 3 The parachutist, m = 80 kg: W = 80 × 9.81 ≈ 785 N. The rugby ball, m = 420 g = 0.42 kg: W ≈ 4.1 N.

Surface reaction force

An object resting on a surface experiences the surface reaction force R: it is what prevents the object from passing through the surface. There is no general expression for it: it must be determined case by case. In the course, it is always orthogonal to the surface.

Friction with the ground, when it matters, is a separate force, f, parallel to the surface: this is the case of the robot on the slope TD 2 · Ex 2. On the plate at the top of the page, R stays perpendicular to the slope when you change α, and its magnitude is mg cos α, not mg.

Tension in a string

A string attached to an object exerts the tension T on it: a contact force, applied at the point of attachment, with the direction of the string, pointing from the object towards the string. A string pulls, it never pushes. Its magnitude is determined case by case.

Example A 50 g ball hangs at rest at the end of a string (g = 10 m·s⁻²). Forces involved: W and T. At rest, so T + W = 0 (part 5): the string is vertical and T = mg = 0.05 × 10 = 0.5 N.

Remember
  • Mass and weight are two different quantities. Mass, in kg, does not depend on the place. Weight, in N, is mg and depends on g: on the Moon, g ≈ 1.6 m·s⁻².
  • R and T have no formula: their magnitudes come out of Newton’s second law.
  • In English, W is both the weight W (a vector, with an arrow) and the work WA→B of chapter 3 (a number, with its indices). Write the arrow and the indices.
Traps
  • R = mg only on a horizontal surface, with no other vertical force. Robot on horizontal ground (TD 2 · Ex 3): R = mg. Robot on the slope (TD 2 · Ex 2): R = mg cos α.
  • The weight is vertical, not perpendicular to the ground: on a slope, these are no longer the same direction.
  • The tension points from the object towards the string (towards the attachment point), never the other way.

Part 4

Friction, gravity, electrostatic force

Friction from a fluid

An object moving with a velocity v in a fluid (air, water) is subjected to a force of friction from the fluid:

f = −α vα: friction coefficient; it depends on the fluid, the shape of the body, its speed…

The minus sign says that f always points opposite to the velocity: it opposes the motion. Its magnitude αv grows with the speed.

Friction between an object and the ground (the robot on the slope in TD 2) has no formula in the course: it is parallel to the surface, opposes sliding, and its magnitude comes out of Newton’s second law.

  1. The sign TD 2 · Ex 3 “What is the meaning of the minus sign in the expression of the friction force?” That f and v point in opposite directions: friction opposes the motion.

  2. The unit of α, by homogeneity: f is in newtons and v in m·s⁻¹, so [α] = kg·m·s⁻²m·s⁻¹ = kg·s⁻¹.

  3. Orders of magnitude with α = 0.5 kg·s⁻¹: at 10 m·s⁻¹, f = 5 N; at 100 m·s⁻¹, f = 50 N, as much as the motor force. That is the robot’s maximum speed (part 5).

Gravity

Two bodies of masses m1 and m2, whose centres are a distance d apart, attract each other. This force is always attractive and has infinite range.

F = G m1 m2d²F1→2 = −G m1 m2d² u1→2G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²·kg⁻² (universal gravitational constant); u1→2: unit vector from 1 to 2

F1→2 is the force exerted by 1 on 2: it pulls 2 towards 1, along −u1→2. The English handout writes F2→1 = G m1m2/d² ur, with no minus sign and without saying where ur points; with a unit vector going from 1 to 2, the minus sign is needed, otherwise the force would be repulsive. Above all, remember the magnitude and the direction: towards the other body.

The weight, recovered. The course points it out: with the mass of the Earth ME ≈ 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg for m1 and its radius RE ≈ 6.4 × 10⁶ m for d, the force on a mass m at ground level is m × GME/RE². And that factor is g:

GMERE² ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 6.0 × 10²⁴(6.4 × 10⁶)²≈ 9.8 m·s⁻²

Electrostatic force (Coulomb force)

Between two charges q1 and q2 (in coulombs), a distance d apart:

F1→2 = k q1 q2d² u1→2k ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²·C⁻² (Coulomb constant; the English handout prints 8.88, a typo)

A charge can be negative. If q1q2 > 0 (charges of the same sign), the force pushes 2 away from 1: repulsive. If q1q2 < 0, it is attractive. No minus sign here: the sign of the product of the charges decides.

Not in the course, but in the TDs
  • The buoyant force (Archimedes) FA: vertical, pointing up, exerted by a fluid on an immersed body; its magnitude is the weight of the displaced fluid. It appears in the TD 2 correction (the floating ball).
  • The restoring force of a spring comes with oscillators (part 2 of the course, second midterm syllabus).

Part 5

Newton’s first law: the law of inertia

The course states it this way: in a Galilean reference frame, a body remains in the same state of motion if no force is acting on it.

  • If the body was at rest, it remains at rest.
  • If the body was in motion, it stays in motion, which is uniform and rectilinear: in a straight line, at constant speed.

Near the Earth, an object with no force acting on it does not exist: the weight is always there. What matters is the sum of the forces. If they balance out, everything happens as if there were none. With the second law (part 6), you can write it both ways:

Σ F = 0 ⟺ v constantin a Galilean reference frame: at rest, or uniform rectilinear motion

This is the sentence of the TD 2 correction: “equilibrium situations translate into a zero vector sum”.

  1. The book at rest TD 2 · Ex 1 At rest, so W + R = 0: R = W = mg.

  2. The robot’s maximum speed TD 2 · Ex 3 With the friction f = −α v, the robot accelerates more and more slowly, then moves in a straight line at a constant speed vmax. Then a = 0 and the sum of the forces is zero. Vertically, W + R = 0; horizontally, F + f = 0:

    α vmax = F ⇒ vmax = Fα = 500.5 = 100 m·s⁻¹

    The motor still pushes, but friction balances it exactly: the velocity no longer changes.

Why “Galilean” matters

In a braking car, your bag slides forward although nothing pushes it: the braking car is not a Galilean reference frame, and the first law does not hold in it. In the terrestrial reference frame, everything makes sense: the bag keeps going straight at the same speed, while the car slows down under it.

Traps
  • “It moves forward, so the sum of the forces points forward”: wrong. At constant speed in a straight line, the sum is zero (the robot at vmax).
  • Uniform is not enough: it must be rectilinear and uniform. The ball swinging round at constant speed changes direction: Σ F ≠ 0.
  • At rest for one instant is not equilibrium. At the top of a vertical throw, v = 0 but Σ F = W ≠ 0: it falls back down.

Part 6

Newton’s second law

The course: in a Galilean reference frame, the derivative of a body’s momentum with respect to time is equal to the sum of all forces acting on it. The mass is constant throughout this course, so dp/dt = m dv/dt = m a:

Σi Fi = dpdt = m aSometimes called the law of acceleration. The TD corrections exist only in French: there, this law is called “PFD”.

What it says. a has the direction of the sum of the forces, not that of the velocity. Its magnitude is ‖Σ F‖ / m: for the same force, a mass twice as large accelerates half as much. The TD 2 correction: “for a given thrust force, the larger the robot’s mass, the harder it is to make it accelerate: that is inertia”.

The link with chapter 1. The forces give a; you then integrate, with the initial conditions, to get v and then OM (chapter 1, part 6).

Σ F → a = Σ F / m → v(t) → OM(t)divide by m, then integrate twice with v(0) and OM(0)

Full example TD 2 · Ex 3 A robot of mass m = 10 kg moves on a horizontal surface, friction neglected. Its motor exerts F, of magnitude 50 N, towards increasing x; at A, at t = 0, its velocity is 2 m·s⁻¹. Origin at A, ux along F, uy pointing up.

  1. Forces involved: weight W, reaction R, motor force F. Second law: W + R + F = m a.

  2. As columns: 0−mg + 0R + F0 = maxmay

  3. Along y: the robot stays on the ground, ay = 0, so R = mg. Along x: F = max, so a = F/m = 50/10 = 5 m·s⁻².

  4. Integrate: v(t) = 5t + 2, then x(t) = 2.5 t² + 2t (with x(0) = 0 at A).

  5. In one minute: x(60) = 2.5 × 3600 + 2 × 60 = 9120 m, that is 9.12 km.

Remember
  • Σ F = m a is an equality between vectors: it gives one equation per axis.
  • If the sum of the forces is constant, so is a: uniformly accelerated or parabolic motion, and the formulas of chapter 1 apply.
  • Homogeneity: F/m is in N/kg = m·s⁻². Check the unit of every result.
Traps
  • a does not have the direction of v. The rugby ball goes up (v pointing up) while a = g points down.
  • m a is not a force: do not add it to the forces involved, it is the other side of the equality.
  • The TD 2 correction projects the weight as (0; P): P there stands for the component, which equals −mg. Write (0; −mg) directly: the sign shows.

Part 7

Newton’s third law: action-reaction

The course: if a body M1 exerts a force F1→2 on body M2, then M2 exerts on M1 a force of the same magnitude but opposite direction:

F2→1 = −F1→2same magnitude, same line, opposite senses

The two forces of a pair act on two different objects. So they never appear among the forces on the same system, and they never cancel out.

  1. The apple and the Earth A 100 g apple: the Earth attracts it with its weight, 0.1 × 9.81 ≈ 0.98 N. The apple attracts the Earth with 0.98 N too. But a = F/m: the apple gets 9.81 m·s⁻², the Earth (6.0 × 10²⁴ kg) about 1.6 × 10⁻²⁵ m·s⁻². Same forces, very different effects.

  2. The book and the table The table pushes the book up (R); the book presses down on the table with the same magnitude. Press on the book with your hand: R increases, and so does the force of the book on the table.

Spotting a pair

Write each force as “exerted by A on B”, then swap A and B: “Earth on apple” ↔ “apple on Earth”. If it is not the same two objects, it is not a pair.

Trap

W and R of the book at rest are opposite, but this is not the third law: they act on the same object (the book) and come from two different authors (the Earth, the table). They balance because the book is at rest (first law). Press on the book: R increases, W does not change.

Part 8

Using Newton’s second law: project on well-chosen axes

“When all forces acting on a system are known, Newton’s second law allows us to determine the equations of motion” (course, p. 6). Every exercise follows the same method: that of the course example and of the TD 2 and 3 corrections.

Write-up method
  1. System: “material point M of mass m”. Reference frame: terrestrial, assumed Galilean.
  2. Coordinate system: origin and axes. Choose them so that as many forces as possible lie along an axis: vertical for a fall, along the slope for an inclined plane.
  3. Forces involved and force diagram.
  4. Second law as vectors: Σ F = m a (Σ F = 0 at equilibrium).
  5. Projection: each vector as a column, then one equation per axis.
  6. Solving: one unknown per equation (R, f, a…), or integrating a(t) with the initial conditions.
  7. Numerical application at the end, with the unit, and a comment.

1. Free fall

Course p. 7 TD 3 · Ex 1 A parachutist of mass m = 80 kg jumps from a height of 3000 m with zero initial velocity; air resistance is ignored. Axis uy vertical, pointing up, origin on the ground, t = 0 at the jump.

  1. Forces involved: the weight alone. Second law: W = m a, projected: −mg = may, so ay = −g. The mass cancels out: the acceleration does not depend on the mass.

  2. Integrate: vy(t) = −gt (zero initial velocity), then y(t) = −gt²/2 + 3000.

  3. It reaches the ground when y(T) = 0: T = √(6000/g) ≈ 24.7 s with g = 9.81 m·s⁻².

  4. Then vy(T) = −gT ≈ −242.6 m·s⁻¹: a speed of 242.6 m·s⁻¹, about 873 km/h. Unrealistic: the air slows the fall down, which is the rest of the exercise (case 3 below).

Same forces for the rugby ball TD 3 · Ex 2: weight alone, so a = g = (0; −g), and the rest is the projectile of chapter 1. With v0 = 23 m·s⁻¹ and β = 38°, the trajectory equation gives y(40) ≈ 7.36 m: above the crossbar (3 m), below the top of the posts (8 m). The goal is scored.

2. The inclined plane

TD 2 · Ex 2 A robot of mass m stays stationary on a slope of angle α = 30°. Forces: weight, surface reaction force, friction with the surface. The TD axes: ux along the slope, pointing down; uy perpendicular to the slope, pointing up. With these axes, R and f each lie along an axis: only the weight needs to be decomposed.

  1. Decompose the weight along the unit vectors (the French correction says “with a Chasles relation”): W = Wx + Wy. The angle between W and the perpendicular to the slope is α (two angles with perpendicular sides). The side adjacent to α is the perpendicular component, the opposite side the component along the slope:

    W = mg sin α−mg cos α

  2. Equilibrium: W + R + f = 0, that is

    mg sin α−mg cos α + 0R + fx0 = 00

  3. Along x: fx = −mg sin α, so f = −mg sin α ux: pointing up the slope, of magnitude mg sin α. Along y: R = mg cos α.

  4. At 30°: R = (√3/2) mg ≈ 0.87 mg and f = mg/2. With m = 10 kg and g = 10 m·s⁻²: R ≈ 86.6 N and f = 50 N, the values on the plate at the top of the page.

Without friction, the robot slides. Along x: mg sin α = ma, so a = g sin α, that is 5 m·s⁻² at 30° with g = 10 m·s⁻². Along y: it does not leave the slope, ay = 0, and R = mg cos α as before. On the plate, choose “No friction”, then “Horizontal and vertical axes”: you then have to decompose R (and f), and each equation contains two unknowns.

3. With friction: the maximum speed

TD 3 · Ex 1 The parachutist again, this time with air resistance: f = −α v. Axis uy pointing up; write v = vy, negative during the fall.

  1. Second law: W + f = m a, projected: m dv/dt = −mg − αv.

  2. Divide by m: dv/dt + v/τ = −g, with τ = m/α, in seconds. This is a first-order linear differential equation.

  3. Solution: that of the homogeneous equation, A e−t/τ, plus a constant solution, −gτ; then v(0) = 0 gives A = gτ: v(t) = gτ (e−t/τ − 1).

  4. When t becomes large compared with τ, e−t/τ tends to 0: the speed tends to the maximum speed vlim = gτ = mg/α (as a magnitude). The first law confirms it: at constant speed, f balances W, so αvlim = mg.

  5. The TD graph gives vlim ≈ 69.5 m·s⁻¹ (250 km/h), much less than 242.6 m·s⁻¹ without air: “this is expected, since we take the air resistance into account”, says the correction.

Traps
  • On the slope, swapping sin and cos is the most frequent mistake. Test α = 0: the component along the slope must vanish (sin 0 = 0), and the perpendicular component must be −mg.
  • Component or magnitude? With the axis pointing up, vy(T) = −242.6 m·s⁻¹; the speed, a magnitude, is 242.6 m·s⁻¹. The TD 3 correction writes “v(t) = −gt = 242 m/s”: it mixes the two.
  • Likewise, “f = −mg sin α” (TD 2 correction) is the component along ux. The magnitude of the friction is positive: mg sin α.
  • Project with the signs read on the diagram: (0; −mg) when uy points up, (0; +mg) when it points down.
