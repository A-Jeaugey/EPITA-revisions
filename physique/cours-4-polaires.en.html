Part 1

Why polar coordinates

In chapter 1, point M was located by x and y, on a fixed basis (ux, uy). The course says so: this coordinate system is suitable for most cases of motion in a plane, except circular motion.

Take the merry-go-round of TD 5: the wooden horse turns at a constant distance r0 from the centre. In Cartesian coordinates, both of its coordinates oscillate. In polar coordinates, one is constant and the other increases steadily.

In Cartesian coordinates
x(t) = r0 cos(ωt)y(t) = r0 sin(ωt)

Two functions that vary. The circle only shows up once you compute x² + y² = r0².

In polar coordinates
r(t) = r0θ(t) = ωt

The distance from the centre is constant: you read a circle straight away, travelled while always turning at the same rate.

The two polar coordinates of point M (course, chapter VI):

  • r = OM, the distance between the origin O and point M, in metres. It is a distance: the course states that it is always positive.
  • θ, the angle between the vector OM and the axis (O, ux). It counts positively in the anti-clockwise direction.

The plate at the top of the page shows such a point. In “Uniform circular”, drag θ: M travels around the circle and r stays equal to 2 m.

Units

θ is an angle, in radians (dimensionless). Its derivative θ = dθ/dt, the angular velocity, is in rad·s⁻¹. In θ = ωt, ω is therefore in rad·s⁻¹ and r0 in metres: that is question 1 of exercise 2 in TD 5.

In the midterm

This is the chapter that pays the most. In the 7 midterm papers found (November 2017 to October 2022), circular motion comes up in all 7, the velocity in polar coordinates in 6, the acceleration in polar coordinates (general formula or circular case) in all 7. In November 2021, two polar exercises were worth 12 points out of 20: exercise 2 (8 points) and exercise 4 (4 points).

Notation: the past papers write ρ and uρ where the course writes r and ur, and often V for the velocity. They are the same objects.

Trap

θ is measured from the x-axis, anti-clockwise, and in radians in calculations: cos(ωt) only makes sense if ωt is in radians. And r is not x: r = √(x² + y²) (part 8).

Part 2

The polar basis (ur, uθ)

At each point M, you attach a right-handed orthonormal basis (ur, uθ):

  • ur is a unit vector collinear to OM, pointing from O towards M: ur = OM‖OM‖.
  • uθ is a unit vector obtained by rotating ur by +π/2, in the anti-clockwise direction. It is tangent to the circle of centre O through M, pointing towards increasing θ.

This basis is a moving basis: when M turns, ur and uθ turn with it. They depend only on the angle θ. On the plate at the top of the page, the small basis drawn at each exposure changes direction from one exposure to the next; the axes do not move.

To write ur and uθ in the Cartesian basis, bring them back to O: their tips are two points of the unit circle.

ur = cos θ ux + sin θ uy = cos θsin θuθ = −sin θ ux + cos θ uy = −sin θcos θ

Where the minus sign comes from. ur makes the angle θ with ux: its components are cos θ and sin θ. uθ makes the angle θ + π/2: its components are cos(θ + π/2) = −sin θ and sin(θ + π/2) = cos θ.

  1. Two angles to know. θ = 0: ur = ux and uθ = uy. θ = π/2: ur = uy and uθ = −ux (the point is at the top, uθ points to the left). Check them on the figure.

  2. Checking the basis. ur · uθ = cos θ × (−sin θ) + sin θ × cos θ = 0: the two vectors are orthogonal. ‖ur‖² = cos² θ + sin² θ = 1: it is a unit vector, and so is uθ.

In the midterm
  • November 2021, exercise 2, question 1 (1 point): “Express uρ and uθ, the unit vectors of the polar basis, as functions of θ and Cartesian unit vectors ux and uy.”
  • October 2022, exercise 3: “On the diagram, draw the polar basis at point M.” ur starts at M, in line with OM; uθ starts at M, perpendicular, towards increasing θ.
Traps
  • The “−” is on the sine of the component along ux: uθ = (−sin θ; cos θ). The vector (sin θ; −cos θ) is −uθ. Test with θ = 0: you must get uy.
  • On a diagram, the polar basis is drawn at point M, not at O, and it changes with M. Do not draw uθ along the arc that marks the angle near O.

Part 3

Differentiating the basis vectors

In Cartesian coordinates, differentiating a vector meant differentiating its coordinates, because ux and uy are fixed. Here, ur and uθ depend on θ, and θ depends on time: their derivative is not zero. That is what changes everything in this chapter.

The course first differentiates them with respect to θ, starting from their Cartesian expressions (ux and uy, being fixed, behave like constants):

durdθ = −sin θ ux + cos θ uy = uθduθdθ = −cos θ ux − sin θ uy = −ur

Then with respect to time, with the chain rule: ur depends on t through θ(t).

durdt = durdθ · dθdt = θ uθduθdt = duθdθ · dθdt = −θ ur
What the figure shows

When θ increases by Δθ, the tip of ur slides along the circle of radius 1. The difference between the two vectors is almost perpendicular to ur, so almost parallel to uθ, and its length is almost Δθ (an arc of radius 1). In the limit, the difference divided by Δθ becomes exactly uθ.

Remember

Differentiating a rotating unit vector means rotating it by +π/2 and multiplying it by the rotation rate θ. ur rotated by +π/2 gives uθ; uθ rotated by +π/2 gives −ur. Hence θ uθ and −θ ur.

  1. If θ does not change (the point moves back and forth along a straight line through O): θ = 0, so dur/dt = 0. The basis does not turn.

  2. The merry-go-round TD 5 · Ex 2 θ = ωt, so θ = ω, dur/dt = ω uθ and duθ/dt = −ω ur.

  3. The chain rule in an MCQ Midterm 2019-2020 f(t) = A cos²(θ(t)) gives df/dt = 2A cos θ × (−sin θ) × θ = −2Aθ cos θ sin θ. Same mechanism: differentiate with respect to θ, then multiply by θ.

In the midterm
  • Midterms 2019-2020 (MCQ 7) and 2020-2021 (MCQ 2): “The unit vector uρ of polar coordinates satisfies: a) duρ/dt = −θuθ; b) duρ/dt = 0; c) duρ/dt = θuρ; d) duρ/dt = θuθ”. Answer d.
  • November 2021, exercise 2, questions 2 and 3 (3 points): calculate duρ/dθ and duθ/dθ, then express them as functions of uθ and uρ. That is exactly the course calculation above.
Traps
  • dur/dθ = uθ, but dur/dt = θ uθ: with respect to time, do not forget the factor θ.
  • The minus sign is on the derivative of uθ: duθ/dt = −θ ur. The derivative of ur has none.
  • “dur/dt = 0 because ur is a unit vector” is false: its norm stays 1, but its direction changes.

Part 4

The position vector

ur has the direction and the orientation of OM, and the length OM is r. So:

OM = r ur = r0column of components in the basis (ur, uθ)

A single component. The angle θ has not disappeared: it is hidden in ur, which depends on θ. Replacing ur by its Cartesian expression gives x and y back:

OM = r cos θ ux + r sin θ uyx = r cos θ  and  y = r sin θ

  1. The merry-go-round TD 5 · Ex 2 x = r0 cos(ωt) and y = r0 sin(ωt). Then r = √(x² + y²) = r0 √(cos² + sin²) = r0, and θ = ωt. So OM(t) = r0 ur.

  2. The spiral Midterm Nov. 2017 · Ex 2 x = a eωt cos(ωt) and y = a eωt sin(ωt). You recognise r cos θ and r sin θ with r = a eωt and θ = ωt: OM = a eωt ur. The distance from the centre grows: the point moves away from O while turning.

Remember

For circular motion with centre O and radius R: OM = R ur, with R constant. The whole motion is then in ur, that is in θ(t).

In the midterm
  • November 2021, MCQ 2: “In polar coordinates, (uρ, uθ), position vector r(t) = OM(t) has for expression: a. ρuρ + θuθ; b. ρuρ; c. θuρ + ρuθ; d. ρuθ”. Answer b.
  • Same paper, exercise 2, question 4 (2 points): OM in the Cartesian basis (x ux + y uy) and in the polar basis (ρ uρ).
  • October 2022, exercise 3: a point turns on a circle of radius R with θ = ωt, and OM = R ur.
Traps
  • OM = r ur + θ uθ is wrong: θ is an angle, not a length. It is not even dimensionally consistent.
  • The 0 in the column does not mean that M is on an axis: it is the component of OM along uθ, and uθ is always perpendicular to OM.

Part 5

The velocity vector

Same definition as in Cartesian coordinates: v = dOM/dt. The difference: ur depends on time, so you must differentiate it too. Here is the derivation, with the three justifications a marker expects.

  1. v = dOMdt: definition of the instantaneous velocity.

  2. = d(r ur)dt = drdt ur + r durdt: derivative of a product, since r and ur both depend on time.

  3. = r ur + r θ uθ: dur/dt = θ uθ (part 3).

v = r ur + r θ uθ = rrθ‖v‖ = √r² + r²θ²

Two components, two causes. r, the radial component, comes from the change in the distance from O. rθ, the orthoradial component (along uθ), comes from the rotation. Both are in m·s⁻¹: r in m, θ in rad·s⁻¹.

On the plate, in “Uniform circular”, r = 0: v is along uθ, tangent to the circle. In “Spiral”, r increases: v leans outwards, it is no longer along uθ alone.

  1. The merry-go-round TD 5 · Ex 2 r = r0 is constant, so r = 0; θ = ω. Thus v = r0 ω uθ and ‖v‖ = r0 ω: m × rad·s⁻¹ = m·s⁻¹, it is dimensionally consistent (questions 4 and 5).

  2. Check in Cartesian coordinates (question 6): ẋ = −r0 ω sin(ωt) and ẏ = r0 ω cos(ωt), so v = r0 ω (−sin(ωt) ux + cos(ωt) uy) = r0 ω uθ. Same vector, same norm r0 ω; comparing with r0 θ gives θ = ω.

  3. The spiral Midterm Nov. 2017 · Ex 2 r = a eωt gives r = aω eωt; θ = ω. So v = aω eωt (ur + uθ) and ‖v‖ = √2 aω eωt.

In the midterm

The formula comes up in 6 of the 7 papers found: as an MCQ (November 2017, 2018-2019, May 2018, 2020-2021, October 2022) or as a derivation (November 2021, exercise 2, question 5, 2 points: “Detail your calculations”). The 7th paper (2019-2020) asks for it in cylindrical coordinates (part 8). The wrong options in the MCQs: r ur + θ uθ, r ur + rθ uθ, r ur + rθ uθ, or only one of the two components.

Traps
  • Forgetting the derivative of ur gives v = r ur. For a circle, that would be v = 0: a point at rest. Absurd.
  • θ uθ alone is not a velocity: it is in rad·s⁻¹, not m·s⁻¹. You need rθ.
  • The TD 5 statement (exercise 2, question 6) writes v = −r0 sin(ωt) ux + r0 cos(ωt) uy: the factor ω is missing (r0 alone is in metres). The derivative gives −r0 ω sin(ωt) ux + r0 ω cos(ωt) uy.

Part 6

The acceleration vector

Differentiate again: a = dv/dt, with v = r ur + r θ uθ. The first term is a product of two factors that depend on time (r and ur), the second of three (r, θ and uθ): five terms in all.

  1. d(r ur)/dt = r ur + r θ uθ

  2. d(r θ uθ)/dt = r θ uθ + r θ uθ + r θ (−θ ur)

  3. Group what is along ur and what is along uθ. The two terms rθ uθ add up: hence the 2.

a = (r − rθ²) ur + (2rθ + rθ) uθ‖a‖ = √(r − rθ²)² + (2rθ + rθ)²

Radial component: r − rθ². Orthoradial component: 2rθ + rθ. Each term is in m·s⁻²: rθ² in m × (rad·s⁻¹)², rθ in m·s⁻¹ × rad·s⁻¹.

The term −rθ² is negative as soon as the point turns: it points towards O. It exists even when r is constant; it is the term that turns v (part 7).

  1. The midterm spiral Midterm Nov. 2017 · Ex 2 r = a eωt and θ = ωt. Along ur: r − rθ² = aω² eωt − aeωt ω² = 0. Along uθ: 2 × aω eωt × ω + 0. So a = 2aω² eωt uθ and ‖a‖ = 2aω² eωt: no radial component at all.

  2. The plate’s spiral r = r0 + bt and θ = ωt: r = b, r = 0, θ = 0. Then a = −(r0 + bt) ω² ur + 2bω uθ. The term 2rθ is there even though θ = 0: choose “Spiral” on the plate and turn on “components of a”.

In the midterm

The paper often gives the formula and asks you to apply it: 5 papers out of 7 (2018-2019, May 2018, 2019-2020, 2020-2021, November 2021). But in November 2017 (spiral) and October 2022 (exercise 3), you had to calculate the acceleration yourself: know how to derive it. That is question 4 of exercise 1 in TD 5.

Traps
  • The 2 in 2rθ: two identical terms, one in each derivative. If you find only one rθ, the derivative of ur is missing from the first one.
  • The “−” in −rθ² comes from duθ/dt = −θ ur. Without it, the acceleration of a merry-go-round would point outwards.
  • The radial component of a is not the derivative of the radial component of v: r − rθ², not r alone. The basis turns, so you must differentiate the vectors too.

Part 7

Circular motion: uniform or not, centripetal acceleration

The motion is circular with centre O when r stays equal to the radius R. Then r = 0 and r = 0, and the formulas simplify:

v = R θ uθ    ‖v‖ = R |θ|a = −R θ² ur + R θ uθ

v is along uθ: tangent to the circle. a has two parts: −Rθ² ur, pointing towards the centre, which turns v; Rθ uθ, tangent, which changes the norm of v. The scalar product v · a = R² θ θ tells you whether it speeds up (chapter 1, part 4).

Circular…ConditionAcceleration
uniformθ = ω constant, so θ = 0a = −Rω² ur = −v²R ur
acceleratedθ and θ have the same signa = −Rθ² ur + Rθ uθ
deceleratedθ and θ have opposite signsa = −Rθ² ur + Rθ uθ
Constant speed: so why does it accelerate?

“Uniform” refers to the norm of the velocity. In uniform circular motion, ‖v‖ = Rω does not change, but the direction of v turns with uθ. Since v = Rω uθ, a = Rω duθ/dt = −Rω² ur: that is the figure of part 3, with uθ instead of ur. The acceleration points towards the centre: it is centripetal, of norm Rω² = v²/R. On the plate, in “Uniform circular”, a aims at O at every position; in “Accelerated circular”, it leans forward.

Without a calculator: the “Uniform circular” setting of the plate, R = 2 m and ω = 1.5 rad·s⁻¹.

  1. v = Rω = 2 × 1.5 = 3 m·s⁻¹.

  2. a = Rω² = 2 × 2.25 = 4.5 m·s⁻², and v²/R = 9/2 = 4.5 m·s⁻²: both formulas give the same value.

  3. One turn lasts T = 2π/ω = 2π/1.5 ≈ 4.2 s.

  1. The merry-go-round TD 5 · Ex 2 r = r0 and θ = ω are constant: the motion is uniform circular (question 7). a = −r0ω² ur: norm r0ω², pointing towards the centre of the merry-go-round (question 8).

  2. The satellite TD 5 · Ex 3 Going a step further, with Newton’s second law (chapter 2). Circular orbit of radius r = RT + h = 12 800 km around the centre O of the Earth, in the geocentric reference frame, assumed Galilean. Only force, gravity: F = −(GMTm/r²) ur, so a = −(GMT/r²) ur.

    Along uθ: rθ = 0, so θ = ω is constant, the motion is uniform and v = rω. Along ur: rω² = v²/r = GMT/r², hence v = √(GMT/r) ≈ 5.6 × 10³ m·s⁻¹ and a period T = 2πr/v ≈ 1.4 × 10⁴ s, about 4 h. The mass of the satellite plays no part.

In the midterm: 7 papers out of 7
  • November 2021, exercise 4 (4 points): the general formula is given. “Circular”: ρ = R constant, so ρ = ρ = 0 and a = −Rθ² uρ + Rθ uθ. “In addition uniform”: θ constant, θ = 0, a = −Rθ² uρ. Each time: “Justify your answer.”
  • MCQs of May 2018 and 2020-2021, uniform circular: (−Rθ²; 0). MCQs of 2018-2019 (accelerated) and 2019-2020 (decelerated): (−Rθ²; Rθ), the same expression.
  • October 2022: “in uniform circular motion, the acceleration is zero” is false. “For uniform circular motion: θ = constant; θ = 0; θ = 0; ρ = 0”: three right answers (all except θ = 0). In exercise 3, v = Rω uθ and a = −Rω² ur.
  • The circle also comes up in Cartesian coordinates (November 2017, 2018-2019, helices of May 2018 and 2019-2020): ‖v‖ = Rω, ‖a‖ = Rω², and a = −ω² OM when the centre is O.
Traps
  • “Uniform” does not mean a = 0. In uniform circular motion, a = −Rω² ur ≠ 0; only uniform rectilinear motion has zero acceleration.
  • Accelerated or decelerated does not change the expression (−Rθ²; Rθ): only the sign of θ relative to θ changes.
  • a = −ω² OM assumes the centre is at O. In the November 2017 midterm, the circle was centred at C(1; 2): a = −ω² CM.
  • A norm is positive: ‖v‖ = R |θ|, which is not Rθ if the point turns clockwise (θ < 0).

From Cartesian to polar coordinates; cylindrical coordinates

Both systems describe the same point and the same vectors. Going from one to the other is asked in 5 of the 7 papers found.

DirectionCoordinatesBasis vectors
Polar → Cartesianx = r cos θ; y = r sin θur = cos θ ux + sin θ uy
uθ = −sin θ ux + cos θ uy
Cartesian → polarr = √(x² + y²)
cos θ = x/r; sin θ = y/r		ux = cos θ ur − sin θ uθ
uy = sin θ ur + cos θ uθ

The last column of the second row is not in the course: you can check it by replacing ur and uθ by their expressions.

Method: recognise r and θ
  1. If x(t) and y(t) are written f(t) cos(g(t)) and f(t) sin(g(t)), with f > 0, then r = f(t) and θ = g(t).
  2. Otherwise, calculate r = √(x² + y²), simplifying with cos² + sin² = 1.
  3. Write OM = r ur, then differentiate with the formulas of parts 5 and 6.

  1. Polar → Cartesian Midterm 2019-2020 · MCQ 8 ρ(t) = ρ0 eωt and θ(t) = ωt give x = ρ0 eωt cos(ωt) and y = ρ0 eωt sin(ωt). The other options swapped cos and sin, or forgot eωt.

  2. Cartesian → polar TD 5 Midterm Nov. 2017 These are the merry-go-round and the spiral of part 4: recognise r(t) and θ(t), then write OM = r ur.

Cylindrical coordinates

For motion in space, you add the height z. The help sheet on coordinate systems, on Moodle, presents them this way: project M onto the plane (O, x, y) at H, locate H in polar coordinates, then go up by z along uz, which is fixed.

OH = r ur    OM = r ur + z uz = r0z

uz is fixed, its derivative is zero: you add one line to the polar formulas.

v = r ur + rθ uθ + z uza = (r − rθ²) ur + (2rθ + rθ) uθ + z uz

  1. The helix Midterms May 2018 and 2019-2020 x = R cos(ωt), y = R sin(ωt), z = Hωt. In cylindrical coordinates: r = R and θ = ωt, so OM = R ur + Hωt uz; v = Rω uθ + Hω uz, of norm ω√(R² + H²); a = −Rω² ur. A circle travelled uniformly, plus a climb at constant speed.

In the midterm

Cylindrical coordinates came up in 5 of the 7 past papers (helices, a spiral on a cone, MCQs). They are not in the text of this year’s course, only in the help sheet: at least know how to write OM and v. October 2022, MCQ 4: “In cylindrical coordinates, the position vector is: a- ρuρ; b- ρuρ + θuθ; c- ρuρ + θuθ + zuz; d- ρuρ + zuz”. Answer d.

Traps
  • r = √(x² + y²), never x + y. And tan θ = y/x is not enough: θ and θ + π have the same tangent. Look at the signs of x and y.
  • No term along uθ in OM, neither in polar nor in cylindrical coordinates.
  • In cylindrical coordinates, r is the distance OH from the axis (O, z), not the distance OM.
