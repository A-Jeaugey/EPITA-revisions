Part 1

Nodes, branches, loops

WordCourse definition
NodeA point serving as a junction for several conductors: the interconnection of at least 3 wires. With 2 wires, it is a bend, not a junction.
BranchA portion of circuit located between two consecutive nodes. The same current flows through it from start to finish.
Loop
mesh		A set of branches forming a closed contour, which you can traverse passing only once through each intermediate node.

How to count

  1. Color. Everything connected by wire alone gets the same color. One color = one potential. A wire joining two black dots merges them into a single node.
  2. Count the nodes. Every color that touches 3 or more elements is a node.
  3. Count the branches. A branch goes from one node to another through one or more components.
  4. Count the loops. The “windows” of the drawing are the basic loops, and there are branches − nodes + 1 of them. The midterm answer keys count all the loops, including the tours that enclose several windows.

In the midterm

The question “how many nodes, branches and loops?” opens the fundamental laws exercise in 2022, 2023 and 2024. The official answer keys count every loop: 10 loops in 2022 (5 nodes, 8 branches), 6 in 2023 (2 nodes, 4 branches). If in doubt, write both numbers: “3 independent loops, 7 in total”. Practice

Key points
  • Plain wire = same point. Color before you count.
  • Node = 3 wires or more. Branch = from one node to the next. Loop = a closed tour.
  • Independent loops = branches − nodes + 1. Here 5 − 3 + 1 = 3 windows, and 6 loops in total.

Kirchhoff’s current law: nothing is lost KCL

A node is a roundabout. The charges that arrive leave again: nothing builds up, nothing disappears. The course, which calls it the node law, writes it as an algebraic sum: currents entering count +, currents leaving count −, and the total is zero.

Σ Iin = Σ Iout
or, equivalently: Σ ± I = 0 (+ if the arrow arrives, − if it leaves)
Roundabout examplecalculate I₄
Show the answer

Entering: 3 + 1 = 4 A. Leaving: 1.5 + I₄. So I₄ = 4 − 1.5 = 2.5 A. As an algebraic sum: I₁ + I₂ − I₃ − I₄ = 0.

An arrow whose current is negative stays where it is in the equation: you write the law with the arrows, then substitute the values, signs included. Example from the 2017 midterm: with I₄ = −3 mA, a “leaving” current of −3 mA is in fact a current of 3 mA entering.

Recurring MCQ trap

“The incoming current in a generator has a lower intensity than the outgoing one”: false, asked in 2017, 2021 and 2022. A generator raises the potential of the charges; it does not make current. What enters a dipole leaves it, whatever it is.

Key points
  • Node: what comes in = what goes out.
  • You write the law with the arrows, then substitute the values with their signs.

Kirchhoff’s voltage law: a loop brings you back to the same floor KVL

You start at a point, go around a loop and come back to where you started: you are on the same floor as at the beginning. In total you have gone neither up nor down, so the sum of the voltages around the loop is 0. This is the relation UAB = UAC + UCB applied to a complete tour. The course calls it the loop law.

  1. Choose a direction of travel (any one).
  2. For each voltage arrow you meet: in your direction, write +; against you, write −.
  3. Write that the sum equals 0.
Go around the loop step by stepTD1 · Ex 3
…

On the course example (three loops around nodes A and B), the same direction of travel gives three equations: E − U₁ − U₃ − U₂ = 0, then E − U₁ − U₄′ − U₄ − U₂ = 0, and U₃ − U₄′ − U₄ = 0. The third is the difference of the first two: only independent loops bring new information.

The same tour with numbers, on the outer loop of TD1 Ex 2 (which came up in the 2020 midterm). Move one component at a time: the graph shows the floor of each point, and each term of the equation takes you up or down until you are back at the starting point. Look at the pass through R₄: you write +U₄ because the arrow is in your direction, but U₄ = −110 V, so you go down.

In the midterm

Exercise 3 of the 2020 midterm (the same as TD1 Ex 2) gives E, U₁, U₄, U₅ and asks for all the other voltages: nothing but well-chosen loop laws. In 2023 and 2024, exercise 2 asks for one voltage and then another “using Kirchhoff’s voltage law”, with values in kΩ and mA. Practice

Key points
  • Loop: arrow in your direction +, against you −, total 0.
  • On the simple loop: E = U₁ + U₂, the push of the battery is entirely spent in the resistors.
  • A voltage between two points can be calculated along any path that links them: choose the one whose voltages you know.

Series or parallel?

In series

Dipoles are in series if the same current flows through them. They sit one behind the other, with no junction between them, and form a branch.

In parallel

Dipoles are in parallel if the same voltage is applied across them, that is, if their ends are connected to the same two nodes.

The trap the course writes in capitals

Dipoles are not necessarily either in series or in parallel. The only criterion is the strict application of the definitions. If a wire leaves between two resistors, they are not in series. If they do not touch the same two nodes, they are not in parallel. In the course example, R₃ is neither in series nor in parallel with R₁, R₂ or R₄.

Key points
  • Series = same current, no node between the two.
  • Parallel = same voltage, ends on the same two nodes.
  • Neither one nor the other is possible, and common. Apply the definition, not the impression the drawing gives.

Equivalent resistance

The equivalent resistance Req is the single resistor that, put in place of a group of resistors, would carry the same current under the same voltage: u = Req·i.

Series

Req = R1 + R2 + … + Rn

The total is larger than each one. Proof: same current, and Kirchhoff’s voltage law adds up the voltages.

Parallel

1/Req = 1/R1 + … + 1/Rn

The total is smaller than the smallest one. Proof: same voltage, and Kirchhoff’s current law adds up the currents. In conductances: Geq = Σ Gk.

R1 // R2 = R1·R2R1 + R2
“product over sum”, for two resistors only · two equal ones: half · n equal ones: R/n

The method: start from the far end

Start from the end farthest from where you are looking (the source, or terminals A and B) and work back. At each step, merge two resistors into one, then redraw. Here is figure 1 of TD 1 with R₁ = R₂ = 4R, R₃ = R₄ = R, R₅ = 2R.

  • Two equal ones in parallel: half. R // 2R = 2R/3, 3R // 6R = 2R.
  • Simplify from the far end and redraw at each step.

The voltage divider

Resistors in series share the voltage like a pizza: each takes a share proportional to its size. The biggest resistor takes the biggest share.

U2 = E · R2R1 + R2
on top: the resistor whose voltage you want · with n resistors: Uk = E·Rk/ΣR

Where it comes from (this is exactly TD1 Ex 3): same current in both, I = E/(R1 + R2), then U2 = R2·I.

Condition: the resistors must be in series, with no current escaping between them. A voltmeter connected in the middle does not count (it draws no current); a load connected in the middle does: you must first group it in parallel with the bottom resistor.

Worked example: the Mars rover TD1 · Ex 3

  1. Setup: R₁ and R₂ in series under E = 12 V, and we want U₂ = 3.3 V for the sensor.
  2. Kirchhoff’s voltage law: R₁ takes the rest, U₁ = 12 − 3.3 = 8.7 V.
  3. Same current in both, so the voltages follow the resistances: R₂/R₁ = U₂/U₁ = 3.3/8.7 = 11/29 ≈ 0.38.
  4. Check with the formula: 12 × 3.3/(8.7 + 3.3) = 3.3 V. For example R₁ = 8.7 kΩ and R₂ = 3.3 kΩ.
Key points
  • Series → voltage divider: U2 = E·R2/(R1+R2).
  • Nothing must escape between the two resistors.

The current divider

Resistors in parallel share the current. The current prefers the easy path: the smallest resistor gets the biggest current.

I2 = I · R1R1 + R2
on top: the other resistor · with n branches, in conductances: Ik = I·Gk/ΣG

Where it comes from (TD1 Ex 4): same voltage, U = I·R1R2/(R1 + R2), then I2 = U/R2: the R2 cancels and the other resistor is left on top.

Trap

The two formulas look alike but are not read the same way. Voltage: the same resistor on top. Current: the other resistor on top. If in doubt, check with intuition: the biggest resistor takes more voltage but less current.

In the midterm

Dividers are everywhere: in the fundamental laws exercise (2020: real generator and dividers, result 3.33 mA) and above all at the end of the Thévenin exercises, where you are asked for the voltage U across the 2R load: U = Eth·2R/(Rth + 2R). Practice

Key points
  • Parallel → current divider: I2 = I·R1/(R1+R2).
  • The small resistor takes the big current.

Solving a circuit: the method

  1. Color the nodes (plain wire = same point) and name them. Choose a ground, preferably at the bottom of the source.
  2. Replace the special cases: switches, voltmeter, ammeter. Redraw if needed.
  3. Simplify series and parallel, from the far end toward the source, down to a single resistor Req.
  4. Calculate the total current: I = E/Req. With a current source: U = Req·I.
  5. Work back down to what you are asked for, with the dividers or Ohm’s law on a piece of the circuit.
  6. Check: the units, the sign (with the direction of the arrows), and one Kirchhoff’s current or voltage law.

Worked example: a current source in the circuit TD1 · Ex 9

E = 15 V, I₁ = 0.1 A, R₁ = 200 Ω, R₂ = 100 Ω. We look for U and I.

  1. The three branches are between the same two wires: each has the voltage E = 15 V across it.
  2. In the middle branch, the source sets I₁ = 0.1 A, which goes up (read the direction of the arrow on the diagram). It flows through R₁ from bottom to top: R₁ has R₁I₁ = 20 V across it, and its bottom side is the higher one in potential.
  3. Loop in this branch, from the bottom wire to the top wire: you go down 20 V in R₁, then up U in the source. E = U − 20, so U = 15 + 20 = 35 V. U and I₁ are arrowed in the same direction and U > 0: the current source supplies 35 × 0.1 = 3.5 W.
  4. Top node: I and I₁ arrive, E/R₂ leaves through R₂. I + I₁ = E/R₂, so I = 0.15 − 0.1 = 0.05 A.
  5. Trap: if you read I₁ as pointing down, you would find −5 V and 0.25 A. The direction of the arrows is part of the problem statement.
