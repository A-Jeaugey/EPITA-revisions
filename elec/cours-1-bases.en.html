Part 1

The 4 quantities

An electrical circuit is a set of components connected by conducting wires (usually copper). Current flows only if the loop is closed: the battery pushes the water, the water goes all the way around and returns to the battery. Four quantities are enough to describe what happens.

Iampere (A)
intensity

Current

The flow rate of charges: how many coulombs pass through the wire each second. i = dq/dt, and 1 A = 1 C/s. It flows through components. The arrow goes on the wire.

Phone charger: about 2 A.

Uvolt (V)
p.d.

Voltage

The height difference between two points, or potential difference. It is what makes the water flow. It is measured between two points. The arrow goes next to the component. 1 V = 1 J/C.

AA cell: 1.5 V. Car battery: 12 V.

Rohm (Ω)
resistor

Resistance

A narrowing of the pipe: it slows the current down. The larger R is, the less current gets through for the same voltage.

1 kΩ = 1,000 Ω.

Pwatt (W)

Power

The energy exchanged each second: P = U·I. A resistor turns it into heat (Joule effect).

12 V × 2 A = 24 W.

Potential: the floor of a point

Every point of the circuit is on a floor: its potential V, in volts. We choose one reference point at 0 V, the ground floor: this is the ground. The voltage between two points is the difference between their floors.

UAB = VA − VB
the voltage between A and B = the floor of A minus the floor of B
Vocabulary trap

The course insists: ground ≠ earth. The earth is the point of zero potential (the soil). The ground is the point you choose as the reference in your circuit. Also, the conventional direction of current is the opposite of the real direction of the electrons (which are negatively charged): this is historical, and we keep the convention.

Key points
  • Current flows through a component. Voltage is measured between two points.
  • A voltage is a difference of potentials. A single point has no voltage, it has a potential.
  • Units: A = C/s, V = J/C, W = V·A, Ω = V/A.

Part 2

Reading a diagram

A diagram is a plumbing plan. A component with two terminals is called a dipole: resistor, battery, switch… Any dipole can be drawn as a simple rectangle. Here are all the symbols of the course, with what they become in a calculation.

Wire

A perfect pipe: it slows nothing down. Two points joined by a plain wire are the same point, at the same potential.

Node

A point where at least 3 wires meet: a crossroads.

Resistor

Slows the current down. Its voltage is U = R·I.

Voltage source

The line follows the wire. It sets a voltage E, whatever the current. Example: a battery.

Current source

The line cuts across the wire. It sets a current I. Its voltage adapts to the rest of the circuit.

Closed switch K

Replaced by a wire. Zero voltage across it.

Open switch

Replaced by a gap. No current, but a voltage can appear across it.

Voltmeter

Connected in parallel, between two points. In a calculation: a gap (infinite resistance).

Ammeter

Connected in series, in the wire. In a calculation: a wire (zero resistance).

Ground

The point you choose as the reference: 0 V. All the ground symbols of a diagram are connected to each other.

Trap

Voltage source or current source? Erase the circle in your head. If a continuous wire is left (the line runs along the wire), it is a voltage source. If a gap is left (the line cuts across the wire), it is a current source. Your course uses exactly this idea to “turn off” a source in chapter 3.

Part 3

Arrows: where it all happens

Most mistakes come from arrows. There are two kinds, and they do not mean the same thing.

The voltage arrow points to the point written first

UAB = VA − VB: the arrowhead is on A (“arrowhead – back of arrow”, as the course puts it). If the result is positive, A is higher than B. If it is negative, A is lower. This is not an error: the arrow only shows which calculation we are doing.

The building of potentialsmove A and B
10 V
6 V

Voltages chain like floors

To go from A to C, you can go through B: UAC = UAB + UBC. This is the “Chasles relation” for voltages, and it is the basis of Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) in chapter 2. Also, reversing the arrow changes the sign: UBA = −UAB.

The current arrow is a bet

You choose the direction of the arrow I at random, then you calculate. Positive result: the current goes in the direction of your arrow. Negative result: it goes the other way. In a resistor, the real current always goes down from the high floor to the low floor, like water.

In the midterm

The midterm of Nov. 2020 (Ex 3, the same circuit as your TD 1 Ex 2) gave voltages like U4 = VC − VD = −110 V and asked you to place the points A to F on the figure, then to calculate the other voltages by chaining. In Oct. 2022, exercise 3 required many arrows and gave negative results (U₅ = −2.5 V, I₅ = −5 mA). Practice

Key points
  • Arrowhead of the U arrow = the point written first.
  • UAC = UAB + UBC and UBA = −UAB.
  • A negative result just means “the other way”.

Part 4

Dipoles and sign conventions

A dipole has two terminals. The current that enters by one terminal comes out entirely by the other: a dipole does not keep any current, not even a source. The course sorts dipoles by three criteria.

CriterionDefinition from the courseExamples
Linear / non-linearLinear if the relationship between U and I does not depend on the current (constant impedance).Resistor, capacitor, coil: linear. Diode, transistor: not.
Passive / activePassive if its characteristic passes through the origin: U = 0 ⇔ I = 0. It can only consume (Joule effect).Resistor: passive. Cell, battery, motor: active.
Receiver / sourceSource if it supplies electrical energy, receiver if it consumes it.A battery is a source when it powers your phone, a receiver when you recharge it.

The two conventions

On a given dipole, we compare the direction of the U arrow with that of the I arrow. A convention is a choice of arrows, not a property of the dipole.

Receiver convention

Arrows in opposite directions. This is the normal setting for a resistor: U = R·I.

P = U·I is then the power received. If U and I have the same sign, the dipole is indeed a receiver.

Source convention

Arrows in the same direction. Normal for a battery, which pushes the current toward its +. On a resistor, you then have to write U = −R·I.

P = U·I is then the power supplied. Same sign: the dipole is indeed a source.

Ohm and the direction of the arrowsflip the I arrow
Advice from the course

In a circuit with a single source, draw it in source convention and all the resistors in receiver convention: all your results will come out positive. With two sources pushing against each other, you cannot satisfy everyone: choose a direction at random and read the sign at the end.

Key points
  • Receiver: opposite arrows, U = R·I, P = UI received.
  • Source: arrows in the same direction, P = UI supplied; on a resistor, U = −R·I.
  • The current that enters a dipole comes out in full, sources included.

Ohm’s law and power

U = R · I
Ohm’s law · in receiver convention · volts = ohms × amperes

Not all materials conduct equally well: this is the resistive effect. A resistor is a passive dipole whose current is proportional to the voltage. Its characteristic I = f(U) is a straight line through the origin. You get the three versions by isolating the letter you are looking for:

I am looking for the voltageU = R·I4 Ω with 3 A through it → 12 V
I am looking for the currentI = U / R12 V across 4 Ω → 3 A
I am looking for the resistanceR = U / I12 V and 3 A → 4 Ω

Prefixes, and a trick

PrefixMeansExample
µ micro× 10⁻⁶5 µA = 0.000005 A
m milli× 10⁻³2 mA = 0.002 A
k kilo× 10³4.7 kΩ = 4,700 Ω
M mega× 10⁶1 MΩ = 1,000,000 Ω

Trick: kΩ × mA gives volts directly, because the kilo and the milli cancel out: 1 kΩ × 2 mA = 2 V. In the same way V / kΩ gives mA: 12 V / 4 kΩ = 3 mA. The numerical exercises in the midterms are almost always in kΩ and mA Midterms 2023 and 2024 · Ex 2.

Power and the Joule effect

P = U·I. With Ohm’s law, for a resistor: P = R·I² = U²/R. A resistor turns all this power into heat. The inverse of the resistance is the conductance G = 1/R, in siemens (S): “how well it lets current through”.

Trap

In the MCQ, the distractors play on units and powers of 10: “100 V / 0.1 A” gives 1 kΩ (dividing by 0.1 is multiplying by 10), not 10 Ω. And an option with the right number but the wrong unit (“1,000 A” for a voltage) is wrong Midterm 2021 · Ex 1.

Key points
  • U = R·I in receiver convention, with a minus sign otherwise.
  • P = UI = RI² = U²/R and G = 1/R.
  • kΩ × mA = V, V / kΩ = mA.

Part 7

Sources, ideal and real

A source can send current into a circuit. An ideal source does so without any loss. There are two types, and each one sets a single quantity.

Ideal voltage sourceu = E for every i

The voltage does not depend on the current supplied: horizontal characteristic. Cell, battery, mains.

Ideal current sourcei = I for every u

The current does not depend on the voltage: vertical characteristic. Example from the course: a battery charger.

Turning off a source: remove the circle

For the theorems of chapter 3, you will need to cancel (turn off) sources. The symbols are made for that: remove the circle and look at what is left.

  • Voltage source turned off: E = 0, its two terminals are at the same potential. What is left is a wire: you short-circuit it.
  • Current source turned off: I = 0, nothing flows in its branch any more. What is left is a gap: you remove the whole branch.

Real sources

A real battery heats up a little: there are Joule losses in the wires. We model them with an internal resistance r.

Real voltage source

E in series with r. The output voltage drops when you draw current:

u = E − r·i

Better when r is small. If the load R ≫ r, you can treat it as ideal.

Real current source

I in parallel with r. Part of the current leaks through r:

i = I − u/r

Better when r is large. If the load R ≪ r, you can treat it as ideal.

A real source supplying a load Rvoltage source
10 Ω

In the midterm

TD1 Ex 5 is the typical exercise: “the generator (E, R) gives a voltage level U = 2 V if RC = 10 Ω and it doubles the voltage level if RC = 25 Ω”. Two situations, two voltage divider equations, two unknowns: E = 12 V and R = 50 Ω. Practice

Key points
  • Voltage source: sets U; its current depends on the circuit. Current source: sets I; its voltage depends on the circuit.
  • Turn off: voltage → wire, current → gap.
  • Real: voltage source = E + r in series; current source = I + r in parallel.

Part 8

Calculating without a calculator

The midterm is taken without a calculator, and almost everything is symbolic (“in terms of E and R”). TD 0 trains the calculation skills you need. Here they are, with examples taken from the TDs and the exams.

Fractions

Common denominator first: a/b + c/d = (ad + cb)/(bd). Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its inverse.

1/R + 1/(2R) = 2/(2R) + 1/(2R) = 3/(2R)
so the resistance is 2R/3

Isolating an unknown

What you do on one side of the “=”, you do on the other. Cross-multiply to get rid of fractions.

3.3·(R₁ + R₂) = 12·R₂
3.3·R₁ = 8.7·R₂
R₂/R₁ = 3.3/8.7 ≈ 0.38

System of two equations

Substitution: isolate one unknown in one equation, replace it in the other. Or combination: add the equations to eliminate one.

2R + 20 = 10E and 4R + 100 = 25E
(1) gives R = 5E − 10, in (2):
20E − 40 + 100 = 25E ⇒ E = 12 V, R = 50 Ω

Checking the units dimensional consistency

Both sides of the “=” have the same unit, and we only add things with the same unit. Building blocks: V = Ω × A and W = V × A.

✓ U = RI + E : V + V
✓ P = U²/R : V²/Ω = V·A = W
✗ I = E/R + E : A + V, impossible

Reflexes that save time

CalculationReflex

R // 2R2R²/3R = 2R/3
3R // 6R18R²/9R = 2R

Dividing by 0.1 or 0.5multiply by 10 or by 2
Symbolic resultcheck the unit, then a limiting case (R → 0, R → ∞) that must make sense
Key points
  • Two unknowns need two equations: in electronics, they often come from two situations (two measurements, two settings).
  • Before handing in a formula, check the units: it is the fastest check there is.
