Part 1

A circuit seen from two terminals

Take any circuit made of sources and resistors, and keep just two terminals A and B to connect something to (a “load”). Seen from A and B, this circuit behaves like a single source and a single resistor. Thévenin’s and Norton’s theorems tell you which ones.

You have already done this without knowing it in the TD 1 black box (Ex 6): a voltmeter between A and B measures the no-load voltage (4 V), an ammeter between A and B measures the short-circuit current (0.5 A). These two measurements are enough to predict everything for any load.

The two measurements

No load (nothing between A and B): UAB = UV, the no-load voltage.

Short-circuited (a wire between A and B): the current ICC.

What you get from them

Eth = UV, IN = ICC and Rth = UV/ICC.

TD 1 black box: Rth = 4/0.5 = 8 Ω. And indeed R₁ // R₂ = 12 // 24 = 8 Ω.

Why it is useful

When you are asked for the voltage across a single resistor in a big circuit, you do not need to solve the whole circuit. You replace everything else with its equivalent, and only one loop is left: a voltage divider.

Key points
  • Equivalent = same voltage and same current in the load, whatever the load.
  • No-load voltage → Eth. Short-circuit current → IN. Their ratio → Rth.

Part 2

Turning off the sources to find Rth

Rth is the resistance seen between A and B when the circuit is made passive: you cancel all the energy sources. The course says it with the symbols: remove the circle.

Voltage source turned off

E = 0: its terminals are at the same potential. You replace it with a wire (you short-circuit it).

Current source turned off

I = 0: nothing flows any more. You replace it with a gap: you remove its branch.

Turn the sources off one by onelook at what is left between A and B
The two mistakes that cost the most
  • Short-circuiting a current source (instead of opening it): Rth comes out wrong, and so does everything after it.
  • Keeping the load (the 2R between A and B) in the calculation of Rth: always disconnect it first.
Key points
  • Voltage → wire. Current → gap.
  • Rth = resistance seen from A and B, load disconnected, sources turned off.

Part 3

Thévenin’s theorem

Statement from the course: any complex source dipole can be replaced, between two terminals A and B, by a Thévenin source: an ideal voltage source Eth in series with a resistor Rth, such that:

  • Eth is the voltage that appears between A and B when they are not connected: the no-load voltage UV.
  • Rth is the resistance seen from A and B when the dipole has been made passive (sources turned off).

The Thévenin model, loaded by the 2R of the midterm papers. The arrow Eth points toward A.

  1. Disconnect the load between A and B.
  2. Eth = voltage between A and B with no load (voltage divider, KVL, Millman…).
  3. Rth = resistance seen from A and B, sources turned off.
  4. Reconnect the load: only one loop is left, U = Eth·RL/(Rth + RL).

Example: the voltage divider bridge seen from its output

The circuit in the header: E = 12 V, R₁ = 6 Ω at the top, R₂ = 12 Ω between A and B.

  1. With no load, no current leaves through A: R₁ and R₂ are in series, voltage divider: Eth = 12 × 12/18 = 8 V.
  2. E turned off becomes a wire: seen from A and B, R₁ and R₂ are in parallel: Rth = 6 × 12/18 = 4 Ω.
  3. With a 4 Ω load: U = 8 × 4/(4 + 4) = 4 V. Check it on the simulator in the header.
Trap

With no load, R₁ and R₂ are in series (same current, nothing leaves through A). With the sources turned off and seen from A and B, they are in parallel. The same resistors change roles depending on where you look from: redraw the circuit at every step.

Key points
  • Eth = no-load voltage; Rth = resistance seen, sources turned off.
  • Then a single voltage divider with the load.

Part 4

Norton’s theorem

Statement from the course: the same dipole can also be replaced by a Norton source: an ideal current source IN in parallel with a resistor RN, such that:

  • IN is the current that flows between A and B when you connect them with a wire: the short-circuit current ICC.
  • RN is the resistance seen from A and B, sources turned off: it is the same as Rth.

The Norton model loaded by RL. The arrow of IN points toward A.

With the load, it is a current divider: I = IN·RN/(RN + RL), and U = (RN // RL)·IN.

On the voltage divider bridge of the example: a wire between A and B short-circuits R₂, so IN = 12/6 = 2 A, and RN = 4 Ω. You get Eth = RN·IN = 8 V back.

Eth = RN·IN    IN = Eth/Rth    Rth = RN
the three conversion formulas from the course
Going further (course)

In theory the two models are equivalent. In practice, Thévenin describes well a source whose internal resistance is very low compared with the load (the voltage stays almost Eth, like an operational amplifier). Norton describes well a source whose internal resistance is very high (the current stays almost IN, like a transistor).

Key points
  • IN = short-circuit current between A and B; RN = Rth.
  • Eth = R·IN and IN = Eth/R.

Thévenin ⇄ Norton: the source transformation

This is the tool of the midterms. Any branch “voltage source E in series with R” can be replaced by “current source E/R in parallel with the same R”, and the other way round. You do not calculate anything new: you redraw, so that simple things to combine appear.

The sign trap

The arrow of the Norton current points toward the + terminal of the voltage source, that is, in the same direction as the arrow of E. The other way round: the arrow of the Thévenin emf points in the direction of the current arrow. In 2024, the 2E source had its arrow pointing down: reading it backward gave Eth = 2(RI + E) instead of 2(RI − E).

Key points
  • (E in series with R) ⇄ (E/R in parallel with R), same R.
  • The current arrow points toward the +; the emf arrow points in the direction of the current.
  • Combine current sources in parallel, voltage sources in series.

The method of chained transformations

This is exercise 3 of the November 2023 midterm, question 1: find Eth and Rth seen by the 2R load, then the voltage U. Go step by step: each diagram is the one the grader expects on your paper (“justify with intermediate diagrams”).

Midterm Nov. 2023 · Ex 3 · Q111.5 points
0 / 6

The recipe, valid for every paper

  1. Disconnect the load and locate A and B.
  2. Look at the shape of the branches. Branches in parallel between A and B → turn them into Norton to add the currents. Elements one after the other on the same path → turn them into Thévenin to add the voltages.
  3. A “current source // R” block sitting on a line (the pattern of the 2023 and 2024 midterms): it is a Norton. Turn it into a Thévenin R·I in series with R, arrow in the direction of I.
  4. Combine, transform again, until you have a single source and a single resistor.
  5. Reconnect the load and apply the voltage divider.
  6. Check Rth by turning off the sources on the starting diagram, and the unit of each term (RI and E are volts).
In the midterm

Question 2 of the same exercise, and the two questions of 2024: same 2R load, answers of the form (E + 4RI)/3, 4(RI − E)/7 or (E − 8RI)/6. They are all solved step by step in the practice section. Practice

Key points
  • Parallel → Norton (you add currents). Series → Thévenin (you add voltages).
  • One intermediate diagram per transformation, with the arrows.

Part 7

Millman’s theorem

Millman’s theorem is Kirchhoff’s current law written with potentials. It gives you directly the potential of a node A where several branches arrive, all the potentials being measured relative to one common reference M (the ground, 0 V). The course proves it by writing the current of each branch (Vk − VA + Ek)/Rk, then Kirchhoff’s current law at A.

VA = Σ (Vk ± Ek)/Rk + Σ Ientering − Σ IleavingΣ 1/Rk
top: what each branch “pushes” toward A · bottom: the sum of the conductances
  1. Choose the ground: the point common to all the sources, often the bottom wire.
  2. For each branch that reaches A through a resistance Rk: in the numerator, the potential of the other end (plus the emf of the branch, + if its arrow points toward A, − otherwise), divided by Rk; in the denominator, 1/Rk.
  3. Current sources that reach A: in the numerator only (+ if they enter, − if they leave). A current source in series in a branch sets the current of that branch: the resistor in series with it does not count in the denominator.

The example from the course

Ground = bottom wire. Three branches reach A: (E₁, R₁), (E₃, R₃) and (I₂ // R₂). The source I₂ leaves A and E₃ has its arrow pointing down, hence the minus signs:

U = E₁/R₁ − E₃/R₃ − I₂1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃

Multiplying top and bottom by R₁R₂R₃: U = R₂(E₁R₃ − E₃R₁ − I₂R₁R₃)/(R₂R₃ + R₁R₂ + R₁R₃). A single formula, where Thévenin or superposition would take three calculations.

In the midterm

Millman is on the syllabus but was not asked explicitly in the 2017 to 2024 papers. It remains the fastest way to check a no-load voltage Eth or a final result: in 2024 Q2, Millman at A gives U = (E − 8RI)/6 again in one line.

Key points
  • VA = (Σ “what each branch pushes”/Rk + entering currents) / Σ 1/Rk.
  • Sign + if the emf or the current goes toward A, − otherwise. A branch with a current source appears only in the numerator.

Part 8

Superposition, and which method to choose

The course cites the superposition theorem: in a linear circuit with several sources, a voltage (or a current) is the sum of the contributions of each source acting alone, the others being turned off (voltage → wire, current → gap). It came up once (2017, 2.5 points) and it is an excellent way to check a result.

Example, 2024 Q2: with E alone (I open), you get E/6; with I alone (E as a wire), I leaves A and flows through 2R // 4R = 4R/3, hence −4RI/3. Total: (E − 8RI)/6, the same result as with the transformations.

The bench below takes the circuit of the 2017 midterm (Ex 4) with numerical values. Change E₁ and I₃: each single-source diagram gives its share of I₁, and the sum of the two shares always lands on the current of the full circuit. The arrow I₁ stays the same in all three diagrams: with I₃ alone, the red band shows the current flowing through R₁ against this arrow, hence the minus sign.

The traps found in the 2023 and 2024 answer keys
  • Including the 2R load in Eth or Rth.
  • Short-circuiting a current source to calculate Rth.
  • Reading a source arrow backward (2E with its arrow pointing down in 2024) or forgetting that a current source draws current from node A.
  • Forgetting the resistor R on the bottom wire (2024 Q2: Rth = 4R, not 3R).
  • Thinking that the bottom node of the left part is B when an “I // R” block separates them.
  • 6R // 6R = 3R, not 12R.
