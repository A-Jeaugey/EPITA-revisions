Method 1

Count nodes, branches and loops

  1. Color each piece of wire: everything joined by wire alone is a single point, even if it goes all the way around the drawing.
  2. Nodes: the colors that touch at least 3 elements. A black dot with 2 wires is not a node.
  3. Branches: the paths from one node to the next. Dipoles one after the other with no node between them form a single branch.
  4. Loops: b − n + 1 independent loops, but the answer keys count all the loops. List them, naming each one by its dipoles.
Quick example · midterm Nov. 2023

4 branches in parallel between A and B (source I, E + 2R, 2R, R + E + R): 2 nodes, 4 branches, 6 loops (every pair of branches, 3 of them independent).

If in doubt, write both numbers: “3 independent loops, 6 in total”.

Method 2

Draw the arrows, then chain the voltages

  1. Resistors in receiver convention: voltage arrow opposite to the current arrow. Then U = RI.
  2. Generator in source convention: arrows in the same direction.
  3. An unknown voltage can be found along any path whose voltages you know: UAC = UAB + UBC, and reversing an arrow changes the sign.
  4. Placing points given by U = VX − VY: X is at the arrowhead, Y at the back of the arrow.
Quick example · TD 1 Ex 2 and midterm 2020

E = VA − VB = 240 V and U₁ = VF − VB = 184 V. Then U₂ = VF − VA = 184 − 240 = −56 V. The negative sign is the right answer, not a mistake.

Method 3

The numerical “fundamental laws” exercise

The 2023 and 2024 format: a few currents in mA, a voltage in V, resistances in kΩ, and chained questions. Each answer feeds the next one.

  1. Copy the data onto the diagram: write each value next to its component.
  2. Look for the direct link: a resistor for which you know two of the three quantities (R, U, I) → Ohm’s law.
  3. Unknown current → KCL at the node where all the other currents are known.
  4. Unknown voltage → KVL on the loop where all the other voltages are known.
  5. Unknown resistance → R = U/I with the two values you found (V/mA = kΩ).
  6. Keep the signs: a current of −5 mA means 5 mA in the other direction; do not reverse the arrow, write the sign.
In the 2024 midterm

Expected results: R₃ = 2 kΩ, I₂ = −5 mA, U₂ = −10 V, U₄ = 2 V. The negative current is deliberate. The solved exercise

Method 4

Reduce a resistor network

  1. Name the nodes (A, B, then N1, N2…) and redraw the network if the wires cross: each resistor connects two nodes.
  2. Spot the obvious pairs: two resistors between the same two nodes (parallel) or one after the other with no node between them (series).
  3. Merge from the end farthest from A and B, one pair at a time, and redraw after each merge.
  4. Repeat until a single resistor remains between A and B.
  5. Reverse question (“what must be R′ value?”): express Req in terms of R′, write the required equality, cross-multiply.
Quick example · midterm 2021

Two 2R between A and N1: R. Two 4R between N2 and B: 2R. Then (R + 2R) // 3R = 3R/2, + 2R = 7R/2, and finally // 3R (top branch):

(7R/2 · 3R)/(7R/2 + 3R) = 21R/13 (multiply top and bottom by 2)

Method 5

Apply a divider without mistakes

Voltage divider

Condition: resistors in series, nothing leaks out between them (a voltmeter does not count, a load does).

Uk = E·Rk/ΣR

The same resistor on top. If a load is connected in the middle, first put it in parallel with the bottom resistor.

Current divider

Condition: two branches in parallel fed by a known current.

I1 = I·R2/(R1 + R2)

The other resistor on top. With more than two branches, switch to conductances: Ik = I·Gk/ΣG.

Sanity check

The largest resistor takes the largest voltage and the smallest current. If your result says otherwise, you put the wrong resistor on top.

Method 6

The table of the 4 switch cases

  1. Draw a table K₁ × K₂ (open / closed): 4 cells, 4 diagrams.
  2. For each cell, redraw: closed = wire (it short-circuits whatever is in parallel with it), open = gap (its branch disappears).
  3. Calculate on the simplified circuit: often a single voltage or current divider.
  4. “Open loop” case: no current, so no voltage across the resistors; the open switch takes the whole voltage (U = E). With a current source, opening its only loop is not possible: look for where the current can still flow.
In the midterm

Came up in 2017 (voltage source, answers in terms of E) and in 2018 (current source, answers in terms of I), for 4.5 to 6 points. Same principle as TD 1 Ex 7. Practice

Method 7

Direct Thévenin

  1. Disconnect the load and locate A and B.
  2. Eth = no-load voltage between A and B: voltage divider, KVL or Millman. No current leaves through A.
  3. Rth: turn off the sources (voltage → wire, current → gap), then the resistance seen from A and B.
  4. Reconnect the load: U = Eth·RL/(Rth + RL).
Quick example

Eth = 12 × 6/(3 + 6) = 8 V; Rth = 3 // 6 = 2 kΩ; with RL = 2 kΩ: U = 8 × 2/4 = 4 V.

Method 8

Chained Thévenin ⇄ Norton transformations

The method for the 11-point exercises. The grader expects one intermediate diagram per transformation.

  1. Disconnect the 2R load. Everything left is the “generator” to simplify.
  2. Branches in parallel between the same two nodes: turn each series (E, R) into a Norton (E/R // R, arrow toward the +), then add the current sources (algebraically) and combine the resistors in parallel.
  3. Elements one after the other on a path: turn each (I // R) into a Thévenin (RI in series with R, emf in the direction of I), then add the emfs (algebraically, from B to A) and the resistors.
  4. Block “(E, R) loaded by a resistor”: intermediate Thévenin, E·R₂/(R₁ + R₂) in series with R₁ // R₂ (2024 Q2: E/2 and 2R).
  5. A single source and a single resistor: that is (Eth, Rth). Reconnect 2R, voltage divider.
  6. Check Rth on the original circuit with the sources turned off, and the units of Eth (volts everywhere, RI is in V).

The arrow rule

Norton: the current arrow points toward the + terminal of the voltage source it replaces. Thévenin: the emf arrow points in the direction of the current arrow it replaces. A current source that leaves node A counts as a minus.

Method 9

Millman in three steps

  1. Ground at the point the sources share (often the bottom wire). Name the node A you are looking for.
  2. Numerator: for each branch reaching A, (potential of the other end ± emf)/R, + if the emf points toward A; plus the currents of the current sources (+ entering, − leaving).
  3. Denominator: sum of the 1/R of the branches with a resistor (not the branches with a current source in series). Multiply top and bottom to keep a single fraction bar.
Quick example

Branches (12 V, 2 Ω) and (6 V, 2 Ω) toward A, and 4 Ω from A to ground:

VA = (12/2 + 6/2)/(1/2 + 1/2 + 1/4) = 9/(5/4) = 7.2 V

Method 10

Check without a calculator

Units

Every term of a sum has the same unit: RI and E are volts, E/R and I are amperes. A result U = E + I is certainly wrong.

Limiting cases

Set I = 0 or E = 0 and check that the result matches the simplified circuit. A resistance tending to 0 (wire) or to infinity (gap) must give a sensible result.

Rth twice

Once through the transformations, once by turning off the sources on the original diagram. If the two differ, an arrow or a grouping is wrong.

Another method

Superposition (one source at a time) or Millman at node A: in 2024 Q2, both give (E − 8RI)/6 again in a few lines.

To avoid

The 15 traps found in the papers

  1. Counting 4 loops instead of 10The 2022 and 2023 answer keys count every loop, not just the independent ones.
  2. Taking a bend for a nodeA point with two wires is not a node; dipoles separated by a bend are in series.
  3. Misreading a resistor networkIn 2018, the 2017 network comes back with a single connection moved: 27R/13 instead of 2R. Name every node before reducing.
  4. Automatically classing two resistors as in series or in parallelThe course insists: they can be neither. Apply the definition.
  5. Forgetting the sign of a result−56 V, −5 mA, −1.84 A are the right answers when the arrow points the other way.
  6. Writing U = RI in source conventionArrows in the same direction on a resistor: U = −RI.
  7. Believing that a generator “adds” currentThe current that enters a dipole comes out of it, generators included. Trick question in 2017, 2021 and 2022.
  8. Connecting the ammeter in parallelAmmeter in series (a wire), voltmeter in parallel (a gap).
  9. Putting the wrong resistor on top of the dividerVoltage: the same one. Current: the other one.
  10. Keeping the 2R load in Eth or RthDisconnect it first, reconnect it at the end.
  11. Short-circuiting a current source for RthCurrent source turned off = gap.
  12. Reading a source arrow backward2024: the 2E points downward, hence 2(RI − E) and not 2(RI + E).
  13. Forgetting that a current source draws current2024 Q2: I leaves A, so it is subtracted: E/(8R) − I.
  14. Forgetting a resistor on the bottom wire2024 Q2: Rth = 2R + R + R = 4R.
  15. Adding resistors in parallel6R // 6R = 3R, not 12R (2023 Q2).
Exam day

Writing your paper

The papers say “Justify your answers” and you answer on the exam sheet itself. What earns points:

  • One diagram per step for the equivalents, with the arrows and the values.
  • The law you use, named: “KVL on loop …”, “voltage divider”, “Norton → Thévenin”.
  • The symbolic expression first, then the numerical value with its unit.
  • The result boxed, simplified (a single fraction bar).
  • The MCQ has no negative points: answer everything.
  • Time: the paper gives no duration; aim for about 5 minutes on the MCQ, 15 on the fundamental laws and the rest on Thévenin/Norton.
