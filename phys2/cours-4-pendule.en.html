The pendulum swings, friction stops it.

For small angles, a simple pendulum obeys the same equation as a mass on a spring. You get it from the second law in the polar basis or from energy: that is TD 7. Then we add fluid friction: the oscillations die out, or disappear altogether. This is the end of part 2 of the course (pages 18 and 19).

Part 1

The simple pendulum: the second law in the polar basis

A mass m, treated as the point M, hangs at O from an inextensible string of length l. The string makes an angle θ with the downward vertical. M stays on a circle of centre O and radius l: the polar basis centred at O is made for this motion, with ur from O to M (along the string) and uθ in the direction of increasing θ.

  1. Forces: the weight P = mg, vertical downwards, and the tension of the string T = −T ur, directed from M to O. Friction is neglected.

  2. Projecting the weight: the angle between P and ur is θ. P = mg cos θ ur − mg sin θ uθ: the tangential component always brings the mass back towards θ = 0.

  3. The acceleration: r = l is constant, so r = r = 0 and a = −lθ² ur + lθ uθ.

  4. Second law projected on the two vectors of the basis:

    on ur: mg cos θ − T = −mlθ²on uθ: −mg sin θ = mlθ
On uθ: the motion

The tension does not appear there. Dividing by ml:

θ + gl sin θ = 0

The mass has cancelled out: the motion of a pendulum does not depend on its mass.

On ur: the tension
T = mg cos θ + mlθ²

At the lowest point, T = mg + mv²/l: more than the weight, like the reaction at the bottom of a bowl (chapter 2).

TD 7, Ex 1

Questions 1 to 4: add the forces on the diagram, write the second law, project it “on the proposed axes” (ur, uθ) and write the equation in standard form. The statement reminds you of the formula for the acceleration in polar coordinates. The statement calls the mass M: it is the same thing.

Traps
  • Forgetting the minus sign of the tangential component of the weight: it is what makes the pendulum come back. Check: for θ > 0, the weight pushes towards decreasing θ.
  • Writing T = mg cos θ: this is true only when the mass is at rest (at the extremes). In motion, you must add mlθ².

Part 2

Small oscillations: a harmonic oscillator again

The equation θ + (g/l) sin θ = 0 is not the one from chapter 3, because of the sine. But for a small angle expressed in radians, sin θ ≈ θ, and the equation becomes

θ + ω0² θ = 0,   ω0 = √gl,   T0 = 2π √lg

This is the harmonic oscillator, with the angle in place of the position. The solution is the one from chapter 3: released from rest at θ0 (TD 7, Ex 1, question 5), θ(t) = θ0 cos(ω0t).

  1. Without a calculator: l = 1 m and g ≈ π² ≈ 9.87 m·s⁻². T0 = 2π√(1/π²) = 2 s: a 1 m pendulum “beats seconds” (one second per swing).

  2. Isochronism: for small angles, the period depends neither on the amplitude nor on the mass, only on l and g. To double the period, you must quadruple the length.

Traps
  • sin θ ≈ θ requires θ in radians: sin 10° ≈ 0.17, not 10.
  • Writing ω0 = √(l/g): check with the units, √(m·s⁻²/m) = s⁻¹.
  • Writing ω0 = √(k/m) for a pendulum: there is no spring.

Part 3

The energy approach to the pendulum (TD 7, exercise 2)

The same pendulum, without friction, but through energy. We take the potential energy to be zero at the lowest point.

  1. Gravitational potential energy: M is at height l − l cos θ above the lowest point, so

    Ep = mgl(1 − cos θ)

    Minimal (zero) at θ = 0, maximal at the extremes θ = ±θ0.

  2. Kinetic energy: on a circle of radius l, v = lθ (chapter 1), so

    Ec = ½ m l² θ²

    Maximal at θ = 0, zero at the extremes, where the mass turns round.

  3. Mechanical energy: EM = ½ ml²θ² + mgl(1 − cos θ). It is conserved: no friction, and the tension, perpendicular to the velocity, does no work.

  4. Recovering the equation: EM is constant, so dEM/dt = 0:

    ml²θθ + mgl sin θ θ = 0⇒   θ + gl sin θ = 0

    (we divide by ml²θ, which is non-zero during the motion). For small angles, we recover the harmonic oscillator of part 2.

  5. Time evolution (question 5): released from rest at θ0, θ(t) = θ0 cos(√g/l t).

Small angles: the analogy with the spring

1 − cos θ ≈ θ²/2, so Ep ≈ ½ mgl θ², like ½kx²: mgl plays the role of k, ml² that of m. And EM = ½ mgl θ0².

The speed at the lowest point

All the initial potential energy has become kinetic: ½mvmax² = mgl(1 − cos θ0), so vmax = √2gl(1 − cos θ0), valid even for a large angle.

Traps
  • Writing Ep = mgl cos θ: with the origin at the lowest point, the height is l(1 − cos θ). Check: zero at θ = 0.
  • Writing Ec = ½mθ²: θ is an angular velocity, the speed is lθ.

Part 4

Fluid friction: the damped oscillator equation

The course goes back to the mass on a horizontal spring (chapter 3) and adds a fluid friction force, that of air for example, proportional to the velocity and opposite to it:

f = −α v    (α in kg·s⁻¹, that is N·s·m⁻¹)

  1. Second law: P + R + F + f = m a.

  2. Projection on x: 0 + 0 − kx − αx = mx.

  3. Standard form: divide by m.

    x + αm x + km x = 0⇔   x + 1τ x + ω0² x = 0,   τ = mα

τ is a characteristic time (in seconds): the stronger the friction, the shorter τ. The term in x is the signature of friction. The mechanical energy is no longer conserved: it decreases, because the power of the friction, f · v = −αv², is always negative.

What the course asks for

“Solving this equation is beyond the scope of this course”: you must know how to derive it with the second law, recognise τ and ω0, and know the form of the solutions for each regime (part 5).

The same friction without a spring

October 2025 midterm, Ex 1: a drop falls with f = −kv. Without a spring, the equation is about the velocity and has only one derivative: dv/dt + v/τ = −g, with τ = m/k. Solution: vy = gτ(e−t/τ − 1), terminal velocity gτ.

Traps
  • Forgetting that f depends on the velocity: its projection is −αx, not −αx.
  • The title in the course speaks of a “pendulum with fluid friction” but the calculation is done for the mass and spring: the equation has the same form for a pendulum, with θ in place of x.

Part 5

The three regimes of a damped oscillator

The course compares a parameter λ with ω0 without defining it. It is half of 1/τ: looking for solutions of the form ert, you arrive at r² + r/τ + ω0² = 0, whose roots are

r = −λ ± √λ² − ω0²,    λ = 12τ = α2m

Depending on the sign of λ² − ω0², there are three families of solutions:

RegimeConditionSolutionShape
Under-damped (pseudo-periodic)λ < ω0e−λt(A cos ωt + B sin ωt)Oscillations whose amplitude decays as e−λt. Pseudo-angular frequency ω = √(ω0² − λ²), slightly smaller than ω0.
Critically dampedλ = ω0(A + Bt) e−ω0tNo oscillation; the fastest return to equilibrium.
Over-damped (aperiodic)λ > ω0A er1t + B er2tNo oscillation; r1 and r2 are negative, the return is slow.

  1. Which regime? Compute λ = α/(2m) and ω0 = √(k/m), then compare. Example: m = 0.5 kg, k = 50 N·m⁻¹, α = 2 kg·s⁻¹: λ = 2 s⁻¹ < ω0 = 10 rad·s⁻¹, under-damped regime.

  2. Without friction (λ = 0), we recover the harmonic oscillator: e0 = 1 and ω = ω0.

Typo in the course

The course writes the solution of the critical regime as (A + Bt) eω0t, without a minus sign: the exponent must be −ω0t, otherwise x would grow without limit instead of returning to equilibrium.

Traps
  • Comparing τ and ω0: they do not have the same unit. You compare λ = 1/(2τ) with ω0, two quantities in s⁻¹.
  • Confusing the pseudo-angular frequency ω with the natural angular frequency ω0: ω < ω0, so the pseudo-period is slightly longer than T0.
