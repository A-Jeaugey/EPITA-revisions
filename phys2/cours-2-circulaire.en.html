On a circle, the second law is projected on the polar basis.

A ball on a sphere, a solid in a chute, a car in a loop: the point follows an arc of a circle, and you look for its speed or the force from the support. Energy gives the speed, the second law projected on (ur, uθ) gives the reaction. It comes up in 4 of the 7 end-of-semester exams found (2017, 2018, June 2018, 2020).

Part 1

La 2e loi de Newton en base polaire

In B1, you projected the second law Σ F = m a on fixed axes (ux, uy). When the point moves on a circle of centre O and radius R, you project it on the polar basis at M: the acceleration takes a simple form here.

on ur: Σ Fr = −mRθ² = −m v²Ron uθ: Σ Fθ = mRθ = m dvdt

With v = Rθ: Rθ² = v²/R and Rθ = dv/dt. The first equation says that the resultant of the forces towards the centre equals mv²/R; the second, that the tangential component changes the speed.

  1. Set-up: system (the point M, of mass m), Earth reference frame assumed Galilean.

  2. Frame: O at the centre of the circle, the angle θ measured from the direction the statement gives (often the vertical), uθ in the direction of increasing θ. The formulas for velocity and acceleration stay true whichever side you measure θ from.

  3. List of forces, drawn at M: the weight P, the reaction N of the support (perpendicular to the support, so along ur, if there is no friction), a tension, a friction force…

  4. Project each force on ur and uθ, then write the two equations.

Projecting the weight without mistakes

The angle between P and −ur is the angle between the vertical and the radius OM. If that is θ: the component with cos θ is on the radius, the one with sin θ on the tangent. Check at θ = 0: at the top, the weight is entirely along −ur, its tangential component is zero.

In the exams

January 2018 (ball on a sphere, 5 points) and January 2020 (chute, 6 points) ask for exactly these two projections, written in the Frenet basis (part 6): on a circle it is the same thing. The satellite of TD 5 (chapter 1, part 7) is the simplest case: a single force, directed towards the centre.

Traps
  • The “centripetal force” is not one more force to add to the list: mv²/R is what the real forces (weight, reaction, tension) must provide towards the centre.
  • The minus sign of −mRθ²: ur points outwards, the acceleration towards the centre.

Part 2

The reaction of a circular support

Without friction, the reaction N is perpendicular to the support, so it lies along the radius. Its direction depends on which side the body is on: a support pushes, it never pulls.

On top (sphere, convex chute)

N = N ur, outwards. On ur: N − mg cos θ = −mv²/R, so

N = m(g cos θ − v²R)
Inside (bowl, loop)

N = −N ur, towards the centre. At the bottom of the bowl, θ measured from the downward vertical: N − mg cos θ = mv²/R, so

N = m(g cos θ + v²R)
The rule to remember

Forces towards the centre − forces outwards = mv²/R. On top, the weight pulls towards the centre and the reaction pushes back: mg cos θ − N = mv²/R, so the reaction decreases as v increases. In a bowl, it is the opposite: at the lowest point, N = mg + mv²/R, more than the weight. That is why you feel heavier at the bottom of a roller-coaster dip.

  1. Without a calculator: a 0.2 kg ball passes the bottom of a bowl of radius 0.5 m at 2 m·s⁻¹, with g = 10 m·s⁻². N = 0.2 × (10 + 4/0.5) = 0.2 × 18 = 3.6 N, almost twice its weight (2 N).

  2. Tangential equation: on uθ, the reaction does not count (it is perpendicular), only the weight is left: on top of a sphere, mRθ = mg sin θ, that is θ − (g/R) sin θ = 0. This is the “differential equation of the motion” asked for in January 2018.

In the exams

January 2018 Ex 2: “Write Newton’s second law in the Frenet basis, deduce the differential equation of the motion and the magnitude of the reaction”. January 2020 Ex 3: the same question for a solid in a quarter-circle chute, with aT = g sin θ et R = m(g cos θ − v²/r).

Traps
  • Papers often call the reaction R and the radius r (or the other way round). Write the letter of the statement, but do not mix them up.
  • Do not copy “N = mg cos θ” from the inclined plane: on a circle, the term mv²/R is missing.

Part 3

Energy first, then the second law

The reaction depends on v²; the second law alone does not give v as a function of position. The energy theorem gives it in one line, without going through time. The method used in the exams takes three steps.

  1. Height of M as a function of θ, from the chosen axis. On a sphere, θ from the upward vertical: z = R cos θ, and M has dropped by R(1 − cos θ) from the top.

  2. Mechanical energy conserved (no friction, the reaction does no work because it is perpendicular to the motion): ½mv² = ½mv0² + mg(z0 − z). From the top, at rest: v² = 2gR(1 − cos θ).

  3. Replace v² in the reaction of part 2: N = mg cos θ − 2mg(1 − cos θ), that is

    N = mg(3 cos θ − 2)
In the exams

January 2020, Ex 3: the statement gives V² = 2gr(1 − cos θ) and asks for the reaction: R = mg(3 cos θ − 2), the result of the official solution. June 2018, Ex 3: you start from a height h, the speed comes from the energy between A and M, then the reaction in the loop (part 5).

Traps
  • The height difference between the top and M is R(1 − cos θ), not R cos θ: draw a sketch and locate the height of M relative to the centre.
  • The work of the reaction is zero only if it is perpendicular to the motion: with friction, you must count it (part 6).

Part 4

When the body leaves the support

A support can only push: N ≥ 0. As long as the calculation gives N > 0, the body stays in contact. It leaves at the angle where N becomes zero; beyond that, the formula would give a negative reaction, which makes no sense: the body is already in free fall.

N = 0 ⇔ 3 cos θ − 2 = 0 ⇔ cos θ = 23 ⇔ θ = arccos(2/3) ≈ 48°
In the exams

January 2020, Ex 3, question 3b: “Determine the expression of the angle θ when the system leaves the chute”: R = 0, cos θ = 2/3, θ = arccos(2/3). No calculator: the expected answer is the expression, not the degrees.

Traps
  • The body does not leave the sphere when its speed vanishes, nor when θ = 90°: it is the reaction that vanishes, well before.
  • Inside a bowl, below the centre, N = m(g cos θ + v²/R) stays positive: the question only arises above the centre, as in the loop.

Part 5

The loop

A mass released with no speed from a height h enters at B, at the bottom of a loop of radius R, and runs through it on the inside. M is located by the angle θ above the horizontal diameter (June 2018): M is at height R + R sin θ above B.

  1. Energy between A and B: vB² = 2gh. With g = 10 m·s⁻² and h = 1 m: vB = √20 = 2√5 m·s⁻¹.

  2. Energy between A and M: vM² = 2g(h − R − R sin θ).

  3. Second law on the radius, towards the centre: the reaction N points to O; the weight has a component mg sin θ towards O (M is above the diameter). N + mg sin θ = mvM²/R, so

    N = mg(2hR − 2 − 3 sin θ)

  4. At the top C (θ = 90°): NC = mg(2h/R − 5). The mass goes round without leaving the track if NC ≥ 0:

    h ≥ 5R2    ⇔    vC² ≥ gR
In the exams

June 2018, Ex 3 (6 points): vB, then vM, then the reaction “using Newton’s second law in the Frenet basis”. The condition h ≥ 5R/2 was not asked for, but it lets you check the sign of the result.

Traps
  • The height of M is counted from the bottom of the loop: R + R sin θ, not R sin θ.
  • At the top, the reaction and the weight both point towards the centre: they add up.

Part 6

Friction on an arc; reading a paper in the Frenet basis

A friction force of constant magnitude

The friction f is tangent and opposite to the motion. On an arc of radius R covered through an angle Δθ, the path has length RΔθ (in radians): the work is

W(f) = −f R Δθ    (−f × πR/2 for a quarter circle)

With the mechanical energy theorem, ΔEm = W(f), you deduce f from the measured speeds.

  1. January 2018, Ex 3: m = 10 g released from the edge of a half-cylinder guide (R = 1 m), vB = 4 m·s⁻¹ at the bottom. ΔEc = ½ × 0.01 × 16 = 0.08 J; ΔEp = −mgR = −0.1 J; ΔEm = −0.02 J = −f × πR/2, so f = 0.04/π ≈ 1.3·10⁻² N.

  2. January 2017, Ex 3: 60° arc (≈ 1 rad), R = 1.5 m, m = 0.1 kg, vA = 2 and vB = 3 m·s⁻¹. ½m(vB² − vA²) = mgR(1 − cos θ) − fRθ: 0.25 = 0.75 − 1.5 f, so f = 1/3 N.

The Frenet basis: another name for the polar basis on a circle

Six exams out of seven ask their questions “in the Frenet basis (uT, uN)”. This basis is not in this year’s course, but on a circle traversed in the direction of increasing θ, you read it with this chapter:

FrenetOn a circle of radius R
uT, tangent, in the direction of motionuθ
uN, normal, towards the inside of the curve−ur (towards the centre)
curvilinear abscissa s, length travelleds = Rθ (from θ = 0), v = ds/dt
aT = dv/dtRθ
aN = v²/RRθ², always positive
In the exams

June 2018, Ex 1: a circle traversed at speed V = V0/(1 + αt). s(t) is the antiderivative of V that vanishes at 0: s = (V0/α) ln(1 + αt) ; aT = dV/dt = −αV0/(1 + αt)² and aN = V²/R = V0²/(R(1 + αt)²). January 2022, Ex 2: θ = αt², s = αRt², aT = 2αR, aN = 4α²Rt².

Traps
  • Decelerating circular motion: aT < 0 but aN > 0, always (June 2018 MCQ).
  • For a curve that is not a circle, the radius of curvature R changes along the path: the Frenet formulas stay true, but the polar basis centred at O no longer coincides with it.
  • An angle in degrees in RΔθ: 60° converts to π/3 ≈ 1 rad.
