A shaken rope, sound, light: a disturbance propagates without carrying matter along. Part 1

The periodic signal and its quantities

A signal is a physical quantity that depends on time: a voltage, a pressure, the position of a point on a rope. The course is limited to periodic signals, which repeat identically. Five quantities describe them.

QuantitySymbol and unitRelation
AmplitudeA, unit of the signalmaximum deviation from the mean value
PeriodT, sduration of one pattern
Frequencyf, Hzf = 1/T
Wave speed (celerity)c, m·s⁻¹propagation speed of the wave
Wavelengthλ, mλ = c/f = cT

The wavelength is the distance travelled by the wave during one period. The three families of signals in the course:

SignalPhysical quantityFrequencies
Electricalcurrent, voltagedepends on the circuit (50 Hz for the mains)
Acousticpressure, air velocityaudible: 20 Hz to 20 kHz; infrasound < 20 Hz; ultrasound > 20 kHz
Electromagneticelectric field, magnetic field10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ Hz for light; microwaves, from 3·10⁸ to 3·10¹¹ Hz, are also electromagnetic

  1. The microwave oven (TD 8, Ex 2): f = 2.45 GHz = 2.45·10⁹ Hz and c = 3·10⁸ m·s⁻¹, so λ = 3·10⁸ / 2.45·10⁹ ≈ 0.12 m = 12 cm. This is not a micrometre length (10⁻⁶ m) but a centimetre one: despite their name, these are electromagnetic waves in the microwave range, between infrared and radio waves.

  2. An A at 440 Hz in air (c = 340 m·s⁻¹): λ = 340/440 ≈ 0.77 m.

Traps
  • Writing λ = c × f: check with the units, (m·s⁻¹)/(s⁻¹) = m.
  • Prefixes: k = 10³, M = 10⁶, G = 10⁹; μ = 10⁻⁶, n = 10⁻⁹.

The travelling wave

Definition from the course

A wave is a phenomenon in which a local disturbance moves through space without any displacement of matter. Its speed of travel is the wave speed (celerity) c.

Each point of the medium repeats the motion of the source, with a delay equal to the travel time x/c. If the source, at x = 0, has the motion f(t), the point with coordinate x has the motion f(t − x/c):

s(x, t) = f(t − xc)   towards increasing xs(x, t) = f(t + xc)   towards decreasing x

The wave speed depends on the medium (the tension and mass of the rope, the temperature of the air), not on the source. The source only decides the shape of the disturbance.

The answers to TD 8, exercise 1

  1. Reaching the red point sooner? No: in 2 s, the wave travels 2c, whatever the hand does. Moving the hand faster makes the bump narrower, not faster. Only a change of rope (or of tension) changes c.

  2. At t = 1 s (c = 1 square per second): the hand is at the top and the wavefront is 1 square from O. The rope rises from the point 1 square away up to the hand, at 10 cm: half a bump.

  3. Two ropes at t = 3 s: the bump has the same height (10 cm) on both, because the hand made the same gesture. On the rope that is twice as fast, it is twice as wide (4 squares instead of 2) and twice as far (peak 4 squares from O instead of 2).

  4. Two waves crossing: the displacements add up (superposition). A bump and a trough of the same shape that overlap give, at that instant, a flat rope. Afterwards, each one carries on as if the other did not exist.

Traps
  • Believing that matter moves along with the wave: each point of the rope moves up and down on the spot.
  • Believing that a more energetic gesture speeds up the wave: it changes the amplitude or the width, not the wave speed.

The plane progressive monochromatic wave (PPMW)

Two assumptions simplify the model: the wave propagates in a single direction (plane wave) and it is perfectly sinusoidal (monochromatic wave). This is the PPMW of the course. Along the Ox axis, towards increasing x:

s(x, t) = A cos(ωt − kx + φ)
QuantityNameRelation
Amaximum amplitudeunit of the signal
ωtemporal angular frequency, rad·s⁻¹ω = 2πf = 2π/T
kspatial angular frequency (wavenumber), rad·m⁻¹k = ω/c = 2π/λ
φinitial phase, radvalue of the phase at x = 0, t = 0

It is indeed a travelling wave: ωt − kx = ω(t − x/c). And it has a double periodicity: at a fixed point, the signal repeats every T; at a fixed instant (a photo of the rope), the shape repeats every λ.

  1. Writing a PPMW: A = 2 mm, f = 50 Hz, c = 10 m·s⁻¹, towards increasing x, φ = 0. ω = 100π rad·s⁻¹, λ = 10/50 = 0.2 m, k = 2π/0.2 = 10π rad·m⁻¹: s = 2 cos(100πt − 10πx) in mm.

  2. Reading a PPMW: s = 3 cos(200πt + 4πx) propagates towards decreasing x (+ sign), f = 100 Hz, λ = 2π/(4π) = 0.5 m, c = ω/k = 50 m·s⁻¹.

Traps
  • This k is not the spring constant of a spring: it is a wavenumber, in rad·m⁻¹.
  • Confusing k = 2π/λ with 1/λ, or ω = 2πf with f.

The phase shift between two signals

Two signals of the same frequency, s1 = A cos(ωt + φ1) and s2 = A cos(ωt + φ2), are compared through their phase shift. If s2 reproduces s1 with a delay Δt:

Δφ = φ2 − φ1 = −ω Δt
Lead or lag

Δφ > 0: s2 leads s1 in phase. Δφ < 0: it lags.

Three special cases

Δφ = 0 (modulo 2π): in phase. Δφ = π: in phase opposition (antiphase). Δφ = π/2: in quadrature.

Two points of the same PPMW a distance d apart are out of phase by kd = 2πd/λ: they vibrate in phase if d is a multiple of λ, in antiphase if d = λ/2, 3λ/2…

Interference in the living room (TD 8, exercise 3)

The ear receives the direct wave from the loudspeaker and the wave reflected by the wall at distance D behind it. The two waves add up; their sum depends on their phase shift.

  1. Time offset: the reflected wave makes one extra round trip between the ear and the wall, that is 2D: Δτ = 2D/c.

  2. Phase shift: Δφ = 2πf Δτ = 4πfD/c (in absolute value; reflection adds no phase shift for the pressure).

  3. Weakened amplitude when the two waves are in antiphase: Δφ = (2n + 1)π, that is

    fn = (2n + 1) c4D,   n ∈ ℕ

    For D = 1 m: 86 Hz, 257 Hz, 429 Hz… right in the bass and mid-range.

  4. No audible frequency? It would need c/(4D) > 20 kHz, that is D < 343/80,000 ≈ 4.3 mm: impossible, the head does not fit against the wall.

  5. The solution: far from the wall, the reflected wave has travelled a longer path and arrives weakened, so the cancellation is only partial. An absorbing coating has the same effect: it weakens the reflected wave.

Traps
  • The sign: with the convention of the course, a delay gives a negative phase shift.
  • Forgetting the round trip: the path difference is 2D, not D.

Standing waves and normal modes

A guitar string is fixed at both ends. A wave travelling along it is reflected at each end: two waves of the same amplitude and the same angular frequency propagate in opposite directions and superpose. At the fixed point, the reflected wave is inverted (the displacement stays zero there):

s1 = A cos(ωt − kx)s2 = −A cos(ωt + kx)s = s1 + s2 = 2A sin(ωt) sin(kx)

Time and space are decoupled: each point oscillates on the spot, as sin(ωt), with an amplitude 2A|sin(kx)| that depends on its position. Nothing propagates: this is a standing wave. The nodes (zero amplitude) are every λ/2; the antinodes (maximum amplitude 2A) are halfway between two nodes.

Typo in the course

The course writes s2 = +A cos(ωt + kx) then finds 2A sin(ωt) sin(kx). With the + sign, the sum is 2A cos(ωt) cos(kx); the sin × sin form, zero at x = 0, requires the inverted reflected wave, as here. The conclusions (nodes, antinodes, modes) do not change.

The normal modes of a string of length L

The string is also fixed at x = L: s(L, t) = 0 at all times, so sin(kL) = 0, that is knL = nπ. Only certain waves can exist, the normal modes:

λn = 2Ln,    fn = cλn = n c2L = n f1n ∈ ℕ*

Mode n = 1 is the fundamental, the following ones are the harmonics, with frequencies that are multiples of f1. In a mode, all points vibrate at the same frequency, in phase or in antiphase. A 65 cm guitar string where c = 286 m·s⁻¹ has fundamental f1 = 286/1.3 = 220 Hz.

A pipe closed at one end (TD 8, exercise 4)

In a wind instrument, the air vibrates: the closed end imposes a vibration node, the open end an antinode. The length is then a quarter of a wavelength plus a whole number of half-wavelengths, as the statement gives it:

L = λn4 + n λn2⇒   λn = 4L2n + 1fn = (2n + 1) c4L,   n ∈ ℕ

  1. The fundamental at 440 Hz (n = 0): L = c/(4 × 440) = 340/1,760 ≈ 0.193 m.

  2. The next frequency (n = 1): 3 × 440 = 1,320 Hz. A pipe closed at one end only produces odd harmonics.

  3. Eliminating 1,320 Hz: a hole opens the pipe and imposes a vibration antinode. Placed where this mode has a vibration node, at 2L/3 from the closed end (≈ 12.9 cm), it makes this mode impossible. No published answer key: this is the answer of the model, which ignores the effect of the hole on the fundamental.

The microwave oven (TD 8, Ex 2, question 4)

Yes, standing waves form: the metal walls reflect the waves. At the nodes, spaced by λ/2 ≈ 6 cm, the food heats little. That is why the turntable rotates.

Traps
  • Two consecutive nodes are separated by λ/2, not λ.
  • For a string fixed at both ends, n starts at 1; for the closed-open pipe, at 0.
