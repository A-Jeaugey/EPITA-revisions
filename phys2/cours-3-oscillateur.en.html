A spring pulls, the mass oscillates: the harmonic oscillator.

A mass hanging from a spring, with no friction, goes back and forth for ever around its equilibrium position. The second law gives a differential equation, x + ω0² x = 0, whose solutions are sinusoids. This is part 2 of the course (“Oscillating systems”, pages 13 to 18) and TD 6.

Part 1

The spring and its restoring force

A spring is described by two numbers: its natural length ℓ0, its length when nothing pulls or pushes it, and its spring constant (stiffness) k, in N·m⁻¹. When its length ℓ differs from ℓ0, it exerts on the solid attached to its end a force that tends to bring it back to the natural length: the restoring force. It is proportional to the extension Δℓ = ℓ − ℓ0.

F = −k (ℓ − ℓ0) uu: unit vector along the spring, from its attachment point towards the mass

In the course, the spring is horizontal and the origin O of the axis is placed at the rest position of the mass, where the extension is zero. Then Δℓ = x, and the force is simply:

Fspring = −k x ux

Writing the force when the origin is not at the rest position

This is exercise 1 of TD 6: the spring is fixed at H, the mass is at M, and positions are measured from an arbitrary point O. The method has three steps.

  1. The length ℓ, always positive, from the positions: the larger minus the smaller. M to the right of H: ℓ = x − xH. M to the left of H: ℓ = xH − x.

  2. The vector u, from H to M: ux if M is to the right of H, −ux if it is to the left.

  3. The force: F = −k(ℓ − ℓ0) u. Check: if the spring is stretched, the force must point from M towards H.

H = O, M to the rightx−k(x − ℓ0) ux

Two springs (TD 6, Ex 1, case 6)

One spring (k, ℓ0) links the wall to M1, a second one (k′, ℓ′0) links M1 to M2. Both forces act on M1: −k(x1 − ℓ0) ux and k′(x2 − x1 − ℓ′0) ux. On M2: −k′(x2 − x1 − ℓ′0) ux, the opposite (third law). The official answer key writes k instead of k′ in this last force: it is a typo.

Traps
  • Writing −kx when the origin is not at the rest position: it is −k(ℓ − ℓ0), with the actual length.
  • The minus sign does not mean “always towards the left”: it says that the force is opposite to the extension.

Part 2

Setting up the equation: Newton’s second law

The system in the course: a mass m, treated as the point M, slides without friction on a horizontal support, attached to a horizontal spring. The Earth frame is assumed to be Galilean. We apply the B1 method, with no change.

  1. List of forces: the weight P (vertical, downwards), the reaction of the support R (vertical, upwards, no friction) and the restoring force F = −kx ux.

  2. Second law: P + R + F = m a, with a = x ux (the motion is rectilinear along x).

  3. Projection on the x axis: the weight and the reaction are vertical, they do not count. What is left is 0 + 0 − kx = mx. On the vertical axis: R = mg.

  4. Standard form: everything on the same side, coefficient 1 in front of x.

x + km x = 0⇔   x + ω0² x = 0,   with   ω0 = √km

This is a second-order differential equation: it relates the unknown function x(t) to its second derivative. ω0 is the natural angular frequency of the system, in rad·s⁻¹. It depends only on k and m.

Recognising a harmonic oscillator

An equation of the form (quantity)″ + (positive constant) × (quantity) = 0. The constant is ω0². The quantity can be a position x, a height z, an angle θ (pendulum, chapter 4) or an electric voltage.

Checking homogeneity

k/m is in N·m⁻¹·kg⁻¹ = (kg·m·s⁻²)·m⁻¹·kg⁻¹ = s⁻². So ω0 is in s⁻¹ (rad·s⁻¹), and ω0²x has the unit of an acceleration, like x.

In the TDs

TD 6, Ex 2: “Project this expression on the relevant axis of the problem and write the resulting differential equation in its standard form”. TD 7, Ex 1: the same thing for an angle. The question always has three steps: vector second law, projection, standard form.

Traps
  • Finding x − ω0² x = 0: with a minus sign, there is no oscillation. Re-read the direction of the restoring force.
  • Keeping m in front of x: the standard form needs a coefficient 1, so you divide by m.

Part 3

Solving the equation with the initial conditions

The course states the form of the solutions without proof: any function

x(t) = A cos(ω0t) + B sin(ω0t)

satisfies x + ω0² x = 0, whatever the constants A and B. There are two constants, so you need two pieces of information to find them: the initial conditions, the position x0 = x(0) in metres and the velocity v0 = x(0) in m·s⁻¹.

  1. Position at t = 0: x(0) = A cos 0 + B sin 0 = A, so A = x0.

  2. Differentiate: x(t) = −Aω0 sin(ω0t) + Bω0 cos(ω0t).

  3. Velocity at t = 0: x(0) = Bω0, so B = v0/ω0.

  4. Conclude, then check at t = 0:

    x(t) = x0 cos(ω0t) + v0ω0 sin(ω0t)
Case 1: released from rest

The mass is released from x0 with v0 = 0: B = 0 and

x(t) = x0 cos(ω0t)

It oscillates between −x0 and x0.

Case 2: launched from O

The mass starts from x0 = 0 with velocity v0: A = 0 and

x(t) = v0ω0 sin(ω0t)

It oscillates between −v0/ω0 and v0/ω0.

The other form: amplitude and phase

The same solution can also be written with a single cosine:

x(t) = Xm cos(ω0t + φ)Xm: amplitude (m), φ: initial phase (rad)

The course also calls this amplitude A; here it is called Xm so that it is not confused with the A of the first form. Expanding the cosine, x0 = Xm cos φ and v0/ω0 = −Xm sin φ, so

Xm = √x0² + (v0/ω0)²

  1. Without a calculator: m = 0.5 kg, k = 50 N·m⁻¹, so ω0 = √100 = 10 rad·s⁻¹. The mass is released at x0 = 4 cm with v0 = 0.3 m·s⁻¹: B = 0.3/10 = 0.03 m, and x(t) = 0.04 cos(10t) + 0.03 sin(10t). The amplitude is √(0.04² + 0.03²) = 0.05 m: 5 cm, more than the 4 cm at the start, because of the initial push.

In the TDs

TD 6, Ex 2, question 6: “Use the initial conditions to find their expressions”; TD 7, Ex 1, question 5: the same thing for the angle of a pendulum. Each time, the course checks that the solution found satisfies the equation: do it too, by differentiating twice.

Traps
  • Differentiating cos into +sin: (cos ω0t)′ = −ω0 sin ω0t. The factor ω0 comes out at each differentiation.
  • Writing B = v0: the condition gives Bω0 = v0, so B = v0/ω0, which is indeed a length.
  • Taking the amplitude equal to x0 when v0 ≠ 0.

Part 4

Natural period and shape of the motion

The cosine repeats when ω0t increases by 2π. The oscillations are therefore sinusoidal, with natural period

T0 = 2πω0 = 2π √mk    and    f0 = 1T0

The oscillator is called harmonic: its amplitude is constant (no friction) and its period depends only on the mass-spring system, not on the amplitude or on how it was started. The heavier the mass, the slower it oscillates; the stiffer the spring, the faster it oscillates.

Reading the curve

At the extremes (x = ±Xm), the tangent is horizontal: the velocity is zero, the mass turns round. At O, the slope is steepest: the velocity is maximal, vmax = ω0Xm.

The acceleration

The equation says x = −ω0² x: the acceleration always points towards O, and is maximal at the extremes, where the velocity is zero.

Traps
  • Writing T0 = 2π√(k/m). Check with the units: √(kg/(N·m⁻¹)) = √(s²) = s. And with common sense: a heavier mass must oscillate more slowly.
  • Confusing ω0 (rad·s⁻¹) and f0 (Hz): ω0 = 2πf0.

Part 5

The energy of the oscillator

Besides kinetic energy, there is energy stored in the spring, the elastic potential energy, where x is the extension of the spring:

Ec = ½ m v²     Epe = ½ k x²

With x = Xm cos(ω0t + φ), v = −Xmω0 sin(ω0t + φ) and mω0² = k:

Ec = ½ kXm² sin²(ω0t + φ)Epe = ½ kXm² cos²(ω0t + φ)EM = Ec + Epe = ½ kXm²   (constant)

The mechanical energy is conserved: there is no friction, the weight and the reaction do no work (they are perpendicular to the motion) and the restoring force is conservative. It is proportional to the square of the amplitude. During an oscillation, energy goes from the spring to the mass and back.

What energy lets you do quickly

  1. The velocity at a given position: ½mv² + ½kx² = ½kXm², so v = ω0 √Xm² − x², maximal at O: vmax = ω0Xm.

  2. Recovering the equation: EM is constant, so its derivative is zero: mxx + kxx = x(mx + kx) = 0, hence x + ω0² x = 0. This is the method of TD 7, Ex 2, for the pendulum.

TD 6, Ex 3: the HCl molecule

The hydrogen atom vibrates, linked to the chlorine atom (36 times heavier, assumed fixed) by a “spring”. With f = 8.5·10¹³ Hz and mH = MH/NA: k = 4π²f²mH ≈ 4.7·10² N·m⁻¹. The total energy hf/2 is entirely kinetic when passing through equilibrium: vmax = √(hf/mH) ≈ 5.8·10³ m·s⁻¹. It is entirely elastic at the extremes: Xm = √(hf/k) ≈ 1.1·10⁻¹¹ m = 11 pm, about 9 % of the bond length (127 pm).

Traps
  • In ½kx², x is the extension ℓ − ℓ0, not a position measured from an arbitrary point.
  • Doubling the amplitude multiplies the energy by 4, not by 2.

Part 6

The vertical spring (TD 6, exercise 2)

A ball of mass m hangs from a vertical spring (k, ℓ0). The z axis is vertical, pointing up, with the origin O at the attachment point: the ball is below, its height z is negative and the length of the spring is −z. This time, the weight does work and is not cancelled: it shifts the equilibrium position.

  1. Forces: the weight −mg uz and the restoring force k(−z − ℓ0) uz (part 1), upwards when the spring is stretched.

  2. Equilibrium (a = 0): k(−ze − ℓ0) − mg = 0, so

    ze = −(ℓ0 + mgk)

  3. Second law projected on z: mz = −kz − kℓ0 − mg, that is, in the requested form z + az + bz = c:

    z + km z = −(g + kℓ0m)a = 0,  b = km,  c = km ze

  4. Change of variable: with Z = z − ze, the offset from equilibrium, the right-hand side disappears:

    Z + ω0² Z = 0,   ω0 = √km

    The weight does not change the angular frequency: it only moves the centre of the oscillations.

  5. Initial conditions: z(0) = −3ℓ0/2 and z(0) = 0, so Z(0) = −3ℓ0/2 − ze and B = 0:

    z(t) = ze − (3ℓ02 + ze) cos(ω0t)

    Check: at t = 0, z = ze − 3ℓ0/2 − ze = −3ℓ0/2.

The rule to remember

For a vertical spring, measure the position from the equilibrium: the weight disappears from the equation, and you recover exactly the horizontal oscillator. The amplitude is the gap between the starting position and the equilibrium, not the length of the spring.

The official TD 6 answer key
  • It concludes “angular frequency ω0 = √(g/l)”: this is a typo, the angular frequency of a spring is √(k/m) (√(g/l) is that of the pendulum, chapter 4).
  • It takes the initial position equal to 3ℓ0/2 although its variable is the offset from equilibrium: the amplitude is |Z(0)| = |mg/k − ℓ0/2|, not 3ℓ0/2.
